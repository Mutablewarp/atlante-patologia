## Definition and epidemiology

**What is a thrombus, and what is it not?** A thrombus is a clot that develops inside the lumen of a vessel; fibrin, platelets and blood cells caught in the meshwork make it up.<!--c:thrombosis-c001--> It is attached to the vascular endothelium.<!--c:thrombosis-c002--> A simple blood clot only shows that coagulation was activated, and it can form in a test tube or after death.<!--c:thrombosis-c003--> A hematoma is blood that has leaked out of the vessels and clotted outside them.<!--c:thrombosis-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What makes blood clot inside a vessel?** Three changes favor it: endothelial injury, stasis or abnormal flow, and a hypercoagulable state (the Virchow triad).<!--c:thrombosis-c005-->

**Where do thrombi form, and why?**
- Arterial thrombi arise mostly in arteries hit by atherosclerosis: coronary, cerebral, mesenteric, renal and leg arteries.<!--c:thrombosis-c006-->
- Aortic aneurysms often hold a thrombus, because disturbed flow and wall disease promote clotting.<!--c:thrombosis-c008-->
- In veins, stasis from heart failure, bed rest, postoperative immobility or travel favors deep vein thrombosis (DVT).<!--c:thrombosis-c009-->
- Trauma, surgery, childbirth and infection also favor DVT.<!--c:thrombosis-c010-->
- Hypercoagulable states include exogenous hormones, late pregnancy, cancer and inherited thrombophilia.<!--c:thrombosis-c011-->
- A mural thrombus can form on the left ventricle over an infarct.<!--c:thrombosis-c012-->
- Atrial fibrillation slows atrial blood, so thrombi tend to start in the left atrial appendage.<!--c:thrombosis-c013-->
- Primary heart muscle disease can also cause left ventricular mural thrombi.<!--c:thrombosis-c014-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does the vessel wall start a thrombus?** Endothelial injury is the event that triggers most thrombosis.<!--c:thrombosis-c016--> After injury, platelets become adhesive, stick to the wall and then aggregate.<!--c:thrombosis-c017--> ADP and thromboxane A2 from activated platelets recruit more platelets.<!--c:thrombosis-c019--> Fibrin then stabilizes the growing platelet mass.<!--c:thrombosis-c020--> Coagulation ends with thrombin turning soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin.<!--c:thrombosis-c021-->
**What keeps clotting in check?** Prostacyclin and nitric oxide from healthy endothelium keep platelets from aggregating.<!--c:thrombosis-c023--> Thrombin bound to thrombomodulin activates protein C, which breaks down factors V and VIII.<!--c:thrombosis-c025--> If flow is good, simply losing endothelium gives platelet pavementing, not a thrombus.<!--c:thrombosis-c027-->

**What raises the risk further?** Turbulence at an aneurysm or bifurcation, or slow flow in a narrowed artery, favors arterial thrombosis.<!--c:thrombosis-c028--> Polycythemia vera, promyelocytic leukemia and some cancers raise coagulability.<!--c:thrombosis-c029--> Factor V Leiden is the most common gene variant linked to venous thrombosis.<!--c:thrombosis-c030--> The prothrombin G20210A mutation is a common but mild risk factor.<!--c:thrombosis-c032--> Deficiencies of protein C, protein S or antithrombin are rare but strong risk factors.<!--c:thrombosis-c033--> Genetic factors account for 60% of DVT risk.<!--c:thrombosis-c034-->

## Gross morphology

**What does a thrombus look like to the naked eye?** When new, an arterial thrombus stuck to the wall has a deep red color and a soft, easily crumbled texture.<!--c:thrombosis-c035--> The lines of Zahn are thin, repeating yellowish layers made of platelets and fibrin.<!--c:thrombosis-c036--> Lines of Zahn belong to a thrombus formed during life.<!--c:thrombosis-c037--> With organization it becomes firm and grayish white.<!--c:thrombosis-c038-->

**How does a postmortem clot differ?** A postmortem clot is gelatinous and forms in stagnant blood.<!--c:thrombosis-c039--> Gravity makes the red cell-rich part a reddish gelatinous "currant jelly" layer.<!--c:thrombosis-c040--> The "chicken fat" clot is the upper layer: plasma that coagulated without red cells, so it is pale yellow and firmer.<!--c:thrombosis-c041-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What is the thrombus made of under the microscope?** Platelets and fibrin form a tight structure that also traps red cells and leukocytes.<!--c:thrombosis-c042--> This organized structure sets a thrombus apart from a postmortem or test-tube clot.<!--c:thrombosis-c043-->

**What happens as it organizes?** Fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells move into the fibrin and lay down matrix.<!--c:thrombosis-c044--> Canalization follows: new channels lined by endothelium open inside it.<!--c:thrombosis-c046-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at?**
- **How it is studied:** at autopsy or in forensic work, the structure of the clot helps decide whether it formed during life or after death.<!--c:thrombosis-c047-->

## Clinical presentation

**What does arterial thrombosis cause?** Because they usually block the lumen, arterial thrombi frequently lead to infarction in the area the artery feeds.<!--c:thrombosis-c048--> Thrombosis on a coronary plaque causes myocardial infarction, and on a cerebral plaque causes stroke.<!--c:thrombosis-c049--> Mesenteric, renal and leg artery thrombosis cause intestinal infarction, kidney infarcts, and an ischemic leg with gangrene.<!--c:thrombosis-c050-->

**What does DVT cause?** Small calf-vein thrombi usually cause no symptoms, and even big iliofemoral ones can be silent.<!--c:thrombosis-c051--> In some patients, forcing the foot upward makes the calf tender: the Homan sign.<!--c:thrombosis-c052--> If a femoral or iliac vein is blocked, the leg becomes congested, swollen and cyanotic.<!--c:thrombosis-c053--> Superior mesenteric vein clots can cause hemorrhagic bowel infarction, cerebral vein clots can reduce brain perfusion, and hepatic vein clots cause Budd-Chiari syndrome.<!--c:thrombosis-c054-->

**What happens when a thrombus reaches the lungs?** Acute pulmonary embolism ranges from silent to sudden cardiovascular collapse and death.<!--c:thrombosis-c055--> Most pulmonary emboli come from the deep veins of the legs.<!--c:thrombosis-c056-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which findings point to a thrombus?** In autopsy and forensic work, telling an antemortem clot from a postmortem one often matters.<!--c:thrombosis-c059-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What can happen to a thrombus?**
- **Lysis:** the blood's thrombolytic activity, through plasmin digesting fibrin, can dissolve it.<!--c:thrombosis-c060-->
- **Propagation:** the thrombus can keep growing, since more clot builds up on it.<!--c:thrombosis-c061-->
- Venous thrombi often grow toward the heart and reach the larger iliofemoral veins.<!--c:thrombosis-c062-->
- **Organization:** connective tissue invades it and makes it firm and grayish white.<!--c:thrombosis-c063-->
- **Recanalization:** the organized thrombus becomes threaded with new channels covered by endothelium.<!--c:thrombosis-c064-->
- Even after canalization, a thrombus typically lets only a small amount of blood pass.<!--c:thrombosis-c065-->
- **Embolization:** part or all of it detaches and lodges in a vessel at a distance.<!--c:thrombosis-c066-->

**What are the major complications?** Embolism makes a heart thrombus most dangerous: pieces break away and become stuck in vessels far from the heart.<!--c:thrombosis-c067--> Large venous thrombi endanger life because they can travel to the lungs.<!--c:thrombosis-c068--> The valves of a vein that has gone through thrombosis and organization are left damaged, and lasting deep venous insufficiency nearly always follows.<!--c:thrombosis-c070--> In industrialized nations, no other cause kills more people than thrombosis of atherosclerotic arteries.<!--c:thrombosis-c072-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Thrombus | Attached to the endothelium, with lines of Zahn.<!--c:thrombosis-c073--> | Gross look for lines of Zahn |
| Postmortem clot | Gelatinous, formed in stagnant blood, with currant jelly and chicken fat layers.<!--c:thrombosis-c074--> | Gross look at autopsy |
| Hematoma | Blood that clotted after hemorrhage outside the vessels.<!--c:thrombosis-c075--> | Location outside the vascular lumen |
| Thromboembolus | A clot, or a fragment of one, that arises in one place and is carried by the blood to a faraway site.<!--c:thrombosis-c076--> | Finding the origin of the clot |

## High-yield summary

- The **Virchow triad** is endothelial injury, stasis or turbulence, and hypercoagulability.<!--c:thrombosis-c077-->
- **Lines of Zahn** are alternating bands of platelets and fibrin in a thrombus formed during life.<!--c:thrombosis-c078-->
- A **postmortem clot** is gelatinous, with "currant jelly" and "chicken fat" layers.<!--c:thrombosis-c079-->
- Arterial thrombi sit mostly on **atherosclerosis**, and most venous thrombi form in **deep veins of the legs**.<!--c:thrombosis-c080-->
- **Factor V Leiden** is the most common gene variant linked to venous thrombosis.<!--c:thrombosis-c081-->
- Fates are **lysis, propagation, organization, recanalization and embolization**.<!--c:thrombosis-c083-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is a thrombus the same as a blood clot?** No. A clot can form in a test tube or after death; a thrombus forms in a vessel and adheres to its endothelium.<!--c:thrombosis-c085-->

**Do lines of Zahn appear in a postmortem clot?** No. They belong to a thrombus formed in life, while a postmortem clot is gelatinous.<!--c:thrombosis-c087-->
