## Definition and epidemiology

**What is a thrombus, and what is it not?** A thrombus is coagulated blood inside a vessel lumen, a fibrin meshwork holding platelets and cells.<!--c:thrombosis-c001--> It is attached to the vascular endothelium.<!--c:thrombosis-c002--> A test-tube or postmortem clot reflects only activated coagulation, not a thrombus.<!--c:thrombosis-c003--> Clotted blood outside the vascular system after a hemorrhage is a hematoma.<!--c:thrombosis-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What makes blood clot inside a vessel?** Three changes favor it: endothelial injury, stasis or abnormal flow, and a hypercoagulable state (the Virchow triad).<!--c:thrombosis-c005-->

**Where do thrombi form, and why?**
- Arterial thrombosis favors the arteries prone to atherosclerosis: leg, renal, mesenteric, cerebral and coronary.<!--c:thrombosis-c006-->
- In an aortic aneurysm, disturbed flow and a diseased wall promote clotting, so thrombi are common.<!--c:thrombosis-c008-->
- Travel, bed rest, immobility after surgery and heart failure slow venous flow and favor deep vein thrombosis (DVT).<!--c:thrombosis-c009-->
- Infection, childbirth, surgery and trauma also favor DVT.<!--c:thrombosis-c010-->
- Hypercoagulability from inherited thrombophilia, cancer, late pregnancy or exogenous hormones favors DVT too.<!--c:thrombosis-c011-->
- After a myocardial infarction, a mural thrombus can settle over the infarcted left ventricle.<!--c:thrombosis-c012-->
- In atrial fibrillation, flow stalls and thrombus usually forms in the left atrial appendage.<!--c:thrombosis-c013-->
- Left ventricular mural thrombi are also seen with primary heart muscle disease.<!--c:thrombosis-c014-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does the vessel wall start a thrombus?** Most thrombi begin with damage to the endothelium.<!--c:thrombosis-c016--> Injury makes platelets sticky, and they then clump into an aggregate.<!--c:thrombosis-c017--> Activated platelets release ADP and thromboxane A2, which draw in more platelets.<!--c:thrombosis-c019--> A fibrin network holds the growing mass together.<!--c:thrombosis-c020--> Last in coagulation, thrombin changes soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin.<!--c:thrombosis-c021-->
**What keeps clotting in check?** Endothelial cells hold platelet aggregation back by secreting prostacyclin and nitric oxide.<!--c:thrombosis-c023--> Activated protein C destroys factors V and VIII; thrombin activates it by binding thrombomodulin.<!--c:thrombosis-c025--> Where flow stays good, a denuded endothelium is only paved with platelets and gives no thrombus.<!--c:thrombosis-c027-->

**What raises the risk further?** Slowed flow in a narrowed artery, or turbulence at a bifurcation or in an aneurysm, favors arterial thrombosis.<!--c:thrombosis-c028--> Some cancers, polycythemia vera and promyelocytic leukemia make blood clot more readily.<!--c:thrombosis-c029--> Among gene variants, venous thrombosis is most often tied to factor V Leiden.<!--c:thrombosis-c030--> Mild as a risk factor, the prothrombin G20210A mutation is nonetheless common.<!--c:thrombosis-c032--> Lack of antithrombin, protein S or protein C is rare but a strong risk factor for DVT.<!--c:thrombosis-c033--> Heredity explains 60% of the risk of DVT.<!--c:thrombosis-c034-->

## Gross morphology

**What does a thrombus look like to the naked eye?** Early on, a thrombus stuck to an artery wall is dark red and breaks apart easily.<!--c:thrombosis-c035--> In its layers, yellowish platelets and fibrin take turns in thin bands, known as the lines of Zahn.<!--c:thrombosis-c036--> A thrombus formed during life carries lines of Zahn.<!--c:thrombosis-c037--> Once organized, it is firm and grayish white.<!--c:thrombosis-c038-->

**How does a postmortem clot differ?** A postmortem clot is gelatinous and forms in stagnant blood.<!--c:thrombosis-c039--> Gravity separates the blood: the red cell-rich layer is reddish, gelatinous "currant jelly".<!--c:thrombosis-c040--> Above it, coagulated plasma without red cells forms the firmer, pale yellow "chicken fat".<!--c:thrombosis-c041-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What is the thrombus made of under the microscope?** Platelets and fibrin in a thrombus interact tightly.<!--c:thrombosis-c042--> Red cells and leukocytes are trapped in it.<!--c:thrombosis-c091--> A postmortem or test-tube clot looks different from this organized structure.<!--c:thrombosis-c043-->

**What happens as it organizes?** Smooth muscle cells (arteries), fibroblasts (veins) and endothelium migrate into the cross-linked fibrin and produce extracellular matrix.<!--c:thrombosis-c044--> Canalization follows: the organized thrombus gains new endothelium-lined lumina.<!--c:thrombosis-c046-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at?**
- **How it is studied:** at autopsy or in forensic work, it often matters whether a clot arose before or after death.<!--c:thrombosis-c047-->

## Clinical presentation

**What does arterial thrombosis cause?** Infarction of the tissue an artery supplies is a frequent result, since most arterial thrombi occlude the vessel.<!--c:thrombosis-c048--> Myocardial infarction follows thrombosis on a coronary plaque, stroke on a cerebral plaque.<!--c:thrombosis-c049--> A leg artery gives an ischemic leg with gangrene, a renal artery kidney infarcts, a mesenteric artery intestinal infarction.<!--c:thrombosis-c050-->

**What does DVT cause?** Small calf-vein thrombi are usually asymptomatic, and large iliofemoral ones may be too.<!--c:thrombosis-c051--> Some patients show the Homan sign: calf tenderness, often when the foot is forced upward.<!--c:thrombosis-c052--> When a thrombus occludes the femoral or iliac vein, the leg becomes blue, swollen and congested.<!--c:thrombosis-c053--> Hepatic vein thrombosis causes Budd-Chiari syndrome, cerebral vein thrombosis can reduce cerebral blood flow, and superior mesenteric vein thrombosis can cause hemorrhagic bowel infarction.<!--c:thrombosis-c054-->

**What happens when a thrombus reaches the lungs?** Acute pulmonary embolism ranges from silent to sudden cardiovascular collapse and death.<!--c:thrombosis-c055--> The deep veins of the legs are the origin of most pulmonary emboli.<!--c:thrombosis-c056-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which findings point to a thrombus?** Autopsy and forensic work often turn on telling a clot formed in life from one formed after death.<!--c:thrombosis-c059-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What can happen to a thrombus?**
- **Lysis:** the blood's thrombolytic activity, through plasmin digesting fibrin, can dissolve it.<!--c:thrombosis-c060-->
- **Propagation:** the thrombus can keep growing, since more clot builds up on it.<!--c:thrombosis-c061-->
- The larger iliofemoral veins are often reached by venous thrombi, which enlarge toward the heart.<!--c:thrombosis-c062-->
- **Organization:** the thrombus turns firm and grayish white as connective tissue grows into it.<!--c:thrombosis-c063-->
- **Recanalization:** the organized thrombus becomes threaded with new channels covered by endothelium.<!--c:thrombosis-c064-->
- Even after canalization, a thrombus typically lets only a small amount of blood pass.<!--c:thrombosis-c065-->
- **Embolization:** part or all of the thrombus breaks away and lodges in a distant vessel.<!--c:thrombosis-c066-->

**What are the major complications?** The main complication of a thrombus in the heart is that fragments detach and lodge in distant vessels.<!--c:thrombosis-c067--> A large venous thrombus can be life-threatening, since it may be carried to the lungs.<!--c:thrombosis-c068--> Valve function is always impaired once a vein has thrombosed and organized, so chronic deep venous insufficiency is almost inevitable.<!--c:thrombosis-c070--> In industrialized nations, no other cause kills more people than thrombosis of atherosclerotic arteries.<!--c:thrombosis-c072-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Thrombus | Attached to the endothelium, with lines of Zahn.<!--c:thrombosis-c073--> | Gross look for lines of Zahn |
| Postmortem clot | Gelatinous, formed in stagnant blood, with currant jelly and chicken fat layers.<!--c:thrombosis-c074--> | Gross look at autopsy |
| Hematoma | Blood that clotted after hemorrhage outside the vessels.<!--c:thrombosis-c075--> | Location outside the vascular lumen |
| Thromboembolus | Clotted material, whole or in part, formed at one site and carried by the blood to lodge at a distant one.<!--c:thrombosis-c076--> | Finding the origin of the clot |

## High-yield summary

- The **Virchow triad** is endothelial injury, stasis or turbulence, and hypercoagulability.<!--c:thrombosis-c077-->
- **Lines of Zahn** are alternating bands of platelets and fibrin in a thrombus formed during life.<!--c:thrombosis-c078-->
- A **postmortem clot** is gelatinous, with "currant jelly" and "chicken fat" layers.<!--c:thrombosis-c079-->
- Arterial thrombi sit mostly on **atherosclerosis**, and most venous thrombi form in **deep veins of the legs**.<!--c:thrombosis-c080-->
- **Factor V Leiden** ranks first among gene variants linked to venous thrombosis.<!--c:thrombosis-c081-->
- Fates are **lysis, propagation, organization, recanalization and embolization**.<!--c:thrombosis-c083-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is a thrombus the same as a blood clot?** No. A clot can form in a test tube or after death; a thrombus forms in a vessel and adheres to its endothelium.<!--c:thrombosis-c085-->

**Do lines of Zahn appear in a postmortem clot?** No. They belong to a thrombus formed in life, while a postmortem clot is gelatinous.<!--c:thrombosis-c087-->
