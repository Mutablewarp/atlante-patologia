## Definition and epidemiology

**Where does colorectal adenocarcinoma come from?** Adenomatous polyps are the usual starting point, so whatever promotes polyps promotes cancer.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c001--> Though treated differently, colon and rectal cancers are grouped because their biology overlaps.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c002--> Rectal cancer is slightly more common in men; colon cancer affects the sexes about equally.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c003--> In Western societies it ranks second as a cause of cancer death and third in frequency.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Who is at risk?**
- No single factor probably outweighs **age** in the general population.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c005-->
- After **one colorectal cancer**, another tumor becomes more likely.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c006-->
- In **[[ulcerative-colitis|ulcerative colitis]] and [[crohn-disease|Crohn disease]]**, a longer course and more colon involved mean higher risk.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c007-->
- Having an affected relative raises risk, hinting at inheritance (**family history**).<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c008-->
- [[lynch-syndrome|Lynch syndrome]] is the commonest autosomal dominant syndrome behind some cases; [[familial-adenomatous-polyposis|FAP]] is another.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c009-->
- **Animal-product intake** tracks cancer rates, a key reason for regional differences.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c010-->
- **Diets low in plant foods and fiber** are implicated, but the mechanism is unclear.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c011-->
- A substantial share of cases is attributed to **physical inactivity and obesity** together.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c012-->
- **Smoking and heavy alcohol use** each carry risk and may reinforce one another.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does normal mucosa become cancer?** Mutations accumulate stepwise: normal mucosa, then a precursor lesion, then invasive adenocarcinoma.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c014--> See [[multistep-carcinogenesis]] for the general model.
- **APC loss:** cyclin D1 and MYC switch on because β-catenin, no longer destroyed, piles up in the nucleus.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c015-->
- As the "gatekeeper" gene, APC is already mutated in normal mucosa before a sporadic adenoma appears.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c016-->
- FAP patients inherit one mutated APC allele, hence their very numerous adenomas.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c017-->
- **KRAS** acquires activating mutations early in adenoma development.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c018-->
- **TP53** mutation comes late and helps an adenoma become a carcinoma.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c019-->
- **Mismatch repair (MMR) route:** copying mistakes stay in the DNA, and microsatellites are hit hardest.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c020-->
- In inherited cases, one MMR allele is mutated from birth and the other is lost later by a somatic second hit.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c021-->
- Sporadic MMR-deficient tumors usually lose MLH1 through promoter methylation.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c022-->
- The sessile serrated adenoma is the precursor shown for this route ([[serrated-colorectal-lesions]]).<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c023-->
- In Lynch syndrome the germline-mutated genes are most often hMSH2 and hMLH1.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c024-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the tumor look like, and does the side matter?** The tumor may form a polyp, an ulcer, a wall infiltrate or a constricting ring.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c025--> The wide right colon, especially the cecum, allows unimpeded growth, so polypoid cancers are commoner there.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c026--> Ring-like narrowing tumors are seen more often distally.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c027--> Ulceration is frequent in every growth pattern.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c028--> Tumor cells often lie in fat around the colon, distant from the mass, because the serosa is a weak barrier.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c029--> Sometimes the cancer reaches the peritoneum and studs the abdomen with several nodules.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c030-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does H&E show?** Adenocarcinoma, with invasive tubules and glands, is the usual type.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c031--> Mucinous adenocarcinomas, rich in secreted mucin, account for about 15%.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c032--> Poorly differentiated tumors grow as sheets of malignant cells, especially MMR-deficient ones.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c033--> In a polyp, cancer glands are cribriform and their nuclei vary in size and shape.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c034--> Tumor in the stalk close to the cut base may call for wider excision.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c035--> Lynch cancers are rich in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and often have medullary or mucinous histology.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c036-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist examine, and with which tests?**

**How it is studied:**
- **Specimen:** endoscopy suffices for small polyps; large lesions need segmental resection.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c037-->
- **H&E:** in the example shown, necrosis often sits at the center of sieve-like glands of intermediate grade.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c038-->
- **MMR status:** immunostaining measures MMR protein levels in the tumor; microsatellite instability testing and gene sequencing are alternatives.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c039-->
- **Relatives:** once the family mutation is known, relatives can be tested.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c040-->

## Clinical presentation

**Why do right- and left-sided cancers present differently?** Early disease is clinically silent.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c041--> On the right, a wide lumen and liquid stool let tumors grow large without obstructing.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c042--> Silent chronic bleeding from right-sided tumors can first show up as iron-deficiency anemia.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c043--> In proximal tumors, hidden blood in stool is the commonest sign.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c044--> The narrow left colon is easily obstructed, causing abdominal pain and a changed stool pattern.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c045--> Early perforation with peritonitis is possible.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c046--> A distal lesion may give hidden or bright red blood.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c047--> Local spread beyond the bowel can produce fistulas and involve nerves, bladder or abdominal wall.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c048--> Spread inside the abdomen can cause malignant ascites and small-bowel obstruction.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c049--> Lynch cancers can arise outside the colon or recur, and appear early despite few adenomas.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c050-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is the tumor staged?** The TNM system is used (see [[tumor-grading-and-staging]]).
<aside class="edition">Categories change between editions: check the current TNM edition before using them.</aside>
- T1 stops at the submucosa; T2 enters the muscularis propria without passing through.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c051-->
- T3 invades pericolorectal soft tissue.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c052-->
- Involvement of adjacent organs is T4b; breach of the serosa is T4a.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c053-->
- N captures nodal metastasis, M distant metastasis.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c054-->

The liver is where distant metastases most often occur; before that, nodes next to the tumor are involved.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c055--> Under the revised Bethesda guidelines, cancer diagnosed before age 50 raises suspicion of Lynch syndrome.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c056-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What decides the outcome?** Size and histology predict outcome less well than depth of invasion through the bowel wall.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c057--> Surgical resection is the mainstay of cure; adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy is added in some patients.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c058--> For rectal cancer, unless very early, chemoradiation is usually given before surgery.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c059-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What can look like or lead to it?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[colorectal-adenoma]] | Precursor lesion; carcinoma is present when tumor glands invade the stalk.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c060--> | Histology of the polyp |
| [[familial-adenomatous-polyposis]] | The bowel is studded with very many adenomas, and cancer invariably follows.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c061--> | Colectomy or endoscopy specimen |
| [[lynch-syndrome]] | Few adenomas, proximal tumors, other organ cancers.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c062--> | MMR immunostaining or MSI |
| Metastasis to the colon | Pancreatic, urinary or female genital tract cancers can spread to the colon.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c063--> | History plus histology |
| Colorectal lymphoma | Uncommon; most are B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c064--> | Histologic typing |
| Small-bowel adenocarcinoma | Much rarer than colonic, mostly proximal.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c065--> | Histology |

**Small-bowel adenocarcinoma, in brief:**
- It arises from adenomatous precursors, like the colonic tumor.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c066-->
- Gross forms: a "napkin ring" band, an ulcer or a polypoid mass.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c067-->
- FAP and Peutz–Jeghers syndrome, like Crohn and celiac disease, raise the risk.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c068-->
- Even small ampullary tumors can cause jaundice.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c069-->

## High-yield summary

- Most cancers follow the **adenoma-carcinoma sequence**.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c070-->
- Classic order: **APC** loss, then **KRAS**, then **TP53**.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c071-->
- **MMR deficiency** arises from Lynch germline mutations or sporadic **MLH1 hypermethylation**.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c072-->
- **Right-sided** cancers bleed silently and cause anemia; **left-sided** cancers constrict the lumen.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c073-->
- **Depth of wall invasion** matters more for prognosis than size.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c074-->
- The **liver** is the commonest site of distant metastasis.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c075-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Do right- and left-sided cancers behave alike?** No: right-sided ones are bulky and bleed, left-sided ones are annular and obstruct.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c076-->

**Does a larger tumor always mean a worse outcome?** No: extension through the bowel wall matters more than size.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c077-->

**Does "nonpolyposis" mean Lynch syndrome has no adenomas?** No: patients have few adenomas, not none.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c078-->

**Is KRAS mutation a late event?** No: it is early, while TP53 is late.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c079-->

**Is small-bowel adenocarcinoma as common as colonic?** No: it is far less frequent.<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c080-->

## Mnemonics

**RIGHT bleeds, LEFT blocks:** Right = Ridiculously pale (anemia); Left = Lumen locked (obstruction).<!--c:colorectal-adenocarcinoma-c081-->
