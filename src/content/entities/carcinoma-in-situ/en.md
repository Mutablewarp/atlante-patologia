## Definition and epidemiology

**What is carcinoma in situ?** It is a malignant epithelial proliferation that has not yet crossed the basement membrane under the epithelium where it arose.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c001--> These lesions are always curable, since invasion has not yet happened.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c002--> A cancer arising from cells with no basement membrane under them, such as lymphoid cells, has no in situ stage.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes it?** It depends on the organ. In the cervix, high-grade lesions usually carry high-risk HPV types such as 16 and 18.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c004--> In the molecular model of bladder neoplasia, tobacco smoke and arylamine chemicals are among the agents that damage urothelial DNA.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c005-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does a cell become a carcinoma in situ, and why does it stop at the membrane?** In most high-grade cervical lesions, the host genome takes up the HPV DNA.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c006--> In HPV-16, the viral protein E6 switches off p53 and E7 switches off Rb, so two cell-cycle brakes are lost.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c007--> The tumor suppressor p16 sits in 9p, and flat bladder carcinoma in situ consistently loses part of it.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c008--> TP53 lies on 17p, and deletions there add to the changes of flat carcinoma in situ and of invasive bladder tumors.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c009--> Crossing the basement membrane is the first step of invasion and metastasis.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c010--> Metastasis therefore needs the cells to get past the membrane first, because only then can they reach blood and lymphatic vessels.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c011-->

## Gross morphology

**What does it look like to the eye?** In the bladder it usually shows as a scatter of reddish, level mucosal areas with a velvety look.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c012--> Papillary carcinomas that grow outward from the lining are often found nearby.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c013--> In high-grade cervical lesions, colposcopy most often shows one of two patterns: mosaic or vascular punctation.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c014-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What is the key finding on the slide?** The basement membrane stays intact below abnormal cells that fill the whole thickness of the epithelium.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c015--> In urothelial carcinoma in situ, atypical cells are present throughout the urothelium, top to bottom.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c016--> Atypical urothelial cells have enlarged, irregular nuclei that lose their orderly polarity.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c017--> There is no invasion of the stroma.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c018--> In cervical HSIL, basal-type cells that fail to mature spread upward through the epithelium and lose their neat alignment at the base.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c019--> Atypical mitoses may appear and point to aneuploidy.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c020--> Koilocytes are infrequent in HSIL.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c021-->

**What is microinvasion?** An HSIL whose base sends out minute, uneven epithelial sprouts has started to invade; this was once called microinvasion.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c022--> At that point the lesion has crossed the basement membrane and entered the stroma.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c023--> The prognosis of HSIL is the same whether or not such tongues of cells are present.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c024-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** in the cervix, abnormal cells occupy the whole epithelium and are shed, which is why a Pap smear picks up SIL.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c025-->
- **How it is studied:** a Pap smear only screens; the lesion is best graded on a biopsy, where the whole thickness of the epithelium can be seen.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c026-->
- **How it is studied:** in the bladder, FISH on cells shed in urine looks for loss of 9p21 and for abnormal copy number of chromosomes 3, 7 and 17.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c027-->
- **How it is studied:** instead of cystoscopy, urine cytology can be used to look for recurrence or progression of bladder disease.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c028-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does it show up?** Bladder carcinoma in situ is often already multifocal when found, and new foci can show up soon after.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c029--> Bladder carcinoma in situ often coexists with in situ disease elsewhere in the urinary tract and in the prostatic ducts.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c030-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it confirmed?** A Pap smear with SIL leads to biopsy: aimed with the colposcope, or by LEEP, an electrosurgical wire loop that lifts off the whole transformation zone.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c031--> In the bladder, cystoscopy with biopsy is used to find out whether disease has recurred or progressed.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c032-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens if it is left alone?** Invasive carcinoma later follows in about one-third of bladder carcinomas in situ.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c033--> Carcinoma in situ, rather than a papillary tumor, is where most invasive urothelial carcinomas begin.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c034--> Of untreated cervical HSIL, 10 to 20% progress to invasive carcinoma.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c035-->

**How is it treated?** Bladder Tis is usually treated conservatively, with transurethral fulguration and intravesical BCG.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c036--> Cervical high-grade lesions are treated by ablation, LEEP or conization.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c037--> After HSIL the patient needs lifelong checks, since squamous cancer of the vagina or vulva can still arise.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c038-->

## High-yield summary

- **Carcinoma in situ** is malignant epithelium that has not crossed the **basement membrane**.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c042-->
- It cannot **metastasize**, because it has not yet reached vessels beyond the membrane.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c043-->
- Examples are cervical **HSIL (CIN-3)** and flat **urothelial carcinoma in situ**.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c044-->
- The key histologic sign is **full-thickness atypia** with an intact membrane.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c045-->
- Bladder carcinoma in situ is a **reddish, level, velvety area** of mucosa, often multifocal.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c046-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is carcinoma in situ a benign lesion?** No. The cells are malignant; they have only not invaded yet.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c047-->

**Can carcinoma in situ metastasize?** No. Metastasis starts only after the basement membrane is crossed.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c048-->

**Does every cancer have an in situ stage?** No. A lymphoma, for example, starts in cells that sit on no basement membrane, so it has no in situ phase.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c049-->

**Is CIN-3 something different from carcinoma in situ?** No. For the cervix, many pathologists still use the older labels CIN, dysplasia and CIS as synonyms.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c050-->

## Mnemonics

**BRIM:** Basement membrane intact, Replaces the full thickness, Invasion absent, Metastasis impossible.<!--c:carcinoma-in-situ-c051-->
