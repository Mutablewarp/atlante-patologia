## Definition and epidemiology

**What is cirrhosis?** Regenerating hepatocyte nodules ringed by fibrous bands, with the normal architecture of the liver lost, make up cirrhosis.<!--c:cirrhosis-c001--> It never arises without liver cell death that keeps going.<!--c:cirrhosis-c002--> Most patients have either alcohol-related liver disease or chronic viral hepatitis.<!--c:cirrhosis-c003--> Cryptogenic cirrhosis is the label for the roughly 15% of cases in which no cause is found.<!--c:cirrhosis-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes cirrhosis?** Four main groups:

- Fatty liver disease, alcoholic ([[alcoholic-liver-disease]]) or not ([[nonalcoholic-fatty-liver-disease]]).<!--c:cirrhosis-c005-->
- Chronic hepatitis, whether drug-induced, autoimmune or viral.<!--c:cirrhosis-c006-->
- Biliary disease: primary sclerosing cholangitis, primary biliary cholangitis, or obstruction of the extrahepatic bile ducts.<!--c:cirrhosis-c007-->
- Metabolic disease: Wilson disease, hemochromatosis, α1-antitrypsin deficiency and several inherited storage disorders.<!--c:cirrhosis-c008-->

What links these diseases is continuing hepatocyte death; in other respects they differ widely.<!--c:cirrhosis-c009--> Many cryptogenic cases are now traced to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis.<!--c:cirrhosis-c010--> Biliary cirrhosis is where a never-relieved extrahepatic bile duct blockage ends up, once septa have joined neighboring portal tracts.<!--c:cirrhosis-c011-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does a liver become cirrhotic?** Scar matrix from activated hepatic stellate cells and the cycle of hepatocyte loss and regrowth together remodel the vessels of the liver.<!--c:cirrhosis-c012--> If the cause is taken away, the scarred liver may slowly rebuild itself over years to decades, shedding collagen as it does.<!--c:cirrhosis-c013--> Portal hypertension can persist, and the liver rarely returns fully to normal, even if its function and structure get better.<!--c:cirrhosis-c014--> In cirrhosis, scar tissue clogs the sinusoids inside the liver, so portal blood meets resistance and portal vein pressure climbs.<!--c:cirrhosis-c015--> Portal hypertension ([[portal-hypertension]]) in Western countries is most often due to cirrhosis.<!--c:cirrhosis-c016-->

## Gross morphology

**Micronodular or macronodular: what is the difference?** Early cirrhosis is typically micronodular: lots of little nodules, all about the same size, with fibrous septa between them.<!--c:cirrhosis-c017--> Nodules, near lobule size, are usually under 3 mm.<!--c:cirrhosis-c018--> Alcohol is the classic cause of the micronodular pattern, though not the only one.<!--c:cirrhosis-c019--> Macronodular cirrhosis, with large lumpy nodules visible to the naked eye, is the usual late form and follows widespread necrosis, as in viral or autoimmune hepatitis.<!--c:cirrhosis-c020--> Cut open, a macronodular liver shows nodules between bands of uneven width, and its shape is distorted.<!--c:cirrhosis-c021--> Many livers sit between the two types, showing both.<!--c:cirrhosis-c022--> Alcoholics who stop drinking often see a micronodular liver turn macronodular, as the existing nodules go on regenerating and growing.<!--c:cirrhosis-c023--> In practice, the cause matters more than the pattern.<!--c:cirrhosis-c024-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What do you see on the slide?** Portal tracts and central venules cannot be found inside the nodules of micronodular cirrhosis.<!--c:cirrhosis-c025--> Wider septa occur where necrotic parenchyma has collapsed, though the septa between micronodules are usually thin.<!--c:cirrhosis-c026--> A ductular reaction and mononuclear inflammatory cells are found in the septa while the disease is active.<!--c:cirrhosis-c027--> Macronodular nodules are uneven in shape and size; the scar tissue between them is wide here, narrow there.<!--c:cirrhosis-c028--> Remnants of the old portal tracts lie in the broad septa of macronodular cirrhosis, and interface hepatitis may be seen there.<!--c:cirrhosis-c029--> Early cirrhosis can still point to its cause: interface hepatitis suggests chronic hepatitis, while fat with Mallory–Denk hyaline suggests alcohol.<!--c:cirrhosis-c030--> Late-stage livers tend to resemble each other regardless of what caused them.<!--c:cirrhosis-c031-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**

- **How it is studied:** under the microscope, look for cause-specific clues while the disease is still early.<!--c:cirrhosis-c032-->
- In a stained section of alcoholic micronodular cirrhosis, the fibrous tissue around the nodules takes a blue color.<!--c:cirrhosis-c033-->

## Clinical presentation

**What are the clinical consequences?** Portal hypertension, parenchymal liver failure and endocrine disturbance are the three groups into which they fall.<!--c:cirrhosis-c034-->

- Bilirubin that the failing liver cannot clear accumulates as jaundice, and most of it is conjugated.<!--c:cirrhosis-c035-->
- Gut-derived toxins, ammonia among them, escape the liver and contribute to hepatic encephalopathy, either because hepatocytes are failing or because blood is shunted around the liver.<!--c:cirrhosis-c036-->
- Encephalopathy worsens in four stages: sleep disturbance and personality change, lethargy and disorientation, deep somnolence, then coma.<!--c:cirrhosis-c037-->
- Early on, encephalopathy makes the hands flap, a tremor called asterixis.<!--c:cirrhosis-c038-->
- Mercaptans in the saliva cause fetor hepaticus, the typical breath odor of hepatic failure.<!--c:cirrhosis-c039-->
- Low albumin, caused by the liver making too little of it, contributes to the swelling seen in chronic liver disease.<!--c:cirrhosis-c040-->
- Men with long-standing liver failure can develop gynecomastia and a feminized pubic hair pattern, because estrogen levels run high.<!--c:cirrhosis-c041-->
- Spider angiomas and palmar erythema are common vascular signs of excess estrogen.<!--c:cirrhosis-c042-->
- Splenomegaly, ascites and bleeding from gastroesophageal varices are consequences of portal hypertension.<!--c:cirrhosis-c043-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which tests show failure and portal hypertension?** Because a failing liver makes less protein overall, levels of prothrombin, fibrinogen and other clotting factors drop.<!--c:cirrhosis-c044--> Liver failure often brings thrombocytopenia, driven by DIC, a depressed marrow and hypersplenism.<!--c:cirrhosis-c045--> Doctors gauge portal hypertension best by threading a balloon-tipped catheter up from the neck into a hepatic vein and subtracting free from wedged pressure.<!--c:cirrhosis-c046--> With the balloon inflated, the catheter records the wedged pressure, which only indirectly reflects portal vein pressure.<!--c:cirrhosis-c047--> The source pages do not cover imaging.

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens when the liver fails?** Sudden hepatic failure is mostly caused by toxins or by viral or autoimmune hepatitis.<!--c:cirrhosis-c048--> Hepatic failure can also come on slowly in chronic liver disease such as cirrhosis.<!--c:cirrhosis-c049--> More than half of patients with acute or acute-on-chronic liver failure die if they are not transplanted.<!--c:cirrhosis-c050--> Cerebral edema, brain swelling, is what usually kills patients in acute hepatic failure.<!--c:cirrhosis-c051--> Transplantation is usually needed once encephalopathy has reached deep somnolence or coma (stages III and IV).<!--c:cirrhosis-c052--> Liver failure is often complicated by DIC.<!--c:cirrhosis-c053--> The source pages do not state the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma ([[hepatocellular-carcinoma]]).

## High-yield summary

- **Cirrhosis** is the end result of continuing hepatocyte necrosis: regenerative nodules ringed by fibrous bands.<!--c:cirrhosis-c058-->
- **Micronodular** (under 3 mm, alcohol) and **macronodular** (chronic hepatitis) are the two classic patterns.<!--c:cirrhosis-c059-->
- About 15% of cases are **cryptogenic**; most of the rest come from **alcohol** or **chronic viral hepatitis**.<!--c:cirrhosis-c060-->
- **Hepatic stellate cells** produce the matrix; sinusoidal fibrosis causes **portal hypertension**.<!--c:cirrhosis-c061-->
- **Hepatic failure** brings jaundice, coagulopathy, hypoalbuminemia and **encephalopathy**.<!--c:cirrhosis-c062-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is cirrhosis always irreversible?** No. If the cause is eliminated the liver can partly recover over time, though a full return to normal is unlikely.<!--c:cirrhosis-c063-->

**Can you always tell the cause from a cirrhotic liver?** No. Early on, the tissue may still carry clues to the cause; in advanced disease they are often gone.<!--c:cirrhosis-c064-->

**Is micronodular cirrhosis a different disease from macronodular?** No. Over time a micronodular liver can change into a macronodular one.<!--c:cirrhosis-c065-->

## Mnemonics

**SCAR:** Septa of fibrous tissue, Cell death that persists, Architecture lost, Regenerative nodules.<!--c:cirrhosis-c066-->
