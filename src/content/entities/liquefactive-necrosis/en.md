## Definition and epidemiology

Liquefactive necrosis is necrosis in which dead cells are broken down by enzymes much faster than the tissue can repair itself, so the necrotic area turns into fluid.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c001--> It has two classic settings: focal bacterial infection, where neutrophil enzymes digest the tissue and an abscess forms, and ischemic necrosis of the brain.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c002--> Pus is a neutrophil-rich (purulent) exudate in which much of the tissue has liquefied; inflammation that produces it is called suppurative.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

- **Pyogenic bacterial infection:** bacteria usually provide the stimulus that crowds neutrophils into one small area, where the tissue quickly dies and dissolves.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c004--> *Staphylococcus aureus* is a pyogenic (pus-forming) coccus: abscesses from microscopic size up to several centimeters are its typical lesion in sites that are normally sterile.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c005-->
- **Brain ischemia:** when a cerebral artery is occluded, the brain tissue it supplies dies and soon liquefies.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c006-->
- **Brain abscess:** bacteria or fungi settle in the brain tissue; they often come from a cardiac or pulmonary infection through the blood, but may also arrive from an infected neurosurgical wound or spread in from a neighboring frontal-sinus or mastoid infection.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c007-->
- **Lung abscess:** aspiration is the leading cause, and over 90% of cases are due to anaerobic bacteria from the mouth and throat, usually several species together.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c065--> The most frequent background is alcoholism; more generally, depressed consciousness favors aspiration, but an abscess forms only if, in addition, impaired coughing or airway clearance leaves the aspirated material in place.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c066--> Poor oral hygiene and periodontitis add to the risk, because the aspirated material then carries a heavy load of anaerobes.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c081-->
- **Toxic injury:** liquefactive necrosis of both globi pallidi is an almost pathognomonic lesion of severe carbon monoxide poisoning.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c008--> Swallowed strong alkalis (such as lye) liquefy the esophageal and gastric wall through its full thickness and turn membrane lipids into soap.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c009-->

## Pathogenesis

The pattern is decided by kinetics: liquefaction appears when dissolution of necrotic cells greatly outpaces repair.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c010--> Acute inflammation drawn in by the necrosis makes the enzymatic digestion of dead cells most intense; the enzymes come partly from the dead cells themselves and partly from outside them.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c011-->

1. **Abscess (enzymes from neutrophils).** Dead cells are digested by the powerful hydrolases that neutrophils contain.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c012--> The abscess cavity opens inside a solid organ because the crowded neutrophils dissolve the tissue faster than it can be repaired.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c013--> An abscess is one possible outcome of acute inflammation: when fibrosis walls in an inflamed area, the trapped tissue is destroyed by substances released from neutrophils.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c014--> The pus stays confined because, in time, a fibrous capsule grows around the abscess.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c015-->
2. **Brain infarct (enzymes from brain tissue).** Acute inflammation does not explain what happens next in a brain infarct: the dead tissue, coagulative at first, soon dissolves.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c016--> Suspects include hydrolases unique to nervous tissue and the brain's abundant lysosomal enzymes, but the reason remains unknown.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c017-->
3. **Alkali injury.** Strong alkalis such as lye liquefy tissue, and liquid preparations usually do severe damage (granular ones less often), whereas strong acids penetrate less because the coagulative necrosis they cause at once forms a protective eschar.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c018-->

## Gross morphology

- **Abscess:** a cavity in a solid tissue filled with liquefied, purulent material.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c019-->
- **Early brain infarct:** softening and a change of color appear only after the first few hours, during which a bland (non-hemorrhagic) infarct is hard to see.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c020--> By days 3–5 the infarct is swollen and liquefying.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c021-->
- **Hemorrhagic infarct:** a bland infarct turns hemorrhagic, and then stands out both to the naked eye and on imaging, if blood flow returns to the softened tissue, as can happen with embolic occlusion.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c067-->
- **Old brain infarct:** over the following weeks to months a cyst replaces the infarct, and the adjacent ventricle may dilate to fill the space.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c022--> The cavity or cyst left by a large liquefied area of the CNS is permanent.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c023-->
- **Brain abscess:** it begins as cerebritis, an edematous, inflamed area of brain, from which an expanding mass of liquefied tissue develops within a few days.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c024-->
- **Lung abscess:** the right main bronchus continues more directly in the line of the trachea, so lung abscesses are more common on the right.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c068-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Abscess.** Neutrophils crowd the cavity, mixed with pink debris that has no recognizable structure.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c025--> A mature brain abscess has a purulent necrotic core enclosed by three layers: granulation tissue, a dense fibroblast-collagen capsule, and a surrounding zone of reactive gliosis with edema.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c026--> The capsule is built by fibroblasts that migrate in from the small blood vessels, since the brain itself has hardly any.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c027-->

**Brain infarct, by age.**

- 6–24 hours: shrunken, eosinophilic "red neurons" with pyknotic nuclei.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c028-->
- 24–72 hours: an edematous, softened infarct with prominent vessels, now infiltrated by neutrophils.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c029-->
- 3–4 days: the tissue is now pulpy; the neutrophils have given way to macrophages, which begin to phagocytose the dead tissue.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c030-->
- Second week onward: dense gliosis gradually encloses the necrotic area, built by reactive astrocytes that now work alongside the macrophages.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c031-->
- Weeks to months: end stage, a fluid-filled cavity walled by gliosis, with scattered foamy and hemosiderin-containing macrophages and a few thin glial strands and vessels crossing it.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c032-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Autopsy:** an infarct only minutes to a few hours old shows no change yet, so a normal-looking brain does not rule it out; from about 6–24 hours, look on the brain slices for a smudged gray–white matter junction.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c069-->
- **Pus for microbiology:** culture of brain abscess pus can fail, because the causative bacteria frequently need anaerobic or low-oxygen conditions.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c033-->
- **H&E:** in a brain infection, the type of inflammation hints at the organism, though not with certainty: neutrophils point to bacteria, granulomas more often to fungi or mycobacteria.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c070-->
- **Gram stain:** on a smear or a tissue section it shows gram-positive bacteria in dark blue and gram-negative bacteria in red; *S. aureus* appears as gram-positive cocci in clusters.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c071-->
- **Silver stain (GMS):** Gram stain and Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) are the best ways to show *Nocardia*, a filamentous bacterium that forms abscesses, and silver stains also outline fungi.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c072--> The shape of a fungus in the section (budding yeasts or branching hyphae) suggests what it is, but naming the species needs laboratory confirmation, such as culture or PCR.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c073-->
- **Perls (Prussian blue):** the yellow-brown granules in the macrophages (siderophages) of an old infarct are hemosiderin, and the Perls stain shows that they contain iron (see [[g:perls-prussian-blue]]).<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c074-->
- **Immunohistochemistry:** GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein) marks reactive astrocytes, which make the gliosis around an old infarct and around an abscess; CD68, a macrophage marker, stains macrophages and activated microglia.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c075-->

## Clinical presentation

- **Skin abscess (furuncle):** a painful nodule forms at a hair follicle; the pain is relieved when the boil ruptures or is incised, by which time its center has died and become fluctuant.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c034-->
- **Brain abscess:** it can kill by causing brain herniation or by bursting into the ventricles, because it grows like any expanding mass.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c035-->
- **Brain infarct:** the swollen, liquefying infarct can exert a lethal mass effect in the first days.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c036-->
- **Lung abscess:** the sputum is typically abundant and foul-smelling, and nearly every patient has fever and cough.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c079-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

- **Ring enhancement:** contrast medium outlines a smooth ring because it leaks out of the granulation tissue of the abscess wall, which has no blood-brain barrier.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c037--> A pyogenic abscess is strongly suggested when contrast-enhanced T1 MRI shows a dark center inside a smooth enhancing rim that is equally thick all the way round.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c038-->
- **Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI):** the necrotic center of a metastasis or of a malignant tumor arising in the brain usually stays dark, whereas the core of an abscess is very bright because neutrophils hold its water and restrict diffusion.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c039--> Cerebral infarcts also show strongly restricted diffusion.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c040-->
- **Air-fluid level:** on a chest radiograph, a lung abscess that has drained part of its contents into a bronchus shows a fluid level with air above it.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c076-->

## Complications and prognosis

- Without drainage or antibiotic treatment, a brain abscess can rupture into the ventricles, or give rise to daughter abscesses, seeded by organisms that the pressure building up inside it pushes into the neighboring brain.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c041-->
- 15–20% of patients have more than one brain abscess, because blood keeps seeding new organisms.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c042-->
- An infarct containing pyogenic bacteria (a septic infarct), for example from emboli of infective endocarditis, can turn into a true abscess.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c043-->
- Clearance is slow: about 1 mL of a brain infarct is removed by macrophages each month.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c044-->
- Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma is far more likely, for the rest of their lives, in people who survive swallowing a corrosive.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c045-->
- Severe hemoptysis is one danger of a lung abscess; another is empyema, when the abscess bursts into the pleural cavity.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c080-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[coagulative-necrosis]] | Cell outlines persist for a time, with eosinophilic cytoplasm and nuclear loss; typical of infarcts outside the brain.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c046--> | Ghost outlines of cells on H&E |
| [[caseous-necrosis]] | Granular, cheese-like debris that persists inside granulomas, typically in tuberculosis: the cells lose their outlines but are not lysed.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c047--> | Granulomas around the necrosis; acid-fast stain |
| [[fat-necrosis]] | Affects adipose tissue; fatty acids bind calcium to form chalky white soaps, mainly in pancreatitis or after trauma.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c048--> | Basophilic calcium-soap deposits among dead adipocytes |
| Necrotic brain tumor or metastasis | Ring-enhancing lesion whose necrotic center shows little or no restricted diffusion.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c049--> | Core usually dark on DWI (abscess core is bright) |

## High-yield summary

- Liquefaction occurs when **enzymatic dissolution** of dead cells outpaces **repair**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c050-->
- Two classic settings: **abscess** (bacterial infection) and **brain infarct**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c051-->
- In an abscess the enzymes are **neutrophil hydrolases**; the end product is **pus** in a cavity enclosed by a **fibrous capsule**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c052-->
- In the brain, liquefaction **does not need acute inflammation**: the necrosis is **coagulative** at first and then dissolves, possibly through the brain's own **lysosomal enzymes**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c053-->
- Brain infarct timeline: **red neurons** (6–24 h), **neutrophils** (24–72 h), **macrophages** (3–4 days), **astrocytes** (second week), **glia-lined cyst** (weeks to months).<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c054-->
- Brain abscess wall: **granulation tissue**, **collagen capsule**, outer **gliosis**; imaging shows a **smooth ring enhancement** and a **bright core on DWI**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c055-->
- **Lung abscess**: usually from **aspiration** of oral **anaerobes**, more often in the **right** lung; once it drains into a bronchus, the radiograph shows an **air-fluid level**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c077-->
- **Carbon monoxide** poisoning: liquefactive necrosis of both **globi pallidi**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c056-->
- **Alkalis** cause liquefactive necrosis; **acids** cause coagulative necrosis.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c057-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- Brain infarcts liquefy even though acute inflammation does not explain it; liquefaction there is not proof of infection.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c058-->
- A brain infarct is not liquefactive from the very first hour: the dead tissue is coagulative at first and liquefies over the following days.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c059-->
- Caseous necrosis is not a variant of liquefaction: its debris persists instead of being lysed.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c060-->
- Ring enhancement alone does not prove an abscess; the DWI signal of the core helps separate it from a necrotic tumor.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c061-->
- A brain abscess is rarely a complication of bacterial meningitis; most arise from blood-borne spread or from neighboring infection.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c062-->
- A cavity in the lung is not proof of an abscess: lung cancer causes cavitation more often than abscess does, and an air-fluid level is unusual in a tuberculous cavity.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c078-->

## Mnemonics

**"Bugs and Brains melt"** (original): liquefactive necrosis has two classic homes, **B**ugs (bacteria draw neutrophils, which digest tissue into pus) and **B**rains (infarcts dissolve into a cyst).<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c063-->

**"ALkali Liquefies, ACid Coagulates"** (original): the second letter of each chemical gives the first letter of its verb (a**L**kali, **L**iquefies; a**C**id, **C**oagulates), so you remember which chemical gives which pattern of necrosis.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c064-->
