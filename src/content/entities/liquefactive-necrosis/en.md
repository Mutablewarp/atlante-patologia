## Definition and epidemiology

Liquefactive necrosis is necrosis in which dead cells are broken down by enzymes much faster than the tissue can repair itself, so the necrotic area turns into fluid.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c001--> It has two classic settings: focal bacterial infection, where neutrophil enzymes digest the tissue and an abscess forms, and ischemic necrosis of the brain.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c002--> Pus is a neutrophil-rich (purulent) exudate in which much of the tissue has liquefied; inflammation that produces it is called suppurative.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

- **Pyogenic bacterial infection:** a dense, localized gathering of neutrophils, usually provoked by bacteria, rapidly kills and dissolves tissue.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c004--> *Staphylococcus aureus* is the prototype pyogenic organism: when it reaches a normally sterile site it typically produces pus, collected in abscesses that span from microscopic foci to masses a few centimeters wide.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c005-->
- **Brain ischemia:** occlusion of a cerebral artery causes necrosis that is soon followed by liquefaction of the dead tissue.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c006-->
- **Brain abscess:** bacteria or fungi settle in the brain parenchyma, usually arriving through the blood from infections of the heart or lungs, or by direct extension from frontal-sinus or mastoid infection or an infected neurosurgical wound.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c007-->
- **Toxic injury:** severe carbon monoxide poisoning produces bilateral liquefactive necrosis of the globus pallidus, a nearly pathognomonic lesion.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c008--> Swallowed strong alkalis (such as lye) liquefy the esophageal and gastric wall through its full thickness and turn membrane lipids into soap.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c009-->

## Pathogenesis

The pattern is decided by kinetics: liquefaction appears when dissolution of necrotic cells greatly outpaces repair.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c010--> In any necrosis, intracellular and extracellular lytic enzymes eventually disintegrate the dead cells, and this is most marked when the necrosis has drawn in acute inflammation.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c011-->

1. **Abscess (enzymes from neutrophils).** Polymorphonuclear leukocytes carry powerful hydrolases that digest dead cells.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c012--> When neutrophils are packed into one focus, cells die and dissolve quickly, and the liquefied area becomes a cavity within the solid organ.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c013--> If the area of acute inflammation is sealed off by fibrosis, neutrophil products destroy the enclosed tissue and an abscess results.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c014--> With time, fibrous tissue encloses the abscess and keeps its contents in place.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c015-->
2. **Brain infarct (enzymes from brain tissue).** After a cerebral artery is blocked the dead tissue is first coagulative, then quickly dissolves, and this liquefaction is not explained by acute inflammation.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c016--> Why the brain behaves this way is not established; a rich content of lysosomal enzymes, or hydrolases peculiar to CNS cells, may be responsible.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c017-->
3. **Alkali injury.** Liquid strong alkalis (such as lye solutions) liquefy tissue and usually cause severe damage; granular forms do so less often. Strong acids instead coagulate the surface at once, and the resulting eschar limits deeper penetration.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c018-->

## Gross morphology

- **Abscess:** a cavity in a solid tissue filled with liquefied, purulent material.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c019-->
- **Early brain infarct:** a bland (non-hemorrhagic) infarct gives few gross clues during the first hours; afterward the tissue softens and changes color.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c020--> By days 3–5 the infarct is swollen and liquefying.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c021-->
- **Old brain infarct:** over the following weeks to months a cyst replaces the infarct, and the adjacent ventricle may dilate to fill the space.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c022--> When large regions of the CNS liquefy, the resulting cavity or cyst remains for the rest of the patient's life.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c023-->
- **Brain abscess:** infection first causes cerebritis, an edematous inflammatory softening of the brain; within days the focus liquefies and grows as a mass.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c024-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Abscess.** The cavity holds pink, structureless debris packed with neutrophils.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c025--> A mature brain abscess has a purulent necrotic core enclosed by three layers: granulation tissue, a dense fibroblast-collagen capsule, and a surrounding zone of reactive gliosis with edema.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c026--> Fibroblasts are rare in the brain; here they migrate in from the microvasculature to build the capsule.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c027-->

**Brain infarct, by age.**

- 6–24 hours: shrunken, eosinophilic "red neurons" with pyknotic nuclei.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c028-->
- 24–72 hours: neutrophils infiltrate, vessels stand out, and the softened tissue swells with edema.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c029-->
- 3–4 days: macrophages take over from neutrophils and begin removing debris; the tissue is now pulpy.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c030-->
- Second week onward: reactive astrocytes join the macrophages and gradually wall off the necrotic area with dense gliosis.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c031-->
- Weeks to months: end stage, a fluid-filled cavity walled by gliosis, with scattered foamy and hemosiderin-containing macrophages and a few thin glial strands and vessels crossing it.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c032-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

Culture of brain abscess pus can fail, because the causative bacteria frequently need anaerobic or low-oxygen conditions.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c033-->

## Clinical presentation

- **Skin abscess (furuncle):** a tender nodule centered on a hair follicle points (forms a yellow head) and turns fluctuant as its center dies; pain eases once it drains, spontaneously or by incision.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c034-->
- **Brain abscess:** behaves as an expanding mass, life-threatening through brain herniation or bursting into the ventricular system.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c035-->
- **Brain infarct:** the swollen, liquefying infarct can exert a lethal mass effect in the first days.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c036-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

- **Ring enhancement:** the granulation tissue layer of a brain abscess has no blood-brain barrier, so contrast leaks into it and outlines a smooth ring.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c037--> A smooth ring of uniform thickness (typically thin) around a dark core on contrast-enhanced T1 MRI strongly suggests a pyogenic abscess.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c038-->
- **Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI):** the abscess core is very bright because neutrophils trap water and restrict its diffusion, whereas necrosis in metastases and malignant primary brain tumors usually shows little or no restricted diffusion.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c039--> Cerebral infarcts also show strongly restricted diffusion.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c040-->

## Complications and prognosis

- An undrained, untreated brain abscess can push organisms into nearby tissue and seed "daughter" abscesses, or rupture into the ventricles.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c041-->
- 15–20% of patients have more than one brain abscess, because blood keeps seeding new organisms.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c042-->
- An infarct containing pyogenic bacteria (a septic infarct), for example from emboli of infective endocarditis, can turn into a true abscess.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c043-->
- Clearance is slow: macrophages remove debris from a brain infarct at roughly 1 mL per month.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c044-->
- Survivors of corrosive ingestion carry a much higher lifelong risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c045-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[coagulative-necrosis]] | Cell outlines persist for a time, with eosinophilic cytoplasm and nuclear loss; typical of infarcts outside the brain.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c046--> | Ghost outlines of cells on H&E |
| [[caseous-necrosis]] | Outlines are lost, but the debris is not lysed: it persists as granular, cheese-like material inside granulomas, typically in tuberculosis.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c047--> | Granulomas around the necrosis; acid-fast stain |
| [[fat-necrosis]] | Affects adipose tissue; fatty acids bind calcium to form chalky white soaps, mainly in pancreatitis or after trauma.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c048--> | Basophilic calcium-soap deposits among dead adipocytes |
| Necrotic brain tumor or metastasis | Ring-enhancing lesion whose necrotic center shows little or no restricted diffusion.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c049--> | Core usually dark on DWI (abscess core is bright) |

## High-yield summary

- Liquefaction occurs when **enzymatic dissolution** of dead cells outpaces **repair**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c050-->
- Two classic settings: **abscess** (bacterial infection) and **brain infarct**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c051-->
- In an abscess the enzymes are **neutrophil hydrolases**; the end product is **pus** in a cavity walled off by a **fibrous capsule**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c052-->
- In the brain, necrosis starts as **coagulative** and then liquefies **without needing acute inflammation**, possibly through the brain's own **lysosomal enzymes**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c053-->
- Brain infarct timeline: **red neurons** (6–24 h), **neutrophils** (24–72 h), **macrophages** (3–4 days), **astrocytes** (second week), **glia-lined cyst** (weeks to months).<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c054-->
- Brain abscess wall: **granulation tissue**, **collagen capsule**, outer **gliosis**; imaging shows a **smooth ring enhancement** and a **bright core on DWI**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c055-->
- **Carbon monoxide** poisoning: bilateral liquefactive necrosis of the **globus pallidus**.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c056-->
- **Alkalis** cause liquefactive necrosis; **acids** cause coagulative necrosis.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c057-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- Brain infarcts liquefy even though acute inflammation does not explain it; liquefaction there is not proof of infection.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c058-->
- A brain infarct is not liquefactive from the very first hour: the dead tissue is coagulative at first and liquefies over the following days.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c059-->
- Caseous necrosis is not a variant of liquefaction: its debris persists instead of being lysed.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c060-->
- Ring enhancement alone does not prove an abscess; the DWI signal of the core helps separate it from a necrotic tumor.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c061-->
- A brain abscess is rarely a complication of bacterial meningitis; most arise from blood-borne spread or from neighboring infection.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c062-->

## Mnemonics

**"Bugs and Brains melt"** (original): liquefactive necrosis has two classic homes, **B**ugs (bacteria draw neutrophils, which digest tissue into pus) and **B**rains (infarcts dissolve into a cyst).<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c063-->

**"ALkali Liquefies, ACid Coagulates"** (original): the second letter of each chemical gives the first letter of its verb (a**L**kali, **L**iquefies; a**C**id, **C**oagulates), so you remember which chemical gives which pattern of necrosis.<!--c:liquefactive-necrosis-c064-->
