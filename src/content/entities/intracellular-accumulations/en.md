## Definition and epidemiology

Intracellular accumulation (intracellular storage) means that a substance is retained inside cells instead of being used, exported or broken down.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c001--> The retained material can be a normal constituent or an abnormal one, of endogenous or exogenous origin, and harmless or injurious.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c002--> In practice the stored material falls into four groups: lipids, glycogen, proteins, and pigments or particles.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c003--> Storage is counted among the main ways cells respond to persistent stress, alongside atrophy, hypertrophy, hyperplasia, metaplasia and dysplasia.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c004--> The inherited forms are individually rare: all glycogen storage diseases together occur in about 1 in 20,000–25,000 births.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

Four routes lead to storage:

- **Too much of a normal substance:** extra free fatty acids reaching the liver in diabetes or alcohol misuse, extra glycogen in poorly controlled diabetes, cholesterol in familial hypercholesterolemia and iron in overload states.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c006-->
- **A missing enzyme:** an inherited defect blocks the breakdown of glycogen, or of complex lipids and mucopolysaccharides inside lysosomes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c007-->
- **An abnormal protein:** a mutation or an acquired folding defect produces a protein the cell can neither export nor degrade.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c008-->
- **Indigestible material:** peroxidized lipid–protein residues (lipofuscin) and inhaled particles such as carbon or silica.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c009-->

## Pathogenesis

**Overloaded normal pathway.** Normally the liver exports most of the triglyceride it makes as lipoproteins; hepatocytes build it from the free fatty acids that fat cells release.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c010--> When fatty acid delivery rises, triglycerides build up in the cytoplasm (see [[fatty-change]]).<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c011--> Blood glucose sets how much glycogen cells store, so hyperglycemia enlarges glycogen stores.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c012-->

**Blocked degradation (storage diseases).** Glycogen is dismantled to glucose by a sequence of enzymes, and a mutation in any one of them produces a glycogen storage disease.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c013--> Lysosomal acid hydrolases digest lipids, glycoproteins and mucopolysaccharides; if one is missing, its substrate stays undigested and swells the lysosomes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c014--> Swollen lysosomes crowd out the cell's other work and may finally kill it; brain and heart are hit hardest.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c015--> The stored substrate gives each group of lysosomal storage diseases its name, as in the gangliosidoses or the mucopolysaccharidoses.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c016--> Classic examples are glucocerebroside in Gaucher disease, gangliosides in Tay–Sachs disease and mucopolysaccharide products in Hurler and Hunter syndromes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c017--> In glycogenoses, symptoms come either from the stored glycogen itself (Pompe disease) or from the glucose that is no longer released (von Gierke disease).<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c018-->

**Misfolded proteins.** Misfolding is a constant by-product of protein synthesis, and chaperones and proteasomes normally refold or remove faulty proteins.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c019--> When this quality control fails or is overwhelmed, misfolded proteins collect as amorphous aggregates or fibrils.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c020--> They harm the cell either by loss of a needed function or by a toxic gain of function.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c021--> Aggregates too large for the proteasome are tagged and delivered to autophagosomes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c022--> Aggregates can trap ubiquitin, generate reactive oxygen species and inhibit proteasomes, pushing the cell toward death.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c023--> Mutant (PiZ) α1-antitrypsin folds abnormally and is held in the hepatocyte endoplasmic reticulum instead of being secreted.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c024--> The retained protein injures hepatocytes and leads to cirrhosis, while the missing circulating antiprotease leaves lung connective tissue exposed to proteolysis (emphysema).<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c025-->

**Undegradable residues.** Lipofuscin is what remains when autophagy cannot clear oxidized material: lipids damaged by peroxidation and proteins that oxidation has cross-linked.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c026--> Alveolar macrophages engulf inhaled carbon and carry it to hilar lymph nodes, where it stays permanently.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c027-->

## Gross morphology

- Hepatic storage enlarges the liver, as in the marked hepatomegaly of von Gierke disease.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c028-->
- In Gaucher disease the spleen is enlarged in nearly every patient.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c029-->
- Clusters of cholesterol-laden macrophages in subcutaneous tissue form xanthomas visible to the naked eye.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c030-->
- Anthracotic lungs and nodes look strikingly black, yet the change is harmless.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c031-->

## Microscopic morphology

- **Triglyceride:** lipid droplets in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c032-->
- **Cholesterol:** lipid-laden macrophages in the arterial intima (fatty streak) and in xanthomas; smooth muscle cells join in as plaques progress.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c033-->
- **Glycogen:** in poorly controlled diabetes, glycogen distends liver cells and the epithelium of the kidney's proximal tubules.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c034-->
- **Lysosomal lipids:** Gaucher cells are large macrophages whose fibrillary cytoplasm looks like creased paper, with the nucleus pushed to one side.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c035--> Sphingomyelin and cholesterol fill the many small, equal-sized vacuoles that give Niemann–Pick macrophages their bubbly look (foam cells).<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c036-->
- **Proteins:** α1-antitrypsin forms faintly eosinophilic cytoplasmic droplets inside dilated ER in hepatocytes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c037--> Mallory (Mallory–Denk) bodies of intermediate filaments appear in hepatocytes in alcoholic liver injury.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c038--> **Russell bodies** are eosinophilic globules of immunoglobulin held in the cytoplasm of plasma cells; they appear both when plasma cells increase in reaction to disease and in plasma cell tumors.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c085--> In Parkinson disease, neurons of the substantia nigra hold Lewy bodies made of α-synuclein; in Alzheimer disease, cortical neurons hold neurofibrillary tangles made of tau.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c039-->
- **Lipofuscin:** golden-brown cytoplasmic granules, mainly in postmitotic or slowly cycling cells such as neurons, cardiac myocytes and hepatocytes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c040-->
- **"Hyaline"** is only a descriptive word for homogeneous eosinophilic material on routine stains.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c041-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Specimen:** besides liver tissue (see the stains below), glycogen or lipid stored in skeletal muscle is studied on a biopsy of a muscle that is weak but not yet end-stage, usually the quadriceps or the biceps.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c086--> Part of the sample is fixed in glutaraldehyde in case electron microscopy is needed, and part is frozen unfixed for histochemical stains.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c087--> In Gaucher and Niemann–Pick disease the storage macrophages gather above all in the spleen and bone marrow.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c088-->
- **H&E:** note first which cell holds the material and what it looks like (see Microscopic morphology); pigments show their own color: lipofuscin golden-brown, hemosiderin yellow-brown, melanin brown-black, carbon black.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c089-->
- **Fat stains:** on H&E stored fat shows only as empty vacuoles, because paraffin processing dissolves most of it.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c090--> Fat is shown on **frozen sections** with a red lipid stain such as Sudan red.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c045--> In muscle, **oil red O** is most useful for lipid myopathies: in carnitine deficiency it colors red the neutral fat that crowds the fibers (see [[g:fat-stains]]).<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c091-->
- **PAS and PAS-D:** **PAS** stains glycogen; after **diastase** digestion the glycogen is gone, and the PAS-positive globules that remain mark α1-antitrypsin.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c042--> On H&E glycogen only makes the cytoplasm pale; with PAS it turns red, as in the glycogen-laden hepatocytes of von Gierke disease.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c092--> Gaucher cells stain strongly with PAS.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c043--> In acid maltase deficiency (Pompe disease and its later-onset forms), the glycogen stored in muscle fibers sits in vacuoles that are PAS-positive.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c093-->
- **Iron:** **Prussian blue** highlights stored iron, for example in the liver in hereditary hemochromatosis.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c044--> Where the blue lies matters: in hereditary hemochromatosis it fills the hepatocytes, whereas iron from transfusions collects first in Kupffer cells (see [[g:perls-prussian-blue]] and [[pigment-accumulation]]).<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c094-->
- **Immunohistochemistry:** an antibody can name a stored protein: in chronic hepatitis B, staining for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) turns brown the "ground-glass" hepatocytes, whose pale cytoplasm is smooth ER filled with the antigen.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c095-->
- **Electron microscopy:** in acid maltase deficiency the glycogen sits in membrane-bound sacs (lysosomes) and, in mild late-onset cases, may be visible only at this level; in McArdle disease it lies free beneath the sarcolemma.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c096--> In Tay–Sachs disease the stored ganglioside forms concentric membrane whorls inside neuronal lysosomes (see [[g:myelin-figures]]), and in Gaucher cells the stored lipid appears as stacks of tubules within enlarged lysosomes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c097-->
- **Enzyme and DNA tests:** in Gaucher disease the slide is not enough: the enzyme assay, usually on white blood cells, must show too little β-glucocerebrosidase activity, and finding a disease-causing variant in each of the two copies of GBA confirms the diagnosis.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c098--> In McArdle disease, enzyme histochemistry on the muscle can show that myophosphorylase is missing, but genetic testing or a biochemical assay of the enzyme must confirm it.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c099-->
- **Limits:** an inclusion may not reveal its cause: Mallory–Denk bodies, for example, turn up in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis and Wilson disease as well as in alcoholic liver disease (see [[g:mallory-denk-bodies]]).<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c100-->

## Clinical presentation

Effects range from none at all to death, depending on the stored substance and the organ involved.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c046-->

- **Hepatic glycogenosis (von Gierke):** marked hepatomegaly and hypoglycemia in infancy or early childhood.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c047-->
- **Lysosomal glycogenosis (infantile Pompe):** profound muscle weakness and fatal cardiac failure before the first birthday.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c048-->
- **Lysosomal lipid storage:** from asymptomatic organ enlargement to rapidly fatal brain disease.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c049--> Tay–Sachs and other sphingolipidoses show a macular cherry-red spot.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c050-->
- **α1-antitrypsin deficiency:** neonatal cholestatic hepatitis in some infants, cirrhosis in childhood or adulthood, and emphysema.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c051-->
- **Iron overload (hereditary hemochromatosis):** damage mainly to liver, pancreas, heart and testes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c052-->
- **Cholesterol:** xanthomas in familial hypercholesterolemia and, in arteries, atherosclerosis.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c053-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

Stored material is identified in tissue by morphology plus special stains: PAS with and without diastase, and Prussian blue for iron.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c054--> Each inherited storage disease reflects one specific enzyme deficiency, such as glucose-6-phosphatase in von Gierke disease or lysosomal acid α-glucosidase in Pompe disease.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c055--> In Pompe disease blood glucose stays normal: the defect is confined to lysosomes, so glycogen turnover in the cytosol is unaffected.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c056-->

## Complications and prognosis

- **Simple triglyceride storage is reversible**, and the lipid droplets themselves have not been shown to impair cell function.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c057-->
- In hemosiderosis the extra iron by definition does not injure cells; in hereditary hemochromatosis iron-driven oxidative stress damages organs and raises the risk of liver cancer.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c058-->
- Lysosomal storage diseases are progressive.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c059-->
- α1-antitrypsin cirrhosis carries a high incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c060-->
- Lipofuscin may not be inert: it may add to oxidant stress by hampering proteasomal and lysosomal clearance.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c061-->
- Cholesterol-laden macrophages in the intima make up the fatty streak, with which atherosclerosis begins; advanced plaques also contain extracellular cholesterol.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c062-->
- Misfolded proteins can also form insoluble aggregates deposited outside cells, the end point being amyloid fibrils.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c063-->

## High-yield summary

- Four routes: **overload** of a normal pathway, **missing enzyme**, **misfolded protein**, **indigestible residue**.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c071-->
- **Fat** storage is **reversible**.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c072-->
- **Von Gierke** (glucose-6-phosphatase): hepatomegaly with **hypoglycemia**; **Pompe** (lysosomal acid α-glucosidase): myopathy and heart failure, **no hypoglycemia**.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c073-->
- Lysosomal storage diseases are named after the **stored substrate**, e.g. **glucocerebroside** in **Gaucher** disease.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c074-->
- **α1-antitrypsin (PiZ)** is retained in hepatocyte **ER**: **PAS-positive, diastase-resistant** globules, cirrhosis and **emphysema**.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c075-->
- **Hemosiderosis** does not injure cells; **hereditary hemochromatosis** does.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c076-->
- **Lipofuscin** is the golden-brown "**wear-and-tear**" pigment of long-lived cells.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c077-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- **"Hyaline" is not one substance:** in plasma cells it is stored immunoglobulin (Russell bodies), in injured hepatocytes it is clumped cytokeratin filaments (Mallory–Denk bodies), and in kidney arterioles it is plasma proteins built up in the wall along with basement-membrane material.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c078-->
- Pompe disease is both a glycogen storage disease and a lysosomal storage disease.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c079-->
- Storage is not always harmful: anthracosis and hemosiderosis do not injure cells.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c080-->
- Extra glycogen does not always mean a glycogenosis: hepatocytes and the kidney's proximal tubular cells also fill with glycogen when blood glucose is simply high, as in poorly controlled diabetes.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c081-->
- Misfolding does not always cause storage: misfolded CFTR is degraded before reaching the membrane, so cystic fibrosis is a loss-of-function disease.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c082-->

## Mnemonics

**SLAM** (original): **S**urplus of a normal substance, **L**ost enzyme, **A**bnormal protein, **M**aterial the cell cannot digest.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c083-->

**Triple D** (original): **D**iastase **D**eletes glycogen, but α1-antitrypsin **D**efies it.<!--c:intracellular-accumulations-c084-->
