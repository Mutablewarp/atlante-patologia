## Definition and epidemiology

**What is Barrett esophagus?** Intestinal metaplasia takes over the lower esophagus: columnar epithelium with goblet cells grows where squamous lining belongs.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c001-->

It is a consequence of long-standing GERD.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c002-->
Barrett epithelium may reach higher up, though it starts in the lower third of the esophagus.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c003-->
Cases are rising, most of all in white men, and nobody knows why.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c004-->
About 5% to 15% of adults with GERD have it.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Who develops Barrett esophagus?**

- Barrett esophagus becomes more common with age, and the diagnosis is usually made after 50.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c006-->
- Men outnumber women about 3 to 1.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c007-->
- Smoking doubles the risk compared with nonsmokers.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c008-->
- Being obese or white also raises the risk.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c009-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does reflux end in cancer?** Only a minority of patients show dysplasia in the Barrett epithelium.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c010-->
Adenocarcinoma can follow if the dysplasia is not treated.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c011-->
Dysplastic Barrett epithelium is where most esophageal adenocarcinomas begin.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c012-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the endoscopist see?** Barrett mucosa looks salmon-pink at endoscopy.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c013-->
The whole lower esophagus may be lined by it, or only part of the circumference in short segments.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c014-->
Some tumors protrude into the lumen as masses; others are flat or ulcerated.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c015-->
A tan strip of Barrett mucosa can lie between the squamous lining higher up and the tumor.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c016-->
The stomach starts where the gastric folds start; metaplasia climbing the esophagus does not alter them.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c017-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Which cells define Barrett epithelium?** The defining feature, called specialized epithelium, is a mixture of gastric foveolar cells and intestinal-type cells with mature goblet cells, which takes the place of the squamous cells.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c018-->

**How is dysplasia graded?** Biopsies are classed as low-grade or high-grade dysplasia, and the grade steers treatment.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c019-->

**What does the cancer look like?** Well-differentiated tumors still build clearly formed glands.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c020--> Poorly differentiated ones form almost no glands.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c021-->
Signet ring cells appear in some of the poorly differentiated cases.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c022-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**

- **How it is studied:** the specimen is an endoscopic biopsy of the lower esophagus.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c023-->
- On routine H&E, look for goblet cells among gastric-type foveolar cells where squamous epithelium should be.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c024-->
- The reference pages used here describe no special stain, immunohistochemistry or molecular test for diagnosis.

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** Patients usually come to endoscopy after GERD complaints.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c025-->
Many report no reflux symptoms at all.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c026-->
GERD history, obesity, white race and male sex typify patients with symptomatic esophageal adenocarcinoma.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c027-->
Most esophageal tumors can no longer be operated on once dysphagia, the usual first symptom, appears.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c028-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it diagnosed and followed?** Endoscopy with biopsy establishes the diagnosis.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c029-->
Patients whose biopsies show no dysplasia are scoped again every 3 to 5 years.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c030-->
The aim is to catch early dysplasia before cancer develops.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c031-->
Once a cancer is found, endoscopy with ultrasound is used for staging.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c032-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How likely is cancer, and what is done about dysplasia?** A longer Barrett segment and a higher grade of dysplasia both raise the chance of adenocarcinoma.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c033-->
The estimated annual risk is 0.1% to 0.3%.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c034-->
For low-grade dysplasia, the 2016 American College of Gastroenterology guidelines prefer ablating the Barrett segment with radiofrequency; nodular areas may also be removed endoscopically.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c035-->
Yearly surveillance biopsies without treatment are an acceptable though less preferred option.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c036-->
In high-grade dysplasia the advice is to ablate the Barrett segment and resect visible nodules.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c037-->
Endoscopic mucosal resection is an option for T1 cancers, the early invasive ones.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c038-->
Over 30 years in the United States, esophageal adenocarcinoma has become seven times as frequent.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c039-->

## High-yield summary

- Barrett esophagus is **intestinal metaplasia**: squamous lining replaced by columnar epithelium with **goblet cells**.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c043-->
- Its cause is chronic **GERD**.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c044-->
- Endoscopy shows **salmon-pink** mucosa in the lower esophagus.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c045-->
- Diagnosis needs **endoscopy with biopsy**.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c046-->
- Cancer risk follows **segment length** and **dysplasia grade**.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c047-->
- The annual **adenocarcinoma** risk is about 0.1% to 0.3%.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c048-->
- With no dysplasia, repeat endoscopy every **3 to 5 years**.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c049-->
- **High-grade dysplasia** is treated with ablation plus resection of nodular lesions.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c050-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is Barrett esophagus the same as reflux esophagitis?** No. Reflux esophagitis is an inflamed squamous lining, while in Barrett esophagus the lining itself has become columnar.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c051-->

**Do all patients with Barrett esophagus have heartburn?** No. Many report no reflux symptoms.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c052-->

**Does Barrett esophagus mean the patient has cancer?** No. Dysplasia develops in only a minority of patients.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c053-->
The estimated annual cancer risk is low, 0.1% to 0.3%.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c054-->

**Is the salmon-pink color enough for the diagnosis?** No. A biopsy must show the specialized epithelium.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c055-->

## Mnemonics

**GOBLET:** Goblet cells, Overflow of acid (GERD), Biopsy to confirm, Length and dysplasia set the risk, Esophagus lower third, Tumor risk is adenocarcinoma.<!--c:barrett-esophagus-c056-->
