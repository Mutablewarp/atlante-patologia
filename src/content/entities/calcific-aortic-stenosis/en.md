## Definition and epidemiology

**What narrows the valve in calcific aortic stenosis?** The aortic valve opening gets smaller because calcium collects in its cusps.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c001--> The lesion is the end result of chronic valve damage.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c002--> The valve stops working normally only after the cusps have become stiff.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which valves end up calcified and stenotic?** A congenital bicuspid valve, degenerative (senile) change in a three-cusp valve, and rheumatic valve disease are the three settings.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c004-->

- Rheumatic narrowing combines calcium with scarring and commissural fusion.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c005--> A rheumatic aortic valve is almost never the only one affected: the mitral valve is usually involved too.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c006-->
- In a three-cusp valve, wear-related (senile) calcific stenosis is a disease of elderly people.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c007--> The commissures stay unfused in this form, even though calcium stiffens the cusps.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c008-->
- A congenital bicuspid valve often becomes stenotic as the person ages.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c009--> Turbulent flow through the oval opening, not the bicuspid shape itself, is what leads to stenosis.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c010--> Faulty development of the endocardial cushions gives rise to a bicuspid valve, the commonest form of congenital aortic stenosis.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c011-->

In the United States rheumatic fever is rare and surgery has replaced most affected valves, so other causes now dominate.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c012--> Aortic valve disease is more frequent when LDL levels are high.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**Is this just passive wear and tear?** No. Living valve cells actively cause the calcification; it is not a passive coating of dead tissue.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c014--> The matrix is mineralized by valvular interstitial cells that have shifted toward an osteoblast-like state.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c015--> Mineralization of the valve is also helped by osteoprogenitor cells that arrive from the circulation.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c016--> Even a structurally normal valve can calcify after long exposure to turbulent blood flow.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c017--> Inflammation with IL-1β, TNFα and TGFβ follows when LDL particles, which pile up under mechanical forces, become oxidized.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c018--> Metalloproteinases switched on by these mediators break down the valve's matrix.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c019--> Fibroblasts turn into osteoblast-like myofibroblasts, which calcify the matrix, chiefly its collagen.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c020--> Statins do not prevent calcific aortic stenosis, even though its mechanisms resemble atherosclerosis.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c021-->

## Gross morphology

**Where do the calcific nodules sit?** Whatever the cause, nodules lie at the cusp base in the sinuses of Valsalva; the free edges are mostly spared.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c022--> Three cusps, or two in a bicuspid valve, stay separate when rheumatic scarring is absent and the commissures are free.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c023--> An older patient's tricuspid valve has leaflets loaded with calcium.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c024--> In a bicuspid valve both leaflets carry large amounts of calcium.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c025--> The mitral annulus may be calcified in senile stenosis, but the mitral valve itself is usually normal.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c026-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Where is the mineral in the cusp?** Osteoblast-like myofibroblasts lay mineral down in the extracellular matrix of the cusp, mainly on collagen.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c027--> Further histologic detail is not covered in the reference pages used for this card.

## Clinical presentation

**What does severe stenosis do to the heart?** The left ventricle thickens markedly and concentrically.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c028--> Later the chamber enlarges and fails.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c029-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How do you recognize the cause in the valve?** Laboratory and imaging criteria are not covered in the reference pages used for this card. A valve without commissural fusion is unlikely to be rheumatic, since fusion is typical of rheumatic disease.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c030-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens once the valve is replaced?** Replacing the valve gives 85% five-year survival, provided it is done while the ventricle can still recover.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c031--> Once the valve is replaced, the hypertrophic ventricle shrinks back toward normal size.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c032--> More and more elderly patients who cannot safely undergo open-chest surgery are treated with transcatheter valve replacement (TAVR).<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c033-->

## High-yield summary

- Calcium in the cusps narrows the orifice; the **three settings** are rheumatic, degenerative (senile) and congenital bicuspid.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c039-->
- **Commissural fusion** points to a rheumatic valve; degenerative and bicuspid valves lack it.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c040-->
- Nodules sit at the cusp base, sparing the **free margins**.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c041-->
- Calcification is an **active** process with an **osteoblast-like** cell phenotype.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c042-->
- **Statins** do not prevent it.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c043-->
- Severe stenosis causes **concentric LV hypertrophy**, later dilation and failure.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c044-->
- **Valve replacement** gives good results while the ventricle can still recover.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c045-->
- **TAVR** serves elderly patients who are poor surgical candidates.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c046-->
- **Mitral annular calcification** rarely matters functionally, though it is common in the elderly.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c047-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is calcific aortic stenosis always a degenerative disease of the elderly?** No. It also develops on rheumatic valves and on congenital bicuspid valves.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c048-->

**Is a bicuspid valve stenotic from birth?** No. Stenosis develops after years of more turbulent flow through the valve; the bicuspid shape alone is not stenotic.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c049-->

**Does a heavily calcified valve with fused commissures mean degenerative disease?** No. Fused commissures point to rheumatic disease.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c050-->

**Do statins help because the risk factors resemble atherosclerosis?** No. They do not prevent calcific aortic stenosis.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c051-->

**Does mitral annular calcification matter as much as the aortic lesion?** Usually not: it rarely causes functional trouble.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c052--> If calcification is extensive, the posterior leaflet may move poorly in systole and the valve may leak.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c053--> Diabetes, hypertension or aortic stenosis in the patient makes it worse.<!--c:calcific-aortic-stenosis-c054-->
