## Definition and epidemiology

Gangrene is the clinical and gross name for death of a large mass of tissue, most often the distal part of a limb or a segment of bowel.<!--c:gangrene-c001--> It is not one of the microscopic patterns of necrosis (coagulative, liquefactive, fat, caseous, fibrinoid); it describes a whole region that has died, and the pattern underneath is usually coagulative necrosis.<!--c:gangrene-c002--> Three forms are taught:

- **Dry gangrene:** ischemic necrosis of a foot or leg after arterial occlusion, which is in substance a very large pale infarct.<!--c:gangrene-c003-->
- **Wet gangrene:** ischemic dead tissue colonized by bacteria (a septic infarct), which the enzymes of recruited neutrophils then liquefy.<!--c:gangrene-c004-->
- **Gas gangrene** (clostridial myonecrosis): a gas-producing clostridial infection that starts in a contaminated wound and destroys healthy muscle.<!--c:gangrene-c005-->

The adjective is also used outside limbs and bowel: gangrenous cholecystitis is the severe form of acute cholecystitis in which the gallbladder mucosa dies over wide areas.<!--c:gangrene-c065-->

Diabetes is the leading cause of limb amputations not due to trauma in the United States, and gangrene from ischemia is the main reason.<!--c:gangrene-c006-->

## Etiology and risk factors

- **Atherosclerosis:** a thrombus forming on a plaque in a leg or mesenteric artery is the classic trigger of limb or bowel gangrene.<!--c:gangrene-c007-->
- **Arterial embolism:** an embolus lodging in a leg artery causes acute limb ischemia; for example, thrombus from an abdominal aortic aneurysm can block the popliteal artery.<!--c:gangrene-c008-->
- **Diabetes:** peripheral atherosclerosis of the legs leads to ulcers and gangrene of toes and feet, and neuropathy and poor resistance to infection add to the risk.<!--c:gangrene-c009-->
- **Disease of small and medium-sized arteries:** Buerger disease (strongly tied to smoking), and rarely Raynaud phenomenon, can cause gangrene of the digits.<!--c:gangrene-c010--> Severe vasculitis after meningococcal sepsis can also lead to gangrene of the distal extremities.<!--c:gangrene-c011-->
- **Purpura fulminans:** in sepsis, most often meningococcal, blood clots in the small vessels of the skin as part of disseminated intravascular coagulation, and the skin dies with bleeding; the necrosis tends to spread into gangrene of the extremities, and amputation is often needed.<!--c:gangrene-c066-->
- **Cold injury:** in frostbite, damaged endothelium promotes local thrombosis, and the resulting vascular occlusion often ends in gangrene.<!--c:gangrene-c012-->
- **Bowel:** mesenteric artery thrombosis or embolism, mesenteric vein thrombosis, volvulus, intussusception and a hernia trapping a loop of gut.<!--c:gangrene-c013--> Low-flow (nonocclusive) ischemia, in shock or low cardiac output, needs no blocked vessel, yet it can damage as long a stretch of bowel as an occlusion.<!--c:gangrene-c064-->
- **Gas gangrene:** wounds containing a large amount of devitalized tissue, such as severe penetrating trauma, war injuries and septic abortion.<!--c:gangrene-c014-->

## Pathogenesis

**Dry gangrene.** Occlusion of an end artery causes coagulative necrosis of everything downstream; the dead zone is an infarct.<!--c:gangrene-c015--> A narrowed but open artery more often produces atrophy and fibrosis from chronic ischemia than outright necrosis; gangrene needs a critical loss of perfusion, usually from occlusion, although severe chronic insufficiency (as in diabetic arterial disease) can also reach that point.<!--c:gangrene-c016-->

**Wet gangrene.** Dead tissue resists bacteria poorly, so infarcts exposed to bacteria, or caused by infected emboli, easily become septic.<!--c:gangrene-c017--> Neutrophils attracted by the infection release hydrolases that digest the dead cells, turning the area into liquefactive necrosis and sometimes a frank abscess.<!--c:gangrene-c018-->

**Gas gangrene.** Spores of *Clostridium perfringens* reach anaerobic, devitalized tissue in a wound and revert to growing bacteria.<!--c:gangrene-c019--> *C. perfringens* type A causes most cases (80% to 90%), with *C. novyi* and *C. septicum* responsible for some others.<!--c:gangrene-c020--> Its α toxin is a phospholipase (lecithinase) that breaks down the membranes of muscle fibers, leukocytes and red cells, so necrosis spreads into muscle that was healthy and intravascular hemolysis follows.<!--c:gangrene-c021-->

**Bowel.** Sudden arterial blockage kills the gut before collateral flow can compensate.<!--c:gangrene-c022--> The larger the occluded artery, the more bowel dies, up to nearly the whole small intestine.<!--c:gangrene-c067--> When a mesenteric vein is blocked, blood and fluid pile up in the segment until arterial inflow is also compromised.<!--c:gangrene-c023-->

## Gross morphology

- **Dry gangrene of a limb:** classed as a pale infarct, a label that here refers to the absence of bleeding into the dead zone, not to its colour; the key gross sign is a sharp boundary with living tissue, where blood leaking into the surviving edge draws a dark-red line.<!--c:gangrene-c024--> To the naked eye, the dead toes or foot are in fact black and mummified.<!--c:gangrene-c068-->
- **Infarcted bowel:** look first at the boundary: crisp when an artery is blocked, blurred when a vein is, around a swollen, hemorrhagic, purple segment.<!--c:gangrene-c025--> Later clues: a dull serosa coated with exudate, a thin and ballooned wall stripped of its lining, and sometimes gas tracking into the wall or the mesenteric veins (pneumatosis).<!--c:gangrene-c026-->
- **Gas gangrene:** the muscle turns blotchy, then clearly necrotic, and can even liquefy; the skin above it darkens as it bleeds and dies, and the edema and gas spreading through the soft tissue beneath pull it tight.<!--c:gangrene-c027-->

## Microscopic morphology

- **Dry gangrene:** coagulative necrosis, with eosinophilic anucleate cells whose outlines persist.<!--c:gangrene-c028-->
- **Wet gangrene:** neutrophils and bacteria in the necrotic tissue, which is being dissolved, up to abscess formation.<!--c:gangrene-c029-->
- **Gas gangrene:** gram-positive rods, typically lozenge-shaped, lie in widely necrotic tissue whose cells are breaking down; neutrophils are almost absent, probably because the toxin kills them, and this lack of inflammation amid so much necrosis is the clue.<!--c:gangrene-c030-->
- **Bowel infarct:** the necrosis starts in the mucosa, where the surface epithelium and the crypts persist only as ghost outlines and the lining ulcerates, with blood seeping into the mucosa and submucosa; in severe cases the necrosis spreads outward until the whole thickness of the wall is dead.<!--c:gangrene-c031-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Specimen:** gangrene reaches the pathologist as tissue the surgeon has removed (amputated toes or a limb, a resected bowel segment, dead tissue débrided from a wound), or it is found at autopsy.<!--c:gangrene-c070-->
- **Amputation specimen:** sections of the leg arteries can show what blocked them, such as a thrombus on an atherosclerotic plaque or an embolus.<!--c:gangrene-c071--> Buerger disease, in which amputation for gangrene is common early on, looks different from atherosclerotic thrombosis: the occluded artery is itself inflamed, small abscesses rimmed by giant cells lie in its wall, and the inflammation reaches the neighboring vein and nerve.<!--c:gangrene-c072-->
- **H&E:** for the three forms, see Microscopic morphology; in the bowel, a mucosal biopsy taken at endoscopy can show ischemic crypts that are dead except for a remnant at their base, the dead part keeping its shape as a ghost outline (coagulative necrosis; see [[g:ghost-cells]]).<!--c:gangrene-c073--> Gangrene means that the whole thickness of the wall has died; a mucosal biopsy cannot show that, so the pathologist sees it on a resected segment or at autopsy.<!--c:gangrene-c074-->
- **Gram stain:** in gas gangrene, the affected tissue shows gram-positive rods with few neutrophils.<!--c:gangrene-c032-->
- **IHC, electron microscopy, molecular tests:** none is needed for the diagnosis; the forms of gangrene are told apart by the gross picture and H&E, with the Gram stain added in gas gangrene.<!--c:gangrene-c075-->

## Clinical presentation

- **Acute limb ischemia:** sudden pain, missing pulses and a cold limb, sometimes with paresthesia and paralysis.<!--c:gangrene-c033-->
- **Buerger disease:** begins in young adults (usually 25 to 40 years) with claudication, then painful ulcers that destroy the tips of the digits.<!--c:gangrene-c034-->
- **Bowel infarction:** an acute abdomen; it may be preceded by abdominal angina, pain soon after meals because narrowed arteries cannot meet digestive demand.<!--c:gangrene-c035-->
- **Gas gangrene**, as a timeline:
  - *Local phase, usually 2 to 4 days after the injury:* the wound abruptly becomes very painful; it is swollen and sore to touch, its skin darkens, and a foul, thick, blood-tinged fluid, sometimes bubbly, drains from it.<!--c:gangrene-c036-->
  - *Systemic phase:* toxin in the blood breaks down red cells, leaving the patient anemic, while the kidneys fail and blood pressure falls; jaundice and coma appear as the patient slides into terminal shock.<!--c:gangrene-c037-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

- **Limb:** the diagnosis is clinical, based on the cold pulseless limb and the appearance of the dead tissue; the resected tissue shows coagulative necrosis.<!--c:gangrene-c038-->
- **Bowel:** gas within the bowel wall or the mesenteric veins points to infarction; at endoscopy, the ischemic mucosa may be ulcerated or covered by a pseudomembrane.<!--c:gangrene-c039-->
- **Gas gangrene:** gram-positive rods with a striking lack of neutrophils in the wound, together with signs of hemolysis.<!--c:gangrene-c040-->

## Complications and prognosis

- **Limb:** arterial embolism sometimes ends in amputation; diabetic gangrene of the toes and feet often does in the long run.<!--c:gangrene-c041-->
- **Bowel:** infarction is a surgical emergency; chronic ischemia that does not infarct can heal with fibrosis and stricture.<!--c:gangrene-c042-->
- **Dry gangrene:** left alone, the dead part can in time come away along the line of demarcation (autoamputation), but meanwhile it may become infected and turn into wet gangrene.<!--c:gangrene-c069-->
- **Wet gangrene:** a septic infarct can progress to a frank abscess.<!--c:gangrene-c043-->
- **Gas gangrene:** can kill within hours of onset; it is rare when wounds are promptly débrided.<!--c:gangrene-c044-->
- **Buerger disease:** quitting smoking may bring remission, whereas continued smoking can cost the patient hands and feet.<!--c:gangrene-c045-->

## High-yield summary

- **Gangrene** is a clinical and gross term, not a separate histologic **pattern of necrosis**.<!--c:gangrene-c051-->
- **Dry gangrene** = arterial occlusion = a **large pale infarct** with **coagulative necrosis**.<!--c:gangrene-c052-->
- **Wet gangrene** = ischemic necrosis plus **bacterial infection**, with **liquefaction** by neutrophil enzymes.<!--c:gangrene-c053-->
- **Gas gangrene** = **Clostridium perfringens** **α toxin**, a **phospholipase** that kills healthy muscle and lyses red cells.<!--c:gangrene-c054-->
- Gas gangrene shows **few neutrophils**, probably because the toxin destroys them.<!--c:gangrene-c055-->
- Main causes of limb gangrene: **atherosclerosis**, **embolism** and **diabetes**.<!--c:gangrene-c056-->
- Bowel gangrene follows **mesenteric artery occlusion**, **mesenteric vein thrombosis**, **volvulus** or a **strangulated hernia**.<!--c:gangrene-c057-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- Gangrene is not a microscopic type of necrosis like caseous or fibrinoid necrosis; under the microscope dry gangrene is ordinary coagulative necrosis.<!--c:gangrene-c058-->
- Gas gangrene is not just a dead limb that has become infected: the clostridial toxin itself kills muscle that had a normal blood supply.<!--c:gangrene-c059-->
- Bowel infarcts are hemorrhagic (red), unlike the pale infarct of a dry gangrenous limb, because the small intestine has abundant collateral circulation.<!--c:gangrene-c060-->
- Expect an abscess full of neutrophils in wet gangrene, but not in gas gangrene, where neutrophils are scarce.<!--c:gangrene-c061-->
- A stenosed artery more often causes atrophy than gangrene; gangrene requires a critical loss of perfusion, usually from occlusion, but severe chronic insufficiency, as in diabetes, can also cause it.<!--c:gangrene-c062-->

## Mnemonics

**D–W–G** (original): **D**ry = **D**eprived of blood (coagulative, sharply delimited); **W**et = **W**ith bacteria (liquefied, neutrophil-rich); **G**as = **G**erms that make gas (*Clostridium*, toxin kills healthy muscle, few neutrophils).<!--c:gangrene-c063-->
