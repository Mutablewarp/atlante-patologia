## Definition and epidemiology

**What is adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC)?** This salivary gland cancer grows slowly but invades the surrounding tissue, and it often comes back after resection.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c001-->

**Where and in whom does it arise?** Roughly 2 cases in 3 start in a minor salivary gland; the other 1 in 3 start in a major one.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c002-->
Of all tumors of the major salivary glands, 5% are ACC.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c003-->
Among tumors of the minor salivary glands the share is 20%.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c004-->
It also arises outside the mouth: in the tear glands and in the airways.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c005-->
Patients are most often 40 to 60 years old.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c006-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes it?** The source pages name no environmental or hereditary risk factor; the only known abnormality is genetic (see Pathogenesis).

## Pathogenesis

**Which genetic change defines ACC?** Deletions on chromosome 6 and a t(6;9) translocation are a constant finding in the tumor.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c007-->
The t(6;9) joins *NFIB*, a transcription factor gene, to the proto-oncogene *MYB*.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c008-->

**Which cell does it come from?** The likely origin is a cell committed to intercalated duct and myoepithelial differentiation.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c009-->

## Gross morphology

**What does it look like to the naked eye?** The source pages give no gross description, so none is stated here.

## Microscopic morphology

**What do the tumor cells look like, and how are they arranged?** Small size and scant cytoplasm mark the tumor cells.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c010-->
Growth is in solid sheets or in thin strands of cells.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c011-->
Cystic spaces form between joined cells; growth is then tubular, cribriform or solid.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c012-->
"Cribriform" means sieve-like.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c013-->
Cribriform spaces contain basophilic material.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c014-->
Because myoepithelial cells surround these spaces, they are pseudocysts.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c015-->
The "cylindromatous" look reflects deposits of basement-membrane-type material secreted by the tumor itself.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c016-->

**How is it graded?** Pathologists grade by how much tubular versus cribriform pattern is present.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c017-->
A tumor is high grade once solid growth exceeds 30%.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c018-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** on histologic sections, look for solid, tubular and cribriform growth.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c019-->
- The *MYB*–*NFIB* fusion from t(6;9) is the typical molecular finding.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c020-->
- The source pages say nothing about specimen type, special stains, immunohistochemistry or electron microscopy.

## Clinical presentation

**Why is ACC often painful?** The tumor invades perineural spaces, which is why it often hurts.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c021-->
In most cases, distant metastases are absent for years.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c022-->
Late diagnosis is common.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c023-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is the diagnosis made?** The source pages describe only the histologic patterns above and give no laboratory or imaging findings.

## Complications and prognosis

**Why is the long-term outlook poor?** Getting rid of the tumor completely is hard.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c024-->
Patients do badly in the long run.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c025-->
Recurrence after resection is frequent.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c026-->
Five years after diagnosis, only about 15% of patients with a high-grade tumor are alive.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c027-->

## High-yield summary

- **Cribriform**, tubular and solid patterns, with pseudocysts, define ACC histology.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c033-->
- More than 30% **solid** growth means high grade.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c034-->
- **Perineural** infiltration explains the pain.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c035-->
- The genetic hallmark is the ***MYB*–*NFIB*** fusion from t(6;9).<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c036-->
- A slow course does not spare patients: **diagnosis is often late** and the long-term outlook is poor.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c037-->
- Two-thirds arise in the **minor** salivary glands.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c038-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Are the cribriform spaces true glandular lumens?** No. Myoepithelial cells surround them, so they are pseudocysts.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c039-->

**Does a long quiet interval mean the patient is cured?** No. Metastases may stay absent for years, yet the long-term prognosis is poor.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c040-->

**Is a solid component a minor detail?** No. Above 30% solid growth the tumor is high grade.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c041-->

**Is ACC mainly a parotid tumor?** No. Two-thirds arise in the minor salivary glands.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c042-->

## Mnemonics

**SNL — "Sieve, Nerve, Late":** sieve-like (cribriform) growth, nerve (perineural) invasion, late diagnosis and poor long-term outcome.<!--c:adenoid-cystic-carcinoma-c043-->
