## Definition and epidemiology

**What is hypersensitivity vasculitis?** Drugs, bacterial products and other foreign agents are thought to provoke this broad group of vessel inflammations.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c001-->
Cutaneous vasculitis, cutaneous necrotizing venulitis and leukocytoclastic vasculitis are the names used when the disease stays mostly in the skin.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c002-->
"Leukocytoclastic" means that neutrophils are breaking down and leaving fragments.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c003-->
In "venulitis," the venules are the vessels mainly hit.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c004-->
In the systemic form, microscopic polyangiitis, only the smallest arteries and arterioles are affected.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c005-->
Immunologic injury to the vessel wall underlies Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis).<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c006-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What sets it off?**
- Thiazide diuretics, penicillin and aspirin are examples of drugs that can precede cutaneous vasculitis.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c007-->
- Bacterial endocarditis, tuberculosis, viral hepatitis, and strep or staph infections are frequent companions of skin vasculitis.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c008-->
- Several malignancies, dysproteinemias, [[sjogren-syndrome|Sjögren syndrome]], [[rheumatoid-arthritis]] or [[systemic-lupus-erythematosus|lupus]] can accompany the systemic form, which may also occur alone.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c009-->
- A viral infection often comes before Henoch–Schönlein purpura in children.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c010-->
- Drug exposure often lies behind Henoch–Schönlein purpura in adults.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c011-->

## Pathogenesis

**What damages the vessel wall?** In Henoch–Schönlein purpura, the vessel wall often receives deposits of IgA and complement complexes carried in the blood.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c012-->

## Gross morphology

**What do the lesions look like?** The legs carry most of the palpable purpura that marks the skin disease.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c013-->
In Henoch–Schönlein purpura, raised urticarial lesions often appear next to purpuric spots.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c014-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does a biopsy show?** Acute inflammation and fibrinoid necrosis are found in the superficial venules of the skin.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c015-->
In Henoch–Schönlein purpura, the infiltrate around the vessels contains neutrophils and eosinophils.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c016-->
The vessel wall shows fibrinoid necrosis.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c017-->
Platelet plugs lie in the lumens.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c018-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**Which stains or tests help?** The reference pages describe no special stains, immunohistochemistry or molecular tests for this disease.
P-ANCA is closely linked to microscopic polyangiitis.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c019-->

## Clinical presentation

**Which organs are involved?**
- Purpuric skin lesions can also be present in the systemic form.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c020-->
- Many of the organs hit in [[polyarteritis-nodosa]] are also hit by the systemic form.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c021-->
- Gut involvement in Henoch–Schönlein purpura shows as abdominal colic and bleeding.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c022-->
- Kidney involvement can end in renal failure.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c023-->
- In adults, Henoch–Schönlein purpura may be chronic.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c024-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What points to the diagnosis?** On the legs, expect palpable purpura; biopsy of superficial venules shows fibrinoid necrosis.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c025-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How does it end?** Cutaneous vasculitis is generally self-limited.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c026-->
In children, Henoch–Schönlein purpura is self-limited.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c027-->
Clinicians fear renal failure from rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis most in microscopic polyangiitis.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c028-->

## High-yield summary

- On the legs, **palpable purpura** is the characteristic skin sign.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c035-->
- **Leukocytoclastic** describes debris from disintegrating neutrophils.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c036-->
- The microscopic hallmark is acute inflammation with **fibrinoid necrosis** of superficial venules.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c037-->
- Triggers include **drugs** and **infections**.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c038-->
- In Henoch–Schönlein purpura, vessel walls collect **IgA and complement** complexes.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c039-->
- Henoch–Schönlein purpura can involve the **gut** and the **kidney**.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c040-->
- Cutaneous vasculitis is generally **self-limited**.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c041-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is leukocytoclastic vasculitis a disease of large arteries?** No. The skin form mainly involves venules.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c042-->
The systemic form involves only the smallest arteries and arterioles.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c043-->

**Does palpable purpura always mean kidney damage?** No. Skin vasculitis is generally self-limited.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c044-->
Renal failure may follow kidney involvement in Henoch–Schönlein purpura.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c045-->

**Is a drug always the trigger?** No. Infections such as streptococcal, staphylococcal and viral hepatitis are also common associations.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c046-->

## Mnemonics

**LEGS:** Leukocytoclastic debris, Elevated (palpable) purpura, Gets better alone, Small vessels. The typical skin lesion, palpable purpura, mostly sits on the legs.<!--c:hypersensitivity-vasculitis-c047-->
