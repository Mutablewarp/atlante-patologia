## Definition and epidemiology

**What is peptic ulcer disease?** Gastric juice erodes a patch of mucosa, mainly in the proximal duodenum but also in the stomach or other small bowel.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c001--> Most ulcers sit in the distal stomach or proximal duodenum, but rare ones arise in the esophagus or in a Meckel diverticulum lined by gastric-type tissue.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c002--> Acid and H. pylori infection are what gastric and duodenal ulcers share.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c003-->

**Who gets it?** Incidence now peaks between 30 and 60 years.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c004--> Duodenal ulcers have become much rarer in the last 30 years.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c005--> Duodenal ulcers are commoner in men, whereas gastric ulcers strike both sexes equally, mostly in middle age and later.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c006-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes a peptic ulcer?** No single cause accounts for the disease.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c007-->
- The antrum of almost every duodenal ulcer patient harbors H. pylori ([[helicobacter-pylori-gastritis]]).<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c008-->
- Few H. pylori carriers ever get a duodenal ulcer, so infection by itself cannot account for the disease.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c009-->
- Gastric ulcers without H. pylori, about one in four, probably sit on another kind of chronic gastritis.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c010-->
- NSAIDs, aspirin included, are linked to ulcers; aspirin matters most for gastric ulcers but also for duodenal ones.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c011-->
- Corticosteroids slightly raise the risk when given at high doses for a long time.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c012-->
- The ulcer risk from smoking is greatest in the stomach, though duodenal ulcers also rise.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c013-->
- Unusual ulcer sites, several ulcers at once and no response to treatment point to a gastrin-secreting neuroendocrine tumor (Zollinger–Ellison syndrome).<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c015-->

## Pathogenesis

**Why is acid essential?** No ulcer can start or last unless there is too much hydrochloric acid.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c018--> Lowering acid output promotes healing.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c019--> Only about a third of ulcer patients secrete excess acid, even though in duodenal ulcer parietal cell mass can reach twice normal.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c020-->

**How does H. pylori lead to a duodenal ulcer?** Gastrin rises and somatostatin falls under the influence of cytokines that inflammatory cells make in response to H. pylori.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c021--> H. pylori may make the duodenal bulb easier to injure by colonizing patches of gastric-type lining that acid has induced there.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c022--> Treating H. pylori shortens duodenal ulcer healing time and reduces recurrences.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c023-->

**How do gastric ulcers differ?** Before a gastric ulcer forms, H. pylori or chemical gastritis has nearly always injured the lining.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c025--> Most patients make less acid than normal.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c026--> Acid leaking back into the mucosa is one proposed factor.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c027--> Zollinger–Ellison syndrome floods the stomach with acid, yet the duodenum and jejunum ulcerate, seldom the stomach.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c029-->

## Gross morphology

**Where are the ulcers and what do they look like?** Ulcers favor two places: the lesser curvature, and the pylorus with the stomach and duodenum on either side of it.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c030--> Gastric ulcers are usually solitary and under 2 cm.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c031--> NSAIDs are commonly behind ulcers on the greater curvature, whereas lesser-curvature ulcers are often accompanied by chronic gastritis.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c032--> Margins tend to overhang, giving a punched-out crater.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c033--> Folds radiate toward the crater from the surrounding mucosa.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c034--> The rim is flat and the floor is clean and gray with fibrin.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c035--> Pyloric stenosis may follow when a prepyloric ulcer heals with severe scarring.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c037--> A duodenal ulcer is usually single, close to the pylorus, on the front or back wall of the bulb.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c038--> Kissing ulcers, a facing pair on opposite walls, are fairly common.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c039-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the base of an ulcer look like under the microscope?** Gastric and duodenal ulcers look alike.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c040--> Going from the lumen to the depth, the base has four layers:<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c041-->
1. Fibrinopurulent exudate on the surface.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c042-->
2. Necrotic tissue.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c043-->
3. Granulation tissue.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c044-->
4. Fibrosis, with variable chronic inflammation at the deepest point.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c045-->

After healing, scar tissue replaces any muscle layers the ulcer has penetrated.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c046--> Vessels at the margin are often thrombosed.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c047--> The adjacent mucosa is mildly hyperplastic.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c048--> As the ulcer heals, a single epithelial layer covers the defect.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c049--> Peptic duodenitis, with overgrown Brunner glands and gastric-type mucin cells, usually surrounds a duodenal ulcer.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c050-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** endoscopic biopsies from both the edge and the bed of the ulcer.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c051-->
- On the slide, read the ulcer base from the surface down as a stack of layers ending in fibrous scar.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c052-->
- The reference pages do not describe special stains, immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy or molecular tests for ulcers.

## Clinical presentation

**How does an ulcer present?** A gastric ulcer and a duodenal ulcer usually look the same on history and physical examination.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c053--> Classic duodenal ulcer pain is epigastric, arrives 1–3 hours after eating or wakes the patient at night.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c054--> Food and alkali ease the pain.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c055-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is the diagnosis made, and what is the trap?** Ulcerated gastric carcinoma and chronic peptic ulcer can resemble each other grossly.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c057--> Sample the margin and the deepest part of the ulcer, since the central area usually gives only dead tissue.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c058--> Iron-deficiency anemia or occult blood in stool may be the only sign of an ulcer that bleeds.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c059-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What can go wrong?** Perforation with peritonitis, obstruction and hemorrhage are the major complications.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c060--> Up to 20% of patients bleed, making hemorrhage the commonest complication; the bleeding is often occult.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c061--> Perforation affects 5%.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c063--> A third of these ulcers were previously silent.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c064--> The anterior duodenal wall is the usual site of perforation, and duodenal ulcers perforate more often than gastric ones.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c065--> Generalized peritonitis and free abdominal air (pneumoperitoneum) are likelier when the perforation is anterior.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c066--> A perforated ulcer of the stomach's back wall enters the lesser sac, which may confine inflammation.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c067--> When a gastric ulcer perforates, 10% to 40% of patients die, a rate two to four times that of duodenal ulcers.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c069--> In adults, ulcers are the leading cause of pyloric obstruction, though at most 10% of patients get it.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c070--> Having one ulcer type greatly raises the later risk of the other.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c072-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Ulcerated gastric carcinoma ([[gastric-adenocarcinoma]]) | Raised border, no clean fibrin floor and no radiating folds, unlike a benign ulcer.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c073--> | Biopsy of edge and bed |
| Zollinger–Ellison ulcers | Ulcers at odd sites, often more than one, that fail therapy.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c074--> | Locate the gastrinoma, which can be tiny.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c075--> |
| Reactive gastropathy ([[acute-gastritis-and-reactive-gastropathy]]) | Little inflammation and corkscrew-like pits point to reactive gastropathy, a hyperplastic mucosal reaction usually caused by NSAIDs.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c076--> | Gastric biopsy |

## High-yield summary

- **Duodenal ulcer**: nearly all patients carry **H. pylori**, yet few carriers get the ulcer.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c077-->
- **Four layers** of the base: exudate, necrosis, granulation tissue, fibrosis.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c078-->
- **Gastric ulcer**: biopsy edge and bed, since the center is only necrosis.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c079-->
- Main complications: **bleeding**, **perforation**, **pyloric obstruction**.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c081-->
- **Zollinger–Ellison syndrome**: a gastrinoma drives multiple ulcers in unusual sites.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c082-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does H. pylori infection always cause an ulcer?** No. Most carriers never get a duodenal ulcer.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c083-->

**Are gastric ulcers caused by too much acid?** Usually not. Most gastric ulcer patients make less acid than normal.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c085-->

**Is a biopsy of the ulcer center enough?** No. The middle is mostly dead tissue; sample the edge and bed.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c086-->

## Mnemonics

**BOP:** the three complications of an ulcer are Bleeding, Obstruction and Perforation.<!--c:peptic-ulcer-disease-c088-->
