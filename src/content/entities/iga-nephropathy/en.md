## Definition and epidemiology

**Which glomerulonephritis is defined by IgA in the mesangium?** Immune complexes that collect in the mesangium and are dominated by IgA, chiefly IgA1, cause this glomerulonephritis.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c001-->

- It is the most common glomerulonephritis in developed countries.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c002-->
- It makes up 40% of glomerulonephritis cases in Asia, 20% in Europe and 10% in the United States.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c003-->
- Native Americans are often affected; black patients are rarely affected.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c004-->
- Diagnosis most often falls between ages 15 and 30, mostly in men.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What brings on an episode?** Flares are often set off by an infection of the airways or the gut.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c006-->

Food or microbial antigens met at mucosal surfaces drive an IgA response, and its complexes lodge in the glomeruli.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c007-->

The antigens inside the deposits are still unknown.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c008-->

## Pathogenesis

**Why does IgA collect in the mesangium?**

- Many patients have more IgA1 in the blood than normal, and it is abnormal; some travels as aggregates or bound in immune complexes.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c009-->
- The deposits contain mostly IgA1, and IgA1 is the only IgA subclass whose hinge carries O-linked sugar chains.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c010-->
- In patients, the ends of these chains carry less galactose and less sialic acid than normal.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c011-->
- The body can make autoantibodies, IgG among them, against the altered chains, and these join the abnormal IgA1 in immune complexes.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c012-->
- Circulating IgA aggregates may build up because abnormally sugared IgA1 binds its receptors poorly and is cleared more slowly.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c013-->
- The mesangium catches these circulating aggregates.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c014-->
- It is not settled whether the deposits arrive preformed or form in place.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c015-->

**Which complement pathway is used?** The alternative pathway is the probable one: C1q and C4 are missing from the deposits, while C3 and properdin are found there.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c016-->

## Gross morphology

Not applicable.

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the glomerulus look like by light microscopy?** Glomeruli range from normal-looking (mild) to scarred, as in chronic sclerosing glomerulonephritis (severe); between lie mesangial hypercellularity and proliferative glomerulonephritis, focal or diffuse.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c017-->

- Focal proliferative glomerulonephritis is what the first biopsy most often shows.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c018-->
- With PAS, look for patchy mesangial widening, with extra cells and matrix, produced by the deposits.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c019-->
- Crescents turn up mainly in unusually severe disease.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c020-->
- The lesions resemble those of lupus nephritis, though usually milder.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c021-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which method?**

**How it is studied:**

- The specimen is a kidney biopsy.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c022-->
- Immunofluorescence is essential for the diagnosis.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c023-->
- The mesangium must stain for IgA at least as much as for IgG or IgM.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c024-->
- C3 staining nearly always accompanies the IgA.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c025-->
- Electron microscopy shows electron-dense deposits in the mesangium.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c026-->
- In severe disease, immune complexes usually extend to the capillary walls on electron microscopy.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c027-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does a patient come to attention?** Patients present in different ways, because the lesions differ in severity.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c028-->

- About 40% of patients have microscopic hematuria without symptoms.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c029-->
- About 40% have recurrent episodes of visible blood in the urine.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c030-->
- About 10% have nephrotic syndrome.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c031-->
- About 10% have renal failure.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c032-->

**What is the link with infection?** Episodes come and go, and a flare often appears together with an upper respiratory infection.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c033-->

**How is it linked to IgA vasculitis?** Systemic IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein purpura) inflames small vessels throughout the body, through IgA deposited in them.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c034-->

Skin involvement causes purpura and gut involvement causes abdominal pain.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c035-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What proves the diagnosis?** A kidney biopsy in which immunofluorescence shows dominant mesangial IgA.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c036-->

Hematuria, microscopic or visible, is the usual presentation, each form in about 40% of patients.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c037-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How fast does it progress?** Slowly. Complete recovery is rare, and after 10 years about 20% of patients have reached end-stage renal failure.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c038-->

Capillary-wall IgA, added to mesangial IgA, tends to mark severe cases and poorer outcome.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c039-->

**What happens after a transplant?** Graft function is usually preserved after transplant, even though IgA deposits may reappear.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c040-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What else gives glomerular deposits of immune proteins?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| IgA-dominant infectious glomerulonephritis | Also IgA-dominant, but an infection is the cause; methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* is the usual culprit.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c041--> | Search for the infection |
| [[lupus-nephritis]] | A similar spectrum of lesions, but usually more severe.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c042--> | Pattern of immunofluorescence |
| [[crescentic-glomerulonephritis\|Anti-GBM glomerulonephritis]] | Linear IgG along the basement membrane, not mesangial IgA, and crescents in over 90% of cases.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c043--> | Immunofluorescence: linear versus mesangial |

## High-yield summary

- The cause is deposition of **IgA1 immune complexes** in the **mesangium**.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c044-->
- In many patients, **under-galactosylated IgA1** at the hinge region is probably an important contributor.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c045-->
- **Immunofluorescence** showing IgA at least equal to IgG or IgM is the diagnostic finding.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c046-->
- Deposits with **C3** and **properdin** but no C1q or C4 point to the **alternative pathway**.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c047-->
- Flares often follow **respiratory or gut infections**.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c048-->
- It is the **most common glomerulonephritis** in developed countries.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c049-->
- Slow course: **1 in 5** reach **end-stage renal failure** by 10 years.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c050-->
- **IgA vasculitis** (Henoch–Schönlein purpura) is the systemic IgA-deposition disease with purpura and abdominal pain.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c051-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a normal light microscopy exclude IgA nephropathy?** No. Light microscopy can show no change, so immunofluorescence is needed.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c052-->

**Is IgA2 the immunoglobulin in the deposits?** No. The deposits are mainly IgA1.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c053-->

**Is IgA nephropathy the same as IgA vasculitis?** No. Both involve IgA deposits, but vasculitis is systemic and strikes skin and gut.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c054-->

**Is the classic complement pathway activated?** No. C1q and C4 are absent, so the alternative pathway is the likely route.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c055-->

## Mnemonics

**MESA:** Mesangial IgA, Episodic flares with infections, Slow progression, Alternative complement pathway.<!--c:iga-nephropathy-c056-->
