## Definition and epidemiology

**What is giant cell arteritis, and who gets it?** The temporal artery is the usual target of this granulomatous inflammation of large arteries.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c001--> The disease is not confined to that vessel: the aorta, its branches and other cranial arteries may also be involved.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c002--> Aortic aneurysm and aortic dissection can occur.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c003--> It is the most frequent of all vasculitides.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c004--> Under 50 years of age it is uncommon; the average patient falls ill at about 70.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c005--> The risk grows as people get older.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c006--> Women are a little more often affected than men.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c007-->

**Which disease is the counterpart in young patients?** Takayasu arteritis also targets the aorta and its branches, but in younger patients.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c008--> Under 30 is the typical age, and women make up about 90% of patients.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c009-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes giant cell arteritis?** Nobody knows the cause.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c010--> The disease is linked to HLA-DR4.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c011--> Cases in first-degree relatives point to a genetic component.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c012--> The cause of Takayasu arteritis is also unknown, and an autoimmune mechanism has been suggested.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**Which cells drive the damage?** Activated macrophages and CD4+ helper T cells point to an immune mechanism.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c014--> The disease is also associated with one specific ICAM-1 polymorphism.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c015--> B lymphocytes are absent.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c016--> MMPs, made by macrophages where intima meets media, break down the matrix.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c017--> ANCA is not found.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c018--> The disease may be related to the rheumatoid diseases: it causes generalized muscle aching and shows up in many places.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c019-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the diseased artery look like?** Nodular thickening makes the affected artery cord-like.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c020--> A thrombus can block the lumen, which otherwise narrows to a slit.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c021--> In Takayasu arteritis, scattered intimal plaques stand out on a thickened aortic wall.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c022--> Flow is often impeded because aortic branches develop a focal stenosis or occlusion.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c023--> The name "pulseless disease" is sometimes applied when the subclavian arteries are affected.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c024--> The distal thoracic and abdominal aorta often carry aneurysms of different sizes.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c025-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What do we see under the microscope?** Granulomatous inflammation fills the intima and media.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c026--> Eosinophils and neutrophils join the infiltrate in varying numbers, alongside macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c027--> The usual position of giant cells is next to the internal elastic lamina.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c028--> Case by case, the giant cell count is very variable.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c029--> Both foreign-body and Langhans types occur.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c030--> Where there is necrosis, the internal elastic lamina is broken up and distorted.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c031--> Once the lesion is advanced, the lamina can vanish completely.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c032--> Giant cells occasionally contain pieces of this lamina.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c033--> Fibrosis of the media and marked thickening of the intima characterize late stages.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c034--> Organization and recanalization follow when a thrombus has blocked the lumen.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c035-->

Few Langhans giant cells appear in early Takayasu arteritis, among neutrophils and mononuclear cells; the entire wall is acutely inflamed.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c036--> In late disease the intima proliferates heavily and the wall is scarred.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c037--> Takayasu arteritis inflames the vasa vasorum much as syphilitic aortitis does.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c038--> Secondary atherosclerosis can mask the original disease.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c039-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** under the microscope, a temporal artery section shows a fragmented internal elastic lamina with giant cells beside it.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c040-->
- **Sampling:** a temporal artery biopsy can miss the diagnosis, since lesions are patchy; this happens in up to 40% of classic cases.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c041-->
- Special stains, immunohistochemistry and molecular tests are not described in the reference pages used for this card.

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** Temple pain that throbs, together with headache, is the typical presentation.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c042--> The skin above the vessel can be red, swollen and sore.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c043--> Weight loss and fever, with a general feeling of being unwell, may appear first.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c044--> Shoulders and hips may ache or feel stiff.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c045--> Nearly half of the patients report problems with vision.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c046--> Sometimes quickly, visual loss that was transient turns permanent, and one eye or both can be affected.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c047--> A rare and sometimes fatal complication is infarction in the gut, the central nervous system or the heart.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c048-->

In Takayasu arteritis, early disease can cause shortness of breath, visual disturbances and dizziness, with general malaise; syncope is occasional.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c049--> Later, arms or legs can show intermittent claudication.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c050--> A pulse in one limb can disappear, and blood pressure may become asymmetric between the sides.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c051--> Renal artery stenosis or aortic coarctation may explain the hypertension.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c052--> Most patients end up with heart failure.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c053--> Total blindness is the extreme of visual loss, with field defects at the milder end.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c054-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How do we tell it from Takayasu arteritis?** Age at onset helps separate the two.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c055--> The reference pages used for this card give no laboratory or imaging criteria.

## Complications and prognosis

**How does it end?** Most cases burn out on their own, with symptoms fading over 6 to 12 months.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c056--> Corticosteroids work strikingly well; symptoms usually ease within days.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c057--> Early Takayasu arteritis also responds to corticosteroids.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c058--> Reconstructive surgery is needed for later Takayasu lesions.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c059-->

## High-yield summary

- **Granulomatous large-vessel arteritis**, most often of the **temporal artery**, in older adults.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c068-->
- **Skip lesions** make a temporal artery biopsy non-diagnostic in up to 40% of classic cases.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c069-->
- **Giant cells** lie at the **internal elastic lamina**, which is fragmented.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c070-->
- **Blindness** can become permanent, whether one eye or both are affected.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c071-->
- **Corticosteroids** relieve symptoms within days.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c072-->
- **Takayasu arteritis** is the young counterpart: mostly women under 30, aorta and branches, "pulseless disease."<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c073-->
- **ANCA is absent** in giant cell arteritis.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c074-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a negative temporal artery biopsy rule out giant cell arteritis?** No. A sample can miss patchy inflammation even in a patient who looks classic.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c075-->

**Is giant cell arteritis limited to the temporal artery?** No. The aorta, its branches and other cranial arteries can be involved too.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c076-->

**Is Takayasu arteritis the same disease as giant cell arteritis?** No. Both affect large vessels, but Takayasu arteritis mostly strikes women under 30 and centers on the aorta and its branches.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c077-->

**Is giant cell arteritis an ANCA-associated vasculitis?** No. ANCA is absent.<!--c:giant-cell-arteritis-c078-->
