## Definition and epidemiology

Atrophy is a reduction in the size of a cell or of an organ.<!--c:atrophy-c001--> Together with hypertrophy, hyperplasia and metaplasia, it is one of the main ways cells adapt to persistent stress.<!--c:atrophy-c002--> Reduced function can be both its cause and its consequence.<!--c:atrophy-c003--> An atrophic cell has adjusted to a less demanding environment and remains viable.<!--c:atrophy-c004--> A small organ can result from two different processes: shrinkage of its cells, which is reversible, or loss of its cells, which is not.<!--c:atrophy-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Physiologic atrophy** follows normal falls in hormone levels:<!--c:atrophy-c006-->

- the endometrium after menopause, as estrogen declines;<!--c:atrophy-c007-->
- the breast once lactation is over.<!--c:atrophy-c008-->

**Pathologic atrophy** has several triggers, many of them the mirror image of the stimuli for hypertrophy:<!--c:atrophy-c009-->

- **Disuse (reduced workload):** muscles of a limb immobilized in a cast shrink; muscle supplied by an injured motor nerve loses both mass and strength (denervation also removes trophic signals; see below).<!--c:atrophy-c010-->
- **Reduced blood supply:** cells that survive in poorly perfused tissue, such as the rim around an infarct in the heart or kidney, may become atrophic.<!--c:atrophy-c011-->
- **Inadequate nutrition:** starvation wastes skeletal muscle and adipose tissue; in marasmus the heart and liver waste as well.<!--c:atrophy-c012-->
- **Loss of trophic signals:** denervation or loss of endocrine stimulation; for example, pituitary damage (Sheehan syndrome) leads to atrophy of the thyroid and adrenal cortex.<!--c:atrophy-c013-->
- **Pressure:** sustained local pressure on the skin of bedridden patients; centrilobular hepatocytes in congestive heart failure, where impaired venous outflow raises sinusoidal pressure.<!--c:atrophy-c014-->
- **Aging:** neurons, cardiac myocytes and skeletal muscle fibers become smaller or fewer; age-related muscle loss is called sarcopenia.<!--c:atrophy-c015-->
- **Chronic disease (cachexia):** advanced cancer, congestive heart failure, COPD, AIDS and rheumatoid arthritis; the tissue loss is greater than reduced food intake can explain.<!--c:atrophy-c016-->
- **Glucocorticoids** are a long-recognized cause of muscle wasting.<!--c:atrophy-c017-->

## Pathogenesis

**An active balance, not passive decay.** Cell size is set by the equilibrium between anabolic and catabolic activity.<!--c:atrophy-c018--> Like hypertrophy, atrophy is an active process in which some cell components are made and others are selectively removed.<!--c:atrophy-c019--> In atrophy the net balance favors breakdown: the cell degrades more protein than it makes.<!--c:atrophy-c020--> Skeletal muscle is the best-studied model.<!--c:atrophy-c021-->

**Two degradation routes.**

- **Ubiquitin–proteasome system:** selected proteins, including those that sustain protein synthesis, are ubiquitinated more often and destroyed by the proteasome.<!--c:atrophy-c022-->
- **Autophagy:** autophagic activity increases as well.<!--c:atrophy-c023-->
- Actomyosin is removed by ubiquitin-dependent pathways, and its proteasomal breakdown is much faster if caspase-3 or calpain has cleaved it first.<!--c:atrophy-c024-->

**Signaling switches in muscle.**

- **IGF-1 → IRS-1 → PI3K → Akt** is the main anti-atrophy axis; in adults, IGF-1 and Akt mostly hold atrophy back rather than drive growth directly.<!--c:atrophy-c025-->
- **FOXO** transcription factors are the pro-atrophy effectors: they increase production of E3 ubiquitin ligases (such as MuRF and MAFbx) and inhibit mTORC1.<!--c:atrophy-c026--> Akt keeps FOXO in check.<!--c:atrophy-c027-->
- **TGF-β family (e.g., myostatin)** signals through Smads, which inhibit Akt and activate FOXO.<!--c:atrophy-c028--> Nutrient deprivation increases these cytokines.<!--c:atrophy-c029-->
- **Inflammatory cytokines** (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) promote atrophy in chronic inflammatory states.<!--c:atrophy-c030--> Tumors also break down the body's fat stores, and the cytokines freed in that process feed muscle wasting.<!--c:atrophy-c031-->
- **Cbl-b**, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, tags IRS-1 for degradation and so cuts off the growth signal.<!--c:atrophy-c032-->

Many stimuli do not trigger atrophy directly; they switch off the opposing growth program, and the result depends on which side prevails.<!--c:atrophy-c033-->

**Cell loss.** In the very old, when replacement by progenitor cells cannot keep pace with cell loss, the heart, skeletal muscle and brain become atrophic.<!--c:atrophy-c034--> In organs that depend on hormones, apoptosis helps restore the cell balance when the stimulus fades, for example as the breast involutes after breastfeeding ends (see [[apoptosis]]).<!--c:atrophy-c070-->

## Gross morphology

The affected organ or tissue is reduced in size.<!--c:atrophy-c035--> In an atrophic brain the gyri are thin and the sulci wide.<!--c:atrophy-c036--> The ventricles enlarge to take up the space left by the lost tissue (hydrocephalus ex vacuo), a compensatory change rather than a disturbance of cerebrospinal fluid flow.<!--c:atrophy-c071--> The endometrium of an elderly woman is thin compared with that of reproductive age.<!--c:atrophy-c037-->

## Microscopic morphology

- Cells are smaller than their normal counterparts, as seen in myocytes and adipocytes during starvation.<!--c:atrophy-c038-->
- In denervated skeletal muscle, fibers lose cytoplasmic protein, so their nuclei appear relatively more prominent.<!--c:atrophy-c039-->
- Cut across, a denervated fiber shrinks to a small angular profile wedged between normal fibers; such fibers are scattered at first, then gather in small and later larger groups.<!--c:atrophy-c072--> If reinnervation fails, the fibers eventually vanish and fat and fibrous tissue take their place.<!--c:atrophy-c073-->
- The atrophic postmenopausal endometrium contains only a few small glands, some of them cystic.<!--c:atrophy-c040--> Its residual glands have a thin epithelial lining and lie in a fibrous, collagen-rich stroma; when the glands are cystically dilated, the picture is called senile cystic atrophy.<!--c:atrophy-c074-->
- Lipofuscin, the golden-brown "wear-and-tear" pigment of long-lived cells such as neurons and cardiac myocytes, can be more conspicuous in atrophic organs.<!--c:atrophy-c041-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Specimen:** muscle atrophy is assessed on a biopsy of a moderately affected muscle, often the quadriceps or biceps (the gastrocnemius together with the sural nerve when a neuropathy is suspected), because a spared muscle may look normal and a very weak one may show only end-stage fibro-fatty tissue.<!--c:atrophy-c075-->
- **Handling:** the sample is divided between formalin, unfixed freezing for enzyme histochemistry (it reveals enzyme activity, while immunohistochemistry only reveals that a protein is there) and glutaraldehyde for electron microscopy.<!--c:atrophy-c076-->
- **H&E and myosin ATPase:** on H&E, selective atrophy of type II fibers produces angular fibers that mimic denervation; myosin ATPase (type I fibers pale, type II dark at alkaline pH) shows that in this pattern, seen with chronic disuse, corticosteroids, old age or cachexia, every angular fiber is type II.<!--c:atrophy-c077-->
- **Nonspecific esterase and NADH-tetrazolium reductase (NADH-TR):** denervated atrophic fibers, of both types, take up these stains very darkly, unlike fibers shrunk by disuse or wasting.<!--c:atrophy-c078-->
- **Fiber-type grouping:** on ATPase, patches of one fiber type lying beside patches of the other prove that denervation was followed by reinnervation; if denervation goes on, whole enlarged motor units atrophy together (grouped atrophy, typical of chronic denervating diseases such as ALS).<!--c:atrophy-c079-->
- **Limits:** the biopsy shows that denervation is present and whether it is recent or chronic, but not its cause: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and diabetic neuropathy can look alike.<!--c:atrophy-c080-->

## Clinical presentation

- **Cachexia**, muscle wasting caused by disease or starvation, is dangerous in itself: it can bring death closer whatever the course of the underlying disease, and it blunts both the benefit of treatment and the capacity to recover.<!--c:atrophy-c042-->
- **Sarcopenia** erodes mobility and independence in older people and is a major driver of care costs.<!--c:atrophy-c043-->
- Different fibers are hit in different settings: heart failure mainly affects type I fibers, because of poor oxygen delivery, while age-related sarcopenia mainly affects type II fibers.<!--c:atrophy-c044-->
- Age-related shrinkage of muscle, heart and brain contributes to weakness and debility.<!--c:atrophy-c045-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

Not applicable.

## Complications and prognosis

- Atrophy from cell shrinkage is reversible: once a cast is removed and the limb is used again, the muscle fibers regain their normal size and function.<!--c:atrophy-c046-->
- Atrophy from cell loss, as in the brain in Alzheimer disease, cannot be reversed.<!--c:atrophy-c047-->
- Pressure atrophy of the skin can progress to decubitus ulcers (bed sores).<!--c:atrophy-c048-->
- Hormone-driven atrophy is used in therapy: androgen-dependent prostate cancer and estrogen-responsive breast cancer may regress under hormone antagonists.<!--c:atrophy-c049-->
- Muscle loss can be slowed or prevented by measures that favor hypertrophy; some data suggest that angiotensin antagonists may help older people keep their muscle mass, but the benefit is not firmly established.<!--c:atrophy-c050-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[hypertrophy]] | The converse change: larger cells, with protein synthesis exceeding degradation (Akt and mTORC1 active).<!--c:atrophy-c051--> | Cell size compared with normal tissue of the same type |
| [[hyperplasia]] | The organ grows because the number of cells increases, not their size.<!--c:atrophy-c052--> | Cell count versus cell size |
| Atrophy from cell loss vs cell shrinkage (subtype distinction) | Fewer cells rather than smaller cells; the organ cannot regain its size.<!--c:atrophy-c053--> | Cell count/density on histology (fewer cells vs smaller cells) |
| [[pigment-accumulation]] (lipofuscin) | Golden-brown pigment that builds up with age in postmitotic cells and may be prominent in atrophic organs; a deposit, not a change in cell size.<!--c:atrophy-c054--> | Pigment granules on H&E |

## High-yield summary

- Atrophy = decreased **cell or organ size**; an adaptation in which the cell stays **viable**.<!--c:atrophy-c055-->
- **Physiologic**: **endometrium after menopause**, **breast after lactation**.<!--c:atrophy-c056-->
- **Pathologic** causes: **disuse**, **ischemia**, **malnutrition**, **loss of trophic signals** (denervation, endocrine), **pressure**, **aging**, **chronic disease**.<!--c:atrophy-c057-->
- Mechanism: protein **degradation exceeds synthesis**, through the **ubiquitin–proteasome system** and **autophagy**.<!--c:atrophy-c058-->
- **FOXO** raises **E3 ubiquitin ligases** and promotes atrophy; **IGF-1/Akt** restrains it.<!--c:atrophy-c059-->
- Cell **shrinkage** is reversible; cell **loss** (e.g., Alzheimer brain) is not.<!--c:atrophy-c060-->
- **Cachexia** and **sarcopenia** are the major clinical forms of muscle atrophy.<!--c:atrophy-c061-->
- **Denervation atrophy**: small **angular** fibers of both types, dark with **nonspecific esterase/NADH-TR**; **fiber-type grouping** on ATPase = denervation followed by reinnervation.<!--c:atrophy-c081-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- **Atrophy is not passive wasting.** It is a regulated process with selective synthesis and degradation of cell components.<!--c:atrophy-c062-->
- A small organ does not always mean small cells: it may reflect cell loss, which is irreversible.<!--c:atrophy-c063-->
- Cachexia is not simply undernutrition: tissue loss exceeds the calorie deficit and reflects cytokines and other mediators.<!--c:atrophy-c064-->
- Atrophy is not always pathologic: the postmenopausal endometrium and the post-lactation breast are physiologic examples.<!--c:atrophy-c065-->
- In adults, IGF-1 and Akt mainly prevent atrophy rather than directly cause growth.<!--c:atrophy-c066-->
- Lipofuscin is not necessarily inert: it may both result from and add to oxidant stress in the cell.<!--c:atrophy-c067-->
- Angular atrophic fibers on H&E do not prove denervation: selective type II atrophy (corticosteroids, disuse) looks the same until ATPase, esterase and NADH-TR are done.<!--c:atrophy-c082-->
- A stroke or multiple sclerosis paralyses muscle through the upper motor neuron; the lower motor neuron is spared, so the muscle shows only diffuse, nonspecific shrinkage, not the denervation pattern.<!--c:atrophy-c083-->

## Mnemonics

**DIP CANE** (original), the causes of pathologic atrophy, pictured as a shrinking old man leaning on a cane: **D**isuse, **I**schemia, **P**ressure, **C**hronic disease, **A**ging, **N**utrient lack, **E**ndocrine or nerve signal loss.<!--c:atrophy-c068-->

**"Akt keeps, FOXO frees"** (original): Akt keeps muscle protein in place; FOXO frees it for the proteasome by switching on ubiquitin ligases.<!--c:atrophy-c069-->
