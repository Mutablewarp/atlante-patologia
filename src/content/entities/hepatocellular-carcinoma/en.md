## Definition and epidemiology

**What is hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and where is it common?** HCC is a malignant tumor of hepatocytes.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c001--> It is the most common primary liver cancer.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c002--> It is also a leading cause of cancer death.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c003--> Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia see far more HCC than industrialized Western countries, where it is uncommon.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c004--> Most of the recent rise in Western incidence is seen in patients with chronic hepatitis C.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which infections, diseases and toxins lead to HCC?**

- HBV-endemic countries account for most HCC cases.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c006-->
- Infants infected with HBV by their mother around birth are at high risk of developing HCC later in life.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c007-->
- HCV is the infection linked to most HCC in Japan, North America and Europe.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c008-->
- A patient infected with both HBV and HCV is at higher risk than one infected with only one of them.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c009-->
- Most virus-related HCC arises in a cirrhotic liver.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c010-->
- Many HBV-related tumors, however, arise in non-cirrhotic liver.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c011-->
- Other causes drive the cancer, and cirrhosis may add to their effect without being sufficient by itself.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c012-->
- How alcoholic cirrhosis adds a small HCC risk is unknown.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c013-->
- It is hard to isolate the effect of alcohol itself, since infection with HBV or HCV is frequent among alcoholics.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c014-->
- People with α1-antitrypsin deficiency or hemochromatosis have a notably raised HCC risk.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c015-->
- Primary biliary cholangitis with cirrhosis, Wilson disease and autoimmune hepatitis are associated with a lower risk.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c016-->
- In the developing tropics, fungal aflatoxin B1 mainly taints food.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c017-->
- It causes liver cancer in several animal species.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c018-->
- A person exposed to both aflatoxin and HBV faces a much higher risk than either exposure alone would give, because the two act synergistically.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c019-->

## Pathogenesis

**How do the viruses and aflatoxin cause cancer?** Viral gene expression, together with insertion of HBV DNA into the host genome, is probably what drives the cancer.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c020--> The HBV X gene encodes HBxAg, which turns on certain oncogenes and disables tumor suppressor proteins.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c021--> Viral proteins likely act on cellular constituents in HCV infection, yet the route to cancer remains poorly understood.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c022--> HCCs from aflatoxin-endemic areas often carry TP53 mutations.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c023--> In experiments aflatoxin B1 induces a G-to-T substitution at codon 249, which is also the usual TP53 lesion in these tumors.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c024-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the tumor look like on the cut surface?** Single or multiple, the masses are tan and soft, and bleed in places.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c025--> Bile production by the tumor can show up as a green color in some cases.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c026--> It tends to invade portal and hepatic veins.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c027--> Tumor growing in a hepatic vein can continue into the vena cava and as far as the right atrium.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c028--> Lungs and portal lymph nodes are the preferred sites of metastasis.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c029-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What patterns do the tumor cells form?** HCC ranges from tumors that look almost like normal liver to anaplastic ones.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c030--> The trabecular pattern reproduces the plate-like architecture of normal liver.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c031--> Between the plates run sinusoids with an endothelial lining.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c032--> A lumen, possibly containing bile, is ringed by tumor cells in the pseudoglandular (acinar) pattern.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c033--> These spaces are not true glands.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c034--> Neither pattern has prognostic value.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c035-->

**What is the fibrolamellar variant?** This uncommon variant mostly affects adolescents and young adults, and the surrounding liver is not cirrhotic.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c036--> Pale, fibrinogen-rich bodies sit in the cytoplasm of big pink cells that grow in clusters.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c037--> The tumor cells lie within thick collagen fibers.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c038-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**

- **How it is studied:** on routine H&E, check whether the cells form plates with sinusoids or surround lumina that contain bile.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c039-->
- In the fibrolamellar variant, look for cytoplasmic pale bodies that contain fibrinogen.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c040-->
- In HCC from aflatoxin-endemic areas, DNA analysis often finds the TP53 codon 249 G-to-T change.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c041-->

The reference pages do not describe sampling, special stains or immunohistochemistry for this tumor.

## Clinical presentation

**How does HCC present?** Typically the tumor shows up as a mass that hurts and keeps growing.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c042--> HCC can secrete hormones ectopically, causing paraneoplastic effects such as polycythemia, hypoglycemia or hypercalcemia.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c043-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which blood test is linked to HCC?** Serum α-fetoprotein (AFP) is often elevated.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c044--> Because AFP also rises in some extrahepatic disorders and in liver disease of both benign and malignant type, it is not specific.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c045--> Tumors are found early when high-risk patients are under surveillance.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c046-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How does HCC end, and what treatment is possible?** Late-stage HCC carries a dismal prognosis.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c047--> Patients die of cirrhotic complications, cancer wasting, or tumor rupture with hemorrhage into the abdomen.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c048--> Segmental resection gives acceptable tumor-free survival when the tumor is small and limited to a single lobe.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c049--> Local ablation can delay the tumor's progression.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c050--> Transplantation keeps cirrhotic patients with a low tumor burden tumor-free longer than other options.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c051--> Fibrolamellar tumors do about as well as other HCCs arising in non-cirrhotic liver.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c052-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What can look like HCC, or be confused with it?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [[cholangiocarcinoma]] | Marked fibrosis is common, and the tumor cells are small and cuboidal, arranged in ducts or glands.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c053--> The gland-like spaces of pseudoglandular HCC are not true glands, so they do not make a cholangiocarcinoma.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c054--> | H&E architecture |
| Combined hepatocellular–cholangiocarcinoma | A tumor combining the morphologies of HCC and of cholangiocarcinoma is classified as a combined type.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c055--> | Sampling of both components on H&E |
| Fibrolamellar carcinoma (variant) | No cirrhosis; thick collagen surrounds big pink cells that contain pale bodies.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c056--> | H&E morphology |

## High-yield summary

- Chronic **HBV** and **HCV** infection are the main viral causes of HCC.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c057-->
- Most virus-related HCC arises in **cirrhosis**.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c058-->
- **Aflatoxin B1** and HBV act synergistically.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c059-->
- Aflatoxin produces the **TP53 codon 249** G-to-T mutation in experiments.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c060-->
- **Trabecular** and **pseudoglandular** patterns have no prognostic value.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c061-->
- **Fibrolamellar** HCC arises in young people without cirrhosis.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c062-->
- **AFP** is often raised but not specific.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c063-->
- HCC invades **portal and hepatic veins** and favors metastasis to the **lungs** and portal lymph nodes.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c064-->
- **Transplantation** keeps cirrhotic patients with little tumor tumor-free longest.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c065-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is cirrhosis required for HCC?** No. Many HBV-related tumors arise in non-cirrhotic liver; cirrhosis magnifies the risk from other causes.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c066-->

**Does a gland-like pattern mean cholangiocarcinoma?** No. The spaces of pseudoglandular HCC are not true glands.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c067-->

**Does a raised AFP prove HCC?** No. Other liver diseases, benign or malignant, can raise it too.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c068-->

**Does the histologic pattern predict outcome?** No. Whether the pattern is trabecular or pseudoglandular says nothing particular about prognosis.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c069-->

**Is fibrolamellar HCC a tumor of cirrhosis in older adults?** No. Its typical patients are adolescents and young adults, and the liver is not cirrhotic.<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c070-->

## Mnemonics

**How can I remember the main risk factors?** ACHE: Aflatoxin, Cirrhosis, Hepatitis B and C, Excess iron (hemochromatosis).<!--c:hepatocellular-carcinoma-c071-->
