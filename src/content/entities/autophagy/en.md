## Definition and epidemiology

Autophagy ("self-eating") is an evolutionarily conserved process in which the cell hands parts of its own contents to lysosomes for digestion.<!--c:autophagy-c001--> It works at a basal level all the time and is required for normal homeostasis and cell survival.<!--c:autophagy-c002--> Stress sharply recruits it, making it a central mechanism of cellular adaptation.<!--c:autophagy-c003--> All forms end in lysosomal breakdown; they differ in regulation, cargo, target recognition and delivery route.<!--c:autophagy-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

- **Nutrient lack:** bulk autophagy keeps cells alive during starvation and when blood supply is poor.<!--c:autophagy-c005-->
- **Housekeeping:** nutrient recycling, clearance of misfolded proteins and damaged organelles, antigen presentation and inflammation.<!--c:autophagy-c006-->
- **Cell stress:** chaperone-mediated autophagy runs at a low constitutive level and rises with starvation, oxidant stress and toxins.<!--c:autophagy-c007-->
- **Intracellular pathogens:** pathogen-sensing pathways activate IRGM and TRIM proteins, which help assemble the initiation complexes that launch xenophagy.<!--c:autophagy-c008-->
- **Ischemia and reperfusion:** falling ATP, ROS-driven assembly of Beclin-1/class III PI3K complexes and accumulating ubiquitinated aggregates all switch autophagy on.<!--c:autophagy-c009-->
- **Fasting:** periodic fasting activates autophagy, which may partly explain its supposed health benefits.<!--c:autophagy-c010-->

## Pathogenesis

**Types of autophagy.**

- **Macroautophagy:** the form we know most about; a large bite of cytoplasm is enclosed wholesale, taking organelles and both soluble and clumped proteins with it.<!--c:autophagy-c011-->
- **Microautophagy:** the lysosomal membrane folds inward and swallows cytosolic cargo directly; it is mostly constitutive and maintains membrane and organelle turnover.<!--c:autophagy-c012-->
- **Chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA):** a chaperone such as Hsc70 escorts a selected protein to the lysosomal receptor LAMP-2A, and the protein crosses the membrane with no vesicle involved.<!--c:autophagy-c013-->
- **Xenophagy:** a selective macroautophagy aimed at intracellular microbes.<!--c:autophagy-c014-->
- **Mitophagy:** selective autophagic removal of damaged mitochondria, for example during adaptation to exercise.<!--c:autophagy-c015-->

**Regulation: mTORC1 brakes, AMPK accelerates.**

- The machinery is always present and is switched on by post-translational changes (phosphorylation, ubiquitination), not new transcription, so it responds within moments.<!--c:autophagy-c016-->
- **Fed state:** insulin and IGF-1 signal through class I PI3K and Akt to activate **mTORC1**, which blocks autophagy.<!--c:autophagy-c017--> mTORC1 binds and inhibits the kinase **ULK1**.<!--c:autophagy-c018-->
- **Starved state:** amino acids become scarce, growth factor signals fall and the AMP/ATP ratio climbs, activating **AMPK**.<!--c:autophagy-c019--> AMPK suppresses mTORC1 through the tuberous sclerosis complex, freeing ULK1, and also phosphorylates ULK1 directly.<!--c:autophagy-c020-->
- ULK1 activates **Beclin-1**, and a complex of Beclin-1 with class III PI3K sets autophagosome formation in motion.<!--c:autophagy-c021-->
- AMPK and mTORC1 oppose each other across many adaptive responses, and AMPK as a rule increases autophagy.<!--c:autophagy-c022-->

**Autophagosome formation.**

1. An open membrane, the **phagophore**, wraps around a portion of cytoplasm, and its edges fuse to form a closed **autophagosome**.<!--c:autophagy-c023-->
2. Its membrane has several possible origins: the ER, the Golgi, the plasma membrane or the outer mitochondrial membrane.<!--c:autophagy-c024-->
3. Once the autophagosome merges with a lysosome, lysosomal enzymes break the cargo into simple building blocks that the cell recycles.<!--c:autophagy-c025-->

**Choosing the cargo.** Starvation-driven autophagy is largely non-selective.<!--c:autophagy-c026--> A damaged mitochondrion loses membrane potential, binds Nix and recruits Parkin and ubiquitin-like ATG proteins; the adaptor **p62** then tethers it to the inside of the phagophore.<!--c:autophagy-c027--> Misfolded proteins expose hydrophobic residues, receive polyubiquitin chains from E3 ligases, and form aggregates too large for the proteasome, which p62 also delivers to autophagosomes.<!--c:autophagy-c028-->

**Division of labor with the proteasome.** The proteasome mainly digests short-lived proteins; autophagy handles longer-lived proteins, aggregates, organelles and engulfed microbes; each can partly cover for the other.<!--c:autophagy-c029-->

**Crosstalk with apoptosis.**

- Beclin-1 carries a BH3 domain; in unstressed cells, Bcl-2, Mcl-1 and Bim bind it and keep it inactive.<!--c:autophagy-c030-->
- Under starvation, JNK1 phosphorylates Bcl-2 and Bim so that Beclin-1 is released; BH3 proteins such as Bad and Puma, and HMGB-1, can displace it too.<!--c:autophagy-c031-->
- Caspases cleave Beclin-1, and caspases and calpains cut Atg proteins and Ambra1, which tilts the cell toward apoptosis.<!--c:autophagy-c032-->
- The reverse also occurs: Atg5 dampens extrinsic apoptotic signaling, and autophagy can degrade caspases.<!--c:autophagy-c033-->
- p53 can restrain autophagy by inhibiting AMPK and can also promote mitophagy through PUMA.<!--c:autophagy-c034-->

**Role in atrophy.** In the atrophic cell, protein breakdown rises through more proteasomal degradation and more autophagic activity.<!--c:autophagy-c035-->

**Role in cell death.** Autophagy is counted with necrosis and apoptosis among the main routes to cell death, and like apoptosis it is an active, signal-driven process.<!--c:autophagy-c036--> In experiments, cells exposed to several lethal agents are spared when autophagy is blocked; even so, its power to kill by itself is unproven, and in cells already dying it may help them survive.<!--c:autophagy-c037--> In ischemia–reperfusion it is both helpful (ATP generation, clearing aggregates) and harmful (excessive removal of proteins and organelles, promotion of apoptosis).<!--c:autophagy-c038-->

## Microscopic morphology

- **Electron microscopy:** the autophagosome is a vacuole bounded by a double membrane that still holds recognizable cytoplasm, often whole organelles such as mitochondria or pieces of endoplasmic reticulum; after fusion with a lysosome it becomes an autolysosome, bounded by a single membrane, with contents at various stages of digestion.<!--c:autophagy-c068-->
- **Light microscopy:** lipofuscin, seen as golden-brown cytoplasmic granules, is made of peroxidized lipids and oxidized proteins that the cell's disposal systems, autophagy included, cannot break down.<!--c:autophagy-c069--> It builds up mainly in long-lived cells such as neurons and cardiac myocytes, and in hepatocytes; it increases with age and is more evident in atrophic organs (see [[pigment-accumulation]]).<!--c:autophagy-c070-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **No single test:** there is no gold-standard method for measuring autophagic activity, so several are combined; electron microscopy, the most traditional, identifies the vacuoles described under Microscopic morphology but does not show their lysosomal digestion directly.<!--c:autophagy-c071-->
- **LC3:** this mammalian homologue of yeast Atg8 stays on the autophagosome membrane and is a widely used marker; immunofluorescence or immunohistochemistry shows autophagosomes as cytoplasmic dots (puncta), and immunoblotting measures LC3-II, its lipid-conjugated, membrane-bound form.<!--c:autophagy-c072-->
- **p62 (SQSTM1):** it binds LC3 and is degraded by autophagy together with the cargo, so its level tends to fall when autophagy is active and to rise when autophagy is deficient.<!--c:autophagy-c073-->
- **Reading the result:** more autophagosomes, more LC3-II or stronger LC3 staining can mean either more autophagy or a block in lysosomal degradation; measuring autophagic flux tells them apart, for example by comparing LC3-II with and without a lysosomal inhibitor such as chloroquine or bafilomycin A1.<!--c:autophagy-c074-->
- **Molecular genetics:** genome-wide association studies have linked Crohn disease to variants of two autophagy genes, ATG16L1 and IRGM.<!--c:autophagy-c075-->

## Clinical presentation

Autophagy defects or excess contribute to many diseases:

- **Cancer:** Beclin-1 and some Atg genes are tumor suppressors, deleted or mutated in many human tumors, yet autophagy also protects tumor cells from hypoxia, nutrient lack and therapy.<!--c:autophagy-c039-->
- **Neurodegeneration:** in Huntington disease, mutations can hamper removal of aggregates and faulty mitochondria; in Alzheimer and Parkinson disease, an age-related drop in Beclin-1 production may help explain why aggregates accumulate faster than they are cleared.<!--c:autophagy-c040-->
- **Aging:** undegraded aggregates build up as macroautophagy and CMA wane with age; animal studies hint that mTOR inhibitors, which boost autophagy, may lengthen life.<!--c:autophagy-c041-->
- **Infection:** xenophagy eliminates intracellular parasites, viruses such as HIV-1, and bacteria such as *Shigella*, *Streptococcus* and *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*.<!--c:autophagy-c042--> Microbes fight back: some viruses make Bcl-2–like proteins that bind Beclin-1, and *M. tuberculosis* blocks phagosome–lysosome fusion.<!--c:autophagy-c043--> Poliovirus, hepatitis C virus and other RNA viruses turn the pathway to their advantage by building their replication sites on autophagic membranes.<!--c:autophagy-c044-->
- **Crohn disease:** variants in two autophagy genes that support xenophagy raise the risk; poor bacterial clearance increases pro-inflammatory output.<!--c:autophagy-c045-->
- **Lung:** autophagy is increased in some COPD, reduced in alveolar macrophages of chronic smokers, and impaired autophagic clearance of mutant protein aggregates in airway epithelial cells is implicated in cystic fibrosis.<!--c:autophagy-c046-->
- **Heart:** some inherited cardiomyopathies stem from mutated autophagy proteins; deficient autophagy also worsens ischemia–reperfusion injury and heart failure.<!--c:autophagy-c047-->

## Complications and prognosis

- Too little autophagy lets abnormal proteins and defective organelles pile up, which can end in cell death or disease.<!--c:autophagy-c048-->
- Too much autophagy is harmful as well, so the cell keeps it finely balanced.<!--c:autophagy-c049-->
- Its many disease links make autophagy an attractive drug target.<!--c:autophagy-c050-->

## High-yield summary

- Autophagy = **lysosomal self-digestion**, basal and stress-induced, essential for **homeostasis**.<!--c:autophagy-c055-->
- Forms: **macroautophagy** (bulk, autophagosome), **microautophagy** (lysosome invaginates), **CMA** (Hsc70 + **LAMP-2A**), **xenophagy** (microbes), **mitophagy**.<!--c:autophagy-c056-->
- **mTORC1** (fed, insulin/IGF-1 → Akt) blocks it; **AMPK** (high AMP/ATP) activates it via **ULK1** and **Beclin-1**/class III PI3K.<!--c:autophagy-c057-->
- **Phagophore → autophagosome → fusion with lysosome**; **p62** links ubiquitinated cargo to the phagophore.<!--c:autophagy-c058-->
- **Bcl-2** holds Beclin-1; **caspases** cleave it, linking autophagy to apoptosis.<!--c:autophagy-c059-->
- Disease links: **cancer** (double-edged), **neurodegeneration**, **aging**, **infection**, **Crohn disease**, **cardiomyopathy**.<!--c:autophagy-c060-->
- How it is studied: **electron microscopy** shows the **double-membrane autophagosome**; **LC3** marks autophagosomes; **p62** builds up when autophagy fails; counting autophagosomes does not measure **flux**.<!--c:autophagy-c077-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- **Not only a starvation response:** autophagy runs continuously as quality control, for instance against ROS from damaged mitochondria.<!--c:autophagy-c061-->
- **Not always anti-cancer:** it suppresses tumor initiation but shields established tumors from chemotherapy.<!--c:autophagy-c062-->
- **"Autophagic cell death" is not proven as an independent killer;** its contribution apart from other forms of programmed death is unclear.<!--c:autophagy-c063-->
- **Not always random:** starvation autophagy is largely bulk, but mitophagy, aggregate removal and CMA are highly selective.<!--c:autophagy-c064-->
- **The proteasome cannot replace it:** aggregates and whole organelles are too large for the proteasome.<!--c:autophagy-c065-->
- **More autophagic vacuoles do not mean more autophagy:** in some neurodegenerative and degenerative muscle diseases, a build-up of early autophagic structures was first read as excess autophagy, but it more likely reflects a block in the later steps of the pathway.<!--c:autophagy-c076-->

## Mnemonics

**"Big bites, small sips, escorted guests, foreign invaders"** (original), the main forms: macroautophagy takes big bites of cytoplasm, microautophagy takes small sips as the lysosome dimples inward, CMA brings escorted guests through LAMP-2A, and xenophagy targets foreign invaders.<!--c:autophagy-c066-->

**"Feast locks, famine unlocks"** (original): in a feast, mTORC1 locks ULK1; in famine, AMPK unlocks it and autophagy starts.<!--c:autophagy-c067-->
