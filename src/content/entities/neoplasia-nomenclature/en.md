## Definition and epidemiology

**What is a neoplasm, and how does its name work?** A neoplasm is a self-driven growth that ignores the usual brakes on cell number.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c001--> The word tumor is in common use for neoplasms because most of them form a space-occupying mass.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c002--> Spread to distant sites makes a tumor malignant, also called cancer; a tumor that stays local is benign.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c003--> The first thing a tumor name tells you is the cell it came from.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c004-->

Today the ending "-oma" cannot be trusted to predict behavior, although it once meant benign.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c005--> Despite their endings, melanoma and seminoma are malignant.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c006--> Carcinoma is a malignant tumor of epithelial cells.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c007--> Sarcoma is a malignant tumor of mesenchymal origin.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c008--> Leukemia is the usual name when the malignant growth arises in blood-forming tissue.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c009--> Lymphomas are the malignancies of lymphoid cells.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c010--> Hodgkin disease and Ewing sarcoma carry a person's name because the cell they came from stayed uncertain for a long time.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c011--> A hamartoma is not a true neoplasm but a haphazard developmental mix of structures.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c012--> Neoplasms become more frequent as people grow older.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**What does a neoplasm change in a cell's growth and identity?** Neoplastic cells keep dividing, and how far they mature is distorted to varying extents.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c014--> Mature neurons and cardiac myocytes cannot form tumors, because only cells that are able to divide can become neoplastic.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c015-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Which microscopic features separate benign from malignant?**
- Well-differentiated tumors look much like their tissue of origin, and benign tumors usually belong here.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c016-->
- The origin of some tumors is uncertain because their cells look very primitive.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c017-->
- Aggressive tumors are generally those whose cells look most abnormal, a feature called atypia or anaplasia.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c018-->
- Abnormal mitotic figures, and pleomorphism (cells and nuclei that vary in size and shape), are signs of anaplasia.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c019-->
- Cartilage shows the contrast: chondrosarcoma cells are misshapen, while a chondroma is almost indistinguishable from normal cartilage.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c020-->
- Malignant tumors often contain many mitotic figures.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c021-->
- A few mitoses already establish malignancy in some tumors, leiomyosarcoma for one.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c022-->
- Sheets of cells are one example of the disorganized growth that malignant tumors often show.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c023-->
- Tumor cells inside blood or lymph vessels suggest malignancy.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c024-->
- Local invasion carries weight in diagnosing follicular thyroid cancer.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c025-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** on routine H&E, look at invasion and growth pattern, then at mitoses, atypia and differentiation.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c026-->
- To establish a tumor's lineage, the lab can use immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c027-->
- Cytokeratin staining can identify a poorly differentiated tumor as a carcinoma.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c028-->
- S-100 staining supports melanocytic origin in an unpigmented metastasis.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c029-->
- Molecular studies, such as sequencing, also help to establish the origin.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c030-->
- Electron microscopy can still reveal specialized structures that tell look-alike tumors apart, even though marker studies now do most of this work.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c031-->
- Gene products made by a neoplasm serve as tumor markers; they can be found in body fluids or on or inside the tumor cells.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c032-->
- Treatment choices often rest on knowing the lineage.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c033-->

## Clinical presentation

**Can a benign tumor be dangerous?** Yes, if it sits in a critical location.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c034--> A benign meningioma can kill by compressing an important part of the brain.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c035--> Sudden death can result when a benign myxoma in the left atrium obstructs the mitral valve opening.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c036--> Slow growth can make some malignant tumors harmless to life; thyroid and prostate tumors are the examples.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c037-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is a tumor labeled benign or malignant?** The label is a prediction of the tumor's future behavior.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c038--> No scientific principle sets these criteria; they rest on experience of how each tumor appearance has behaved clinically.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c039--> Most cases are straightforward, though some need extra study.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c040--> For a few tumors even an expert cannot decide from the slide, and only the clinical course settles the diagnosis.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c041--> A tumor that metastasizes is, by that fact, malignant.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c042--> A metastasis found before any known primary can be traced to its tissue of origin with tissue-linked markers.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c043--> After treatment of the primary tumor, blood tests for PSA or CA 125 can be used to monitor whether the disease advances or spreads.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c044--> Such markers have worked less well for screening populations.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c045--> Estrogen and progesterone receptors guide treatment in breast cancer.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c046-->

## Complications and prognosis

**Does the microscopic look always predict behavior?** No: appearance and behavior do not always go together.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c047--> Skin basal cell carcinoma looks malignant yet rarely spreads and is not lethal, though it burrows locally.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c048--> Endocrine tumors, islet-cell tumors among them, often cannot be classed benign or malignant from the slide; only metastasis, or its absence, decides.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c049--> Above all, a benign tumor is one that stays put: no invasion of nearby tissue, no metastasis.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c050-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Benign tumor | Does not invade or metastasize, and is usually well differentiated.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c051--> | Histology and follow-up |
| Malignant tumor | Grows into neighboring tissue and spreads to distant sites.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c052--> | Invasion, metastasis, atypia |
| Hamartoma | Not a true neoplasm: a haphazard developmental mix of structures.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c053--> | Histology |
| Nodular fasciitis (reactive) | A reactive lesion that can be mistaken for fibrosarcoma because its fibroblasts look atypical.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c054--> | Histology |
| Well-differentiated endocrine carcinoma | Its microscopic picture can match that of a benign adenoma.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c055--> | Metastasis on follow-up |

## High-yield summary

- The **cell of origin** is the primary descriptor of a tumor.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c056-->
- The ending **-oma** does not guarantee benignity: melanoma and seminoma are malignant.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c057-->
- **Carcinoma** is epithelial and **sarcoma** is mesenchymal.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c058-->
- A **benign** tumor does not invade or metastasize.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c059-->
- A **hamartoma** is not a true neoplasm.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c060-->
- **Tumor markers** (immunohistochemistry) help identify the origin of undifferentiated tumors.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c061-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does "-oma" always mean benign?** No. Melanoma and seminoma are malignant despite the ending.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c062-->

**Is a hamartoma a tumor in the true sense?** No. It is not a true neoplasm, only a haphazard developmental mix of structures.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c063-->

**Can a benign tumor kill?** Yes. A meningioma that compresses an important brain structure can kill.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c064-->

**Does a malignant-looking tumor always spread?** No. Basal cell carcinoma of the skin rarely metastasizes.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c065-->

**Is a reactive lesion always bland?** No. Nodular fasciitis is reactive, yet misdiagnosing it as fibrosarcoma can lead to unneeded surgery.<!--c:neoplasia-nomenclature-c066-->
