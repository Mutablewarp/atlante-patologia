## Definition and epidemiology

**What is ulcerative colitis?** It is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease of the rectum and a variable length of colon, with inflammation largely in the mucosa.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c001--> Untreated, the lesion is continuous and climbs from the rectum with no skipped segments.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c002--> Pancolitis involves the whole colon; proctitis stays in the rectum; left-sided colitis (proctosigmoiditis) reaches the splenic flexure.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c003--> It is more frequent in developed countries, and its incidence is climbing as populations take on a Western way of life.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c004--> Onset peaks at 15 to 30 years and again at 50 to 70 years.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What raises or lowers the risk?** Rising rates where Western habits spread hint at an environmental role.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c006--> Former smokers carry a raised risk, while current smoking seems protective.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c007--> Relatives with inflammatory bowel disease raise the risk, but less than in Crohn disease.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c008--> *C. difficile* or cytomegalovirus infection can provoke or aggravate an attack.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c009-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does the mucosa get damaged?** The reference gives no immune mechanism. Crypt abscesses form when neutrophils enter crypts and the lining epithelium dies with pus formation.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c010--> Merged crypt abscesses hollow out the mucosa, so ulcers form with patches of intact mucosa nearby.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c011--> The disease can reach these morphologic changes quickly or over several years.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c012-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the colon look like?** Early on, the denuded mucosa is red and grainy, bleeds at a touch, and often wears a yellowish exudate.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c013--> Shallow ulcers can grow as small erosions run together, leaving patches of intact mucosa among them.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c014--> The muscularis and serosa are not usually involved, so the strictures and fistulas of Crohn disease do not occur.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c015--> Old disease leaves a smooth or granular mucosa without folds, and the bowel may shorten, mostly on the left.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c016--> A spared rectum is possible, but Crohn disease should come to mind.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c017--> Only mild backwash ileitis occasionally escapes into the ileum; the ileocecal valve is the usual limit, even when the cecum is inflamed.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c018-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What do the crypts and the lamina propria show?** Early on, pinpoint bleeds dot a swollen, congested mucosa.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c019--> Lymphocytes and plasma cells stand out in the mixed lamina propria infiltrate.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c020--> Neutrophils often gather around damaged crypts and invade them (cryptitis).<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c021--> A crypt abscess is a dilated crypt filled with neutrophils.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c022--> Mucosal islands left beside the ulcers are called inflammatory pseudopolyps.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c023--> Late on, the mucosa thins and shows Paneth cell metaplasia, while crypts shorten and become twisted or forked.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c024--> Distorted crypts often remain after remission, a trace of earlier chronic damage.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c025--> Inflammation stays in the mucosa, except in rare fulminant cases.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c026--> Crypt abscesses are typical of Crohn disease too, so they do not separate the two.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c027-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**

- **How it is studied:** resected colon and biopsies are examined; early microscopic changes match what the endoscopist sees.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c028-->
- On histology, look for cryptitis, crypt abscesses, crypt distortion and a mucosal infiltrate.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c029-->
- Dysplastic cells do not mature normally, and their nuclei are atypical and crowd the cell.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c030-->
- Dysplasia is graded low or high.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c031-->
- Biopsies are read as "indefinite for dysplasia" when heavy inflammation prevents a firm answer.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c032-->
- Chromoendoscopy (topical dye) or confocal laser endomicroscopy may find more dysplastic lesions on biopsy.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c033-->

The reference gives no special stain, IHC or molecular test.

## Clinical presentation

**What are the symptoms and the course?** The usual pattern is relapsing: attacks alternate with complete or partial remissions.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c034--> Half of patients have mild disease of the rectum and lower sigmoid; they mainly pass blood, and some have tenesmus.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c035--> Moderate disease (about 40%, often left-sided) brings bouts of bloody diarrhea with pain and low fever, and chronic blood loss often causes anemia.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c036--> Severe disease, in about 10% and usually pancolitis, drains blood, fluid and electrolytes through frequent bloody stools, often with fever.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c037--> In toxic megacolon the colon dilates massively, and perforation becomes a major danger.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c038--> Sometimes colectomy cannot wait, since fulminant colitis is an emergency needing intensive treatment at once.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c039-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it recognized and separated from Crohn disease?** It depends on anatomic location and histology.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c042--> Treatment, drugs and surgery alike, differs between them, and the cancer risk is higher in ulcerative colitis.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c043--> *C. difficile* and cytomegalovirus should be looked for and treated if found.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c045--> Indeterminate colitis is the label for the roughly 10% of cases in which the two diseases cannot be told apart, mostly fulminant ones.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c046--> The reference gives no laboratory or imaging criteria.

## Complications and prognosis

**What can go wrong?** Bleeding, perforation and toxic megacolon can occur, as can pseudopolyps and carcinoma.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c047--> Long-standing disease makes colorectal cancer more likely than in the general population.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c049--> More extensive and longer disease raises the risk, which is elevated beyond 10 years.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c050--> Inflammation restricted to the rectum leaves the risk near the general level.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c051--> Primary sclerosing cholangitis makes dysplasia and colorectal cancer more likely.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c052--> Dysplasia precedes carcinoma and may be flat or appear as a polypoid lesion.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c053--> Colectomy is strongly favored when a biopsy shows high-grade dysplasia, given the high cancer risk.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c054--> To catch dysplasia, most patients are scanned by regular colonoscopy with biopsies once the disease has set in.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c055--> Anti-inflammatory therapy and endoscopic monitoring with biopsies can reduce dysplasia and cancer risk.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c056-->

## High-yield summary

- **Continuous mucosal disease** starts in the **rectum** and spreads upward without skip lesions.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c064-->
- **Crypt abscesses** and **architectural distortion** are the microscopic hallmarks.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c065-->
- Strictures, fistulas and granulomas point to Crohn disease, not to ulcerative colitis.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c066-->
- **Inflammatory pseudopolyps** are islands of regenerating mucosa among ulcers.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c067-->
- **Toxic megacolon** is a massively dilated colon with a major risk of **perforation**.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c068-->
- Cancer risk grows with extent and duration, so patients need **surveillance colonoscopy** with biopsies for **dysplasia**.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c069-->
- **High-grade dysplasia** on biopsy points strongly toward colectomy.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c070-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is a spared rectum typical of ulcerative colitis?** No. The rectum is usually involved; sparing suggests Crohn disease.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c071-->

**Do crypt abscesses prove ulcerative colitis?** No. They are typical of both ulcerative colitis and Crohn disease.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c072-->

**Does ulcerative colitis involve the small bowel?** No. It ends at the ileocecal valve, apart from mild backwash ileitis.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c073-->

**Is everyone with ulcerative colitis at the same cancer risk?** No. Disease limited to the rectum carries a risk similar to the general population.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c074-->

## Mnemonics

Remember **R-C-M**: **R**ectum first, **C**ontinuous spread, **M**ucosa only.<!--c:ulcerative-colitis-c075-->
