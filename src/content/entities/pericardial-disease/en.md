## Definition and epidemiology

**What counts as pericardial disease?** It covers fluid collecting in the pericardial sac and inflammation of the pericardium.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c001--> A pericardial effusion is extra fluid in the sac; it can be a transudate or an exudate.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c002--> Cardiac tamponade is the dangerous case: fluid builds up so quickly that the heart fills poorly.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c003--> Pericarditis is inflammation of the pericardium, whether it affects the layer on the heart or the one lining the sac.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c004--> In constrictive pericarditis, scar tissue stiffens the pericardium, so the heart is compressed and fills poorly.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What fills the sac, and what causes pericarditis?**

- Heart failure and nephrotic syndrome raise extracellular fluid volume, and a serous effusion often goes with that rise.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c006-->
- Chylous effusion, rich in chylomicrons, follows tumor or infection that blocks lymphatics so that the thoracic duct opens into the sac.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c007-->
- Chest injury, including injury during resuscitation, may lead to a blood-tinged (serosanguineous) effusion.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c008-->
- Hemopericardium, blood poured directly into the sac, is most often caused by a ruptured ventricular free wall in an infarct.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c009--> Hemopericardium also follows penetrating wounds of the heart and rupture of an aortic dissection.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c010-->
- Undiagnosed viral infection is the usual explanation for most acute pericarditis, which is therefore labeled idiopathic.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c011--> Myocardial infarction and rheumatic fever can both be complicated by pericarditis.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c012--> Antibiotics have made bacterial pericarditis unusual.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c013--> Dialysis has made uremic fibrinous pericarditis rare in the United States.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c014--> Malignant pericardial effusions come most often from metastases of breast or lung cancer.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c015-->
- Cardiac surgery and earlier mediastinal radiation are known causes, but in developed countries most constrictive pericarditis has no identified cause.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c016--> A purulent or tuberculous infection precedes some cases.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c017--> Outside industrialized countries tuberculosis is still the main cause of constrictive pericarditis, whereas within them it is a minor one.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c018-->

## Pathogenesis

**Why does fluid cause tamponade, and why does constriction develop?** Slowly collecting fluid, even a large volume, barely affects circulation.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c019--> As little as 150 to 200 mL of fluid or blood collecting fast raises sac pressure and hinders filling, mainly of the right heart.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c020--> Cardiac output and blood pressure drop because venous return is limited once the pressure around the heart exceeds central venous pressure.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c021--> After an acute pericardial injury, healing can overshoot; constrictive pericarditis is that scar, not active inflammation.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c022--> Healing can be gentle: in adhesive pericarditis only a few fibrous strands tie the two layers together.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c023-->

## Gross morphology

**What do the inflamed or scarred pericardial surfaces look like?**

- Most acute cases are fibrinous: a layer of fibrin leaves the pericardium dull and rough instead of shiny.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c024-->
- Purulent pericarditis produces an exudate that looks like pus.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c025-->
- Hemorrhagic pericarditis is the bloody form, seen with coagulation disorders and with aggressive infections or tumors.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c026-->
- Fresh blood distends the sac in hemopericardium.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c027-->
- Constrictive pericarditis leaves no pericardial space: hard scar tissue binds both layers into a shell around the heart.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c028--> A scar thick enough can narrow the openings of the venae cavae.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c029--> Calcium may be deposited in the scar.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c030-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Which cells and tissue are seen?**

- In fibrinous pericarditis the pericardial infiltrate is mostly mononuclear cells.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c031-->
- Neutrophils fill the exudate of purulent pericarditis.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c032-->
- The fluid of a serous effusion holds little protein and few cells.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c033-->
- The constrictive pericardium is dense fibrous tissue rather than an active inflammatory infiltrate.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c034-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**

- **How it is studied:** surfaces and fluid, seen by eye and under the microscope, decide whether acute pericarditis is fibrinous, purulent or hemorrhagic.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c035-->
- Fluid in fibrinous pericarditis is usually rich in protein, while serous effusion fluid has little.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c036-->

## Clinical presentation

**How do the main forms present?**

- Chest pain behind the sternum, severe and of sudden onset, typically opens acute pericarditis; neck, shoulder or back may hurt too.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c037-->
- The left arm is spared: pericarditis pain, unlike that of angina or infarction, does not travel down it.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c038-->
- The friction rub heard over the heart in pericarditis comes from inflamed pericardial surfaces that have turned rough.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c039-->
- On the ECG, repolarization abnormalities are seen.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c040-->
- Tamponade may cause hardly any symptoms or may end in sudden circulatory collapse and death.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c041--> Nearly all patients with tamponade show pulsus paradoxus: systolic pressure falls more than normal while breathing in.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c042-->
- Constrictive pericarditis leaves a small, quiet heart: the unyielding sac does not let it fill during diastole.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c043--> Constrictive pericarditis brings high venous pressure, with ascites and swelling of the limbs.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c044-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What points to the diagnosis?** A friction rub, severe chest pain and repolarization changes on the ECG are the features of acute pericarditis.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c045--> Pain that does not radiate down the left arm helps separate it from infarction.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c046--> Constrictive pericarditis resembles right heart failure.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c047--> Tamponade is relieved by needle pericardiocentesis, which removes the pericardial fluid.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c048-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How does it end?**

- Unless the fluid is drained, by a needle (pericardiocentesis) or by surgery, acute tamponade is nearly always fatal.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c049-->
- Viral or idiopathic pericarditis resolves spontaneously; constriction seldom follows.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c050-->
- Constrictive pericarditis is best treated by surgically removing the whole pericardium.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c051-->
- Adhesive pericarditis is commonly found incidentally at autopsy.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c052-->

## High-yield summary

- **Cardiac tamponade** is fluid building up so fast around the heart that the heart cannot fill properly.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c057-->
- **Pulsus paradoxus** is present in almost all patients with tamponade.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c058-->
- **Fibrinous pericarditis** is the most common acute form and causes a **friction rub**.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c059-->
- Most acute pericarditis is **idiopathic**, attributed to **viral** infection.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c060-->
- **Constrictive pericarditis** is a healed fibrotic scar, not active inflammation.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c061-->
- The usual cause of **hemopericardium** is rupture of the ventricular free wall at an infarct.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c062-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a large effusion always cause tamponade?** No. Speed matters: slow build-up is tolerated even when large; a small, fast one can impede filling.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c063-->

**Is constrictive pericarditis an active inflammation?** No. It is the fibrous outcome of healing after an acute injury.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c064-->

**Does pericarditis pain radiate down the left arm like infarct pain?** No. It does not.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c065-->

**Is bacterial infection the usual cause of acute pericarditis?** No. Most cases are idiopathic and attributed to viruses, and bacterial pericarditis is unusual.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c066-->

**Is trauma the most common cause of hemopericardium?** No. Rupture of the ventricular free wall at an infarct is the most common cause.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c067-->

## Mnemonics

**How can I recall the three acute forms?** Think **FPH**: Fibrin (the most common, with a rub), Pus (neutrophils) and Hemorrhage.<!--c:pericardial-disease-c068-->
