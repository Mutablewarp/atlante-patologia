## Definition and epidemiology

**What is malabsorption?** In many diseases the gut takes up too little of the nutrients the body needs.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c001--> Absorption that matters clinically happens mainly in the proximal small intestine.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c002--> Nutrients are first prepared in the lumen (luminal phase) and then moved through mucosal cells (intestinal phase).<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c003--> One faulty component of either phase can impair it.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Where can absorption fail?**
- **Luminal:** pancreatic failure or blocked juice flow impairs fat digestion, which needs enzymes and bile acids.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c005-->
- **Mucosal, enzymes:** epithelial cells take up only monosaccharides, so missing brush-border disaccharidases leave sugars unabsorbed.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c006-->
- **Mucosal, surface:** short gut, or a gastrocolic fistula that lets food skip the small bowel, leaves less absorbing surface.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c007-->
- **Mucosal, damage:** small bowel diseases such as celiac disease injure the mucosa and cut absorption.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c008-->
- **Mucosal, metabolism:** in abetalipoproteinemia, missing apoproteins prevent chylomicron assembly.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c009-->
- **Post-mucosal, transport:** in Whipple disease, nutrients probably fail to move on through capillaries and lymphatics.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c010-->

**Which diseases must I know?**
- **Lactase deficiency:** brush-border lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose before absorption.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c011--> The acquired form is common.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c012--> Unrecognized congenital deficiency can kill, though it is uncommon.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c013-->
- **Celiac disease:** gluten from wheat, rye or barley triggers it, only in genetically susceptible people.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c014--> Nearly all patients carry HLA-DQ2 or DQ8.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c015--> Lacking them, a patient almost certainly does not have it.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c016-->
- **Tropical sprue:** cases come from South and Southeast Asia and from Central America and the Caribbean.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c017--> The agent is unknown, but relief with antibiotics supports a bacterial cause.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c018-->
- **Whipple disease:** the cause is the actinobacterium *Tropheryma whippelii*.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c019-->
- **Bacterial overgrowth:** slow gut motility, as in diabetes or scleroderma, lets bacteria overgrow.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c020--> Stasis from an anatomic defect (Billroth II operation, small bowel diverticula) is called blind loop syndrome.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c021-->

## Pathogenesis

**How do these diseases impair absorption?**
- **Celiac disease:** T cells are activated when HLA-DQ2 or DQ8 molecules show them gliadin that has been changed.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c022--> Cytokines from the activated mucosal T cells injure the mucosa.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c023-->
- **Whipple disease:** macrophages cannot degrade the bacteria, which build up inside them.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c024--> Transport suffers, probably because crowded macrophages squeeze villous lymphatics.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c025-->
- **Autoimmune enteropathy:** apoptotic epithelial cells point to an immune attack.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c026-->
- **Bacterial overgrowth:** bacteria that consume micronutrients or deconjugate bile salts are thought to be the main driver.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c027-->

## Gross morphology

Not applicable.

## Microscopic morphology

**What does a small-bowel biopsy show?**
- **Celiac disease:** villi are variably distorted, and inflammation, IELs (intraepithelial lymphocytes) included, increases.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c028--> Severe disease flattens the mucosa: the villi vanish while hyperplastic crypts remain.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c029--> Villi shrink and crypts deepen because lost epithelium is replaced by faster crypt division.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c030--> A raised IEL count is nearly universal.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c031--> NSAID exposure and *H. pylori* gastritis can also raise IELs, so an increase alone is not specific.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c032-->
- **Tropical sprue:** it often affects the full length of the small intestine, down to the ileum.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c033--> Unlike celiac disease, the mucosa is less often flat, and IELs favor crypts over villus tips.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c034-->
- **Autoimmune enteropathy:** IELs are less prominent than in celiac disease, though villous atrophy may be severe.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c035-->
- **Whipple disease:** macrophages with foamy cytoplasm crowd the villi, which become swollen and bulbous.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c036--> They stain strongly with PAS, since their bacteria hold polysaccharide.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c037-->
- **Bacterial overgrowth:** mucosa can look normal; any inflammation is spotty, with no telltale pattern.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c038-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**

**How it is studied:**
- **H&E** on small-bowel mucosa: assess villous height, crypt depth and IEL number.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c039-->
- **Molecular:** PCR detection of the bacterium is increasingly used to confirm Whipple disease, where PAS staining used to be the standard.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c040-->
- **Serology:** tissue transglutaminase antibody is the test of choice for celiac disease.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c041-->
- **Serology:** anti-enterocyte antibodies characterize autoimmune enteropathy.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c042-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does malabsorption present?**
- Specific malabsorption involves one nutrient only: lactose when lactase is lacking, or B12 when autoimmune stomach damage leaves no intrinsic factor.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c043-->
- When many nutrients are poorly absorbed, malnutrition follows: adults lose weight, children fail to thrive.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c044-->
- Anemia can result from lack of vitamin B12, folate or iron.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c045-->
- Osteomalacia in adults, rickets in children and tetany can follow poor calcium and vitamin D absorption.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c046-->
- Steatorrhea, fat in the stool, results from fat malabsorption.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c047-->
- **Lactase deficiency:** dairy brings on diarrhea, flatulence and distention, usually from adolescence.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c048-->
- **Celiac disease:** typical complaints are diarrhea and discomfort in the abdomen.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c049--> In late disease, fat in the stool, swelling from low albumin, brittle bones and stunted growth appear.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c050--> Anemia from iron deficiency that does not improve with oral iron can be the only clue to celiac disease, which may otherwise cause no symptoms.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c051-->
- **Tropical sprue:** megaloblastic change can follow the severe folate and vitamin B12 deficiency it may cause.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c052-->
- **Autoimmune enteropathy:** heavy diarrhea starts it; the disease can also reach organs beyond the small bowel.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c053-->
- **Whipple disease:** neuropsychiatric symptoms can be the first sign, because the CNS is involved.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c054-->
- **Bacterial overgrowth:** bloating and diarrhea.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c055-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which tests help find the cause?**
- Quantitative fecal fat is the benchmark test; other malabsorption tests are compared with it.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c056-->
- Blood and urine xylose after a test dose assess the intestinal phase, because xylose needs no luminal-phase step.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c057-->
- The 14C-cholylglycine breath test (ileal bile salt absorption) diagnoses blind loop syndrome.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c058-->
- Breath testing for hydrogen and methane diagnoses bacterial overgrowth.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c059-->
- No single test settles celiac disease; symptoms, serology and biopsy are weighed together.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c060-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How do these conditions respond to treatment, and what can go wrong?**
- Gluten is ubiquitous, so the diet that usually works in celiac disease is hard to keep.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c061-->
- Long-standing celiac disease makes small bowel adenocarcinoma and enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma more likely.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c062-->
- Autoimmune enteropathy needs immunosuppressants; gluten restriction does nothing.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c063-->
- Antibiotics produce a dramatic response in Whipple disease.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c064-->
- Antibiotics usually improve bacterial overgrowth.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c065-->

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |

| [[celiac-disease]] | Villous atrophy with crypt hyperplasia and many IELs, mainly proximal.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c066--> | Tissue transglutaminase antibody; biopsy |
| Tropical sprue | Geographic restriction; changes extend to the ileum, and IELs favor the crypts.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c067--> | Travel history; response to antibiotics |
| Autoimmune enteropathy | Loss of goblet and Paneth cells, anti-enterocyte antibodies, no response to gluten restriction.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c068--> | Anti-enterocyte antibodies |
| Whipple disease | Foamy macrophages in bulbous villi, multiorgan disease.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c069--> | PAS stain; PCR |
| Bacterial overgrowth | Normal or patchily inflamed mucosa, with stasis or dysmotility.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c070--> | Hydrogen and methane breath test |

## High-yield summary

- Absorption fails in the **luminal** or the **intestinal** phase.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c071-->
- **Quantitative fecal fat** is the best test; fat malabsorption gives **steatorrhea**.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c072-->
- **Whipple disease:** **PAS-positive** foamy macrophages, *Tropheryma whippelii*, antibiotics.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c073-->
- **Celiac disease:** **tTG antibody**, **HLA-DQ2/DQ8**, villous atrophy with **increased IELs**.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c074-->
- **Autoimmune enteropathy:** **anti-enterocyte antibodies**, **immunosuppression**, not gluten restriction.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c075-->
- **Bacterial overgrowth:** **breath test**, **blind loop** stasis, antibiotics.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c076-->
- **Tropical sprue:** **geographic**, can involve the **ileum**, **folate and B12** deficiency.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c077-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is a positive serology enough to diagnose celiac disease?** No. Symptoms and histology must back it.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c078-->

**Do increased IELs prove celiac disease?** No. NSAIDs and *H. pylori* gastritis can raise them too.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c079-->

**Does a gluten-free diet fix every enteropathy with villous atrophy?** No. Autoimmune enteropathy needs immunosuppressants.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c080-->

**Is malabsorption always a disease of the small bowel mucosa?** No. Lack of intrinsic factor in autoimmune gastritis causes vitamin B12 malabsorption.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c081-->

**Is "sprue" the same as celiac disease?** Not quite. Sprue just means diarrhea in Dutch; celiac disease is sometimes called nontropical sprue.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c082-->

## Mnemonics

**LMT** (original): **L**umen digests, **M**ucosa absorbs, **T**ransport carries away.<!--c:malabsorption-syndromes-c083-->
