## Definition and epidemiology

**What is familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)?** It is an inherited syndrome, transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait, in which the lining of the colon and rectum is studded with adenomas numbering in the hundreds to thousands.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c001--> FAP is rare: fewer than 1% of colorectal cancers are due to it.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c002-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which gene is mutated, and who carries the mutation?** *APC* sits at 5q21-22 on the long arm of chromosome 5; FAP arises when a germline mutation disables it.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c003--> New mutations account for 30% to 50% of patients.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c004--> About a quarter of familial cases have no *APC* mutation.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c005--> Two mutated *MYH* copies cause a recessive polyposis that is rare and can look like FAP clinically.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c006-->

## Pathogenesis

**What does the APC protein normally do?** *APC* is a tumor suppressor gene.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c007--> Through Wnt/β-catenin signaling, it helps set how fast the cells of the colonic mucosa multiply.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c008-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the colon look like?** The rectosigmoid is the most densely covered region, but the carpet of adenomas, hundreds to thousands in number, sometimes extends through the whole colorectal mucosa.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c009--> Several of the many polyps in the specimen pictured in the reference measure more than 1 cm across.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c010--> In some patients, adenomas occur outside the colon too, in the stomach and small intestine.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c011-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What are the polyps made of?** Tubular adenomas predominate; a minority are tubulovillous or villous.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c012--> Under the microscope you also find many tiny adenomas, a few of them confined to one crypt.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c013--> For the general adenoma, see [[colorectal-adenoma]].

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** the reference page gives no sampling protocol, stain or immunohistochemistry; it supports only the histologic and molecular findings in this card.
- **Molecular:** the key finding is a germline *APC* mutation, which most familial cases carry but about one in four lacks.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c014-->
- **Molecular:** *MYH* is a DNA repair gene; some *APC*-negative patients have both copies mutated.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c015-->

## Clinical presentation

**When do polyps and symptoms start, and what are the variants?** By age 10, patients usually already have a few polyps.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c016--> Patients typically become symptomatic by age 36.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c017-->
- **Gardner syndrome** adds osteomas, for example of the skull and jaw.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c018--> It also brings desmoid tumors and epidermoid cysts.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c019--> Congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium can occur too.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c020-->
- **Attenuated FAP** has fewer than 100 colonic adenomas.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c021--> Compared with classic FAP, its cancers arise about 15 years later, on average.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c022--> By age 80, the risk that invasive cancer has developed is 70%.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c023-->
- **Turcot syndrome** is rare and pairs FAP with malignant tumors of the central nervous system.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c025--> Germline *APC* mutations underlie many cases, particularly those with medulloblastoma.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c026-->

Which *APC* mutation a patient has does not predict the Gardner phenotype.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c024-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is FAP recognized?** The reference page gives no diagnostic criteria. The picture rests on the polyp burden described under gross morphology and on the molecular findings above.

## Complications and prognosis

**What is the outlook?** Every FAP patient will develop colorectal carcinoma.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c027--> Cancers arise at a mean age of 40 years.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c028--> The curative treatment is a total colectomy, provided it is done before cancer begins.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c029--> A colonic adenoma and a gastric or small-intestinal one are equally capable of turning malignant.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c030-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What else looks like FAP?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Attenuated FAP (see above) | Fewer than 100 colonic adenomas.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c031--> | Adenoma count |
| MYH-associated polyposis | Recessive inheritance, with both *MYH* alleles mutated, and a clinical picture that overlaps FAP.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c032--> | *MYH* mutation analysis |
| [[colorectal-adenocarcinoma]] | Most cases develop from a preexisting adenoma.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c033--> | Histology of the lesion |

## High-yield summary

- **APC** on 5q21-22 is a tumor suppressor gene of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c034-->
- Mostly tubular adenomas, **hundreds to thousands** of them, cover the colon, thickest in the rectosigmoid.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c035-->
- Without surgery cancer is **inevitable**; **total colectomy** done early is curative.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c036-->
- **Attenuated FAP**: fewer than 100 adenomas, cancer about 15 years later.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c037-->
- **Gardner syndrome**: extracolonic osteomas, desmoid tumors and epidermoid cysts.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c038-->
- **Turcot syndrome**: FAP plus malignant CNS tumors.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c039-->
- **MYH** polyposis is the recessive look-alike.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c040-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is FAP a common cause of colorectal cancer?** No. It accounts for fewer than 1% of cases.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c041-->

**Is FAP always inherited from a parent?** No. A sizeable share of patients, 30% to 50%, have a new mutation.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c042-->

**Does a given APC mutation tell you the patient has Gardner syndrome?** No. APC mutations do not predict that phenotype.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c043-->

**Does a negative APC test rule out polyposis?** No. Two mutated copies of MYH cause a distinct polyposis even when no APC mutation is found.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c044-->

**Are the adenomas confined to the colon?** No. The small intestine and stomach can also hold adenomas.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c045-->

## Mnemonics

**APC** = **A**denomas by the hundreds, **P**rophylactic colectomy, **C**ancer by about age 40 if untreated.<!--c:familial-adenomatous-polyposis-c046-->
