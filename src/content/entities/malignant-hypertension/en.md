## Definition and epidemiology

**What is malignant hypertension?** Malignant hypertension is a sudden, extreme pressure surge that damages small vessels and organs.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c001--> The pressure reaches 180 mm Hg or more systolic and 120 mm Hg or more diastolic.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c002--> Among hypertensive patients, it is rare.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c003--> Patients of African descent are affected more often than others.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What triggers it?** A cerebrovascular accident, or stopping antihypertensive treatment, can set it off.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c005--> Overactivity of the autonomic nervous system caused by cocaine or amphetamines can produce it.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c006--> Glomerulonephritis, autoimmune disease, and the pregnancy disorders preeclampsia and eclampsia can also cause it.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c007--> Chronic kidney injury from a range of diseases underlies many cases, and about half of patients had benign hypertension first.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c008--> In some cases it develops in people who seem healthy, with no prior disease.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c009-->

## Pathogenesis

**How are the vessels injured?** Exactly how the vessels are injured remains uncertain.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c010--> Endothelial injury in narrowed small vessels is proposed to result from the combination of extreme pressure and microvascular vasoconstriction.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c011--> Segments of small muscular arteries widen after their smooth muscle cells die.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c012--> Plasma proteins escape into the wall of these segments, because the endothelium has lost its integrity.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c013--> Fibrinoid necrosis is the result when fibrin is laid down in the wall at these sites.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c014--> The onion-skin look comes from smooth muscle proliferation that adds concentric layers of cells soon after the injury.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c015--> Platelets and other cells at the injury site may release growth factors that stimulate this proliferation.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c016--> In the kidney, thrombi forming in damaged vessels can infarct patches of cortex.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c017-->

## Gross morphology

**What do the kidneys look like?** Kidney size depends on how long benign hypertension was present beforehand, so the organ can be either small or enlarged.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c018--> Look for small cortical infarcts; the cut kidney is blotchy red and yellow.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c019-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What is seen under the microscope?** Arterioles show fibrinoid necrosis.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c020--> The intima of arteries is expanded by edema and has a myxoid appearance.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c021--> The kidney form of these combined changes is malignant nephrosclerosis; by vessel size, the general terms are malignant arteriosclerosis or arteriolosclerosis.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c022--> Glomeruli may be only congested or badly damaged, up to necrosis.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c023--> In the most severe cases the cortex is infarcted focally, and thrombi are present.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c024--> A fast pressure rise causes microaneurysms and focal bleeding in retinal arterioles.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c025-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**

- **How it is studied:** on kidney sections, fibrinoid necrosis of an arteriole wall looks red on Masson trichrome.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c026-->
- By electron microscopy, a widened layer beneath glomerular capillary endothelium holds pale material.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c027-->
- Other forms of thrombotic microangiopathy produce an identical picture.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c028-->

## Clinical presentation

**What does the patient feel and show?** Patients may complain of visual problems, dizziness, headache and nausea, and some progress to encephalopathy.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c029--> Papilledema and retinal hemorrhage can be found on examination.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c030--> Proteinuria and hematuria are common.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c031--> The condition is commoner in men.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c032-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it recognized?** No single cutoff defines the disease; a very high diastolic pressure comes with kidney impairment and eye damage such as papilledema.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c033--> Ophthalmoscopy can sometimes settle the diagnosis, since the retinal arteriolar changes may be conspicuous.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c034--> A cotton wool spot seen through the ophthalmoscope is a patch of retina with ischemic necrosis and edema.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c035-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens without treatment?** Infarction of the kidneys, heart or central nervous system can be fatal.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c036--> The longer the disease lasts, the more kidney function is lost.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c037--> The disease can often be controlled with intensive treatment that lowers the blood pressure.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c038-->

## High-yield summary

- **Malignant hypertension** means blood pressure of at least 180/120 mm Hg with organ injury.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c042-->
- Small vessels show **fibrinoid necrosis** and **onion-skin** thickening.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c043-->
- In the kidney this is **malignant nephrosclerosis**, with possible cortical infarcts.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c044-->
- In the retina look for **papilledema**, hemorrhages and **cotton wool spots**.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c045-->
- Control is often achieved with intensive **antihypertensive therapy**.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c046-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is malignant hypertension a cancer?** No. The term describes a rapid, severe rise in pressure with organ injury.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c047-->

**Is it the same as benign hypertensive nephrosclerosis?** No. The benign form shows hyaline arteriolosclerosis, while the malignant form adds fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c048-->

**Does it always follow long-standing hypertension?** No. Only about half of patients have a prior benign phase.<!--c:malignant-hypertension-c049-->
