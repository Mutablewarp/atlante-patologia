## Definition and epidemiology

**What is silicosis?** It is a pneumoconiosis caused by breathing in silica (silicon dioxide) dust.<!--c:silicosis-c001--> Through the first half of the twentieth century, silica was the leading killer of exposed workers, and the disease is among the oldest on record.<!--c:silicosis-c002--> Silica exposure occurs in mining, stone cutting and many other trades; the disease was first described in sandblasters.<!--c:silicosis-c003--> Ventilation systems and masks have cut the number of cases.<!--c:silicosis-c004--> Thinner coal seams force miners to cut through more quartz, so their silicosis rates have recently risen.<!--c:silicosis-c005--> Almost every worker with long-term silica exposure develops simple nodular silicosis, the most common form.<!--c:silicosis-c006--> The latency from first exposure to nodules is 20 to 40 years, occasionally as short as 10.<!--c:silicosis-c007-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which dust causes it, and what makes it more dangerous?** Quartz, the crystalline form of silica, harms the lung more than non-crystalline (amorphous) silica.<!--c:silicosis-c008--> How active silica is in tissue is tied to the properties of its particle surface.<!--c:silicosis-c009--> Silica is most harmful when its particles measure between 0.2 and 2.0 μm.<!--c:silicosis-c010--> Acid washing (which removes the soluble coat) and sandblasting (which exposes fresh surface) both make silica more reactive.<!--c:silicosis-c011--> Compared with coal and iron dusts, which are only weakly fibrogenic, silica causes extensive fibrosis even in small amounts.<!--c:silicosis-c012--> Exposure time and dust concentration give the dose; dose and particle size decide how badly inorganic dust harms the lung.<!--c:silicosis-c013--> Where a particle lands depends on its size: those under 2.5 μm reach the acini, while those of 2.5 to 10 μm stay on bronchi and bronchioles, where mucociliary clearance removes them.<!--c:silicosis-c014-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does silica scar the lung?** Alveolar macrophages take up the inhaled silica particles.<!--c:silicosis-c015--> Macrophages die because the silica surface attaches to phospholipids and proteins of their membranes, and this damages the membrane.<!--c:silicosis-c016--> When the macrophage dies, the silica it took up is released, along with fibrogenic factors.<!--c:silicosis-c017--> The cycle feeds itself: new macrophages take up the silica set free, and the damage builds.<!--c:silicosis-c018--> These repeated rounds end in scarring, and the scar is the silicotic nodule.<!--c:silicosis-c019-->

## Gross morphology

**What does a silicotic lung look like?**
- Silicotic nodules in simple silicosis are small, usually 2 to 4 mm and under 1 cm.<!--c:silicosis-c020-->
- Eggshell calcification names the calcium that often outlines the margins of enlarged hilar nodes.<!--c:silicosis-c021-->
- In progressive massive fibrosis, small nodules coalesce into large lesions; most measure 5 to 10 cm.<!--c:silicosis-c022-->
- They are usually found in the upper zones of both lungs.<!--c:silicosis-c023-->
- They often cavitate in the center.<!--c:silicosis-c024-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does a silicotic nodule look like on a slide?** Under the microscope the nodule is mostly collagen in concentric whorls, with few cells.<!--c:silicosis-c025--> The rim of the nodule carries clusters of single-nucleus cells, chiefly lymphocytes, plus fibroblasts.<!--c:silicosis-c026--> Inside the nodule are birefringent silicate needles, which show up under polarized light.<!--c:silicosis-c027--> Acute silicosis has no nodules, only diffuse lung fibrosis.<!--c:silicosis-c028--> In silicoproteinosis the air spaces are packed with dense pink (eosinophilic) material, as in alveolar lipoproteinosis.<!--c:silicosis-c029-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**

**How it is studied:**
- **Histology:** on sections of lung, the nodule is recognized by whorled collagen and a rim of lymphocytes and fibroblasts.<!--c:silicosis-c030-->
- **Polarized light:** silicate needles in the nodule are birefringent under polarized light.<!--c:silicosis-c031-->

The packet gives no special stain, immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy or molecular test for silicosis.

## Clinical presentation

**What does the patient feel?** Simple silicosis usually causes no significant symptoms.<!--c:silicosis-c032--> Progressive massive fibrosis, or another complication, should be suspected when breathlessness in silicosis begins with effort and later occurs at rest.<!--c:silicosis-c033--> Sandblasting or boiler scaling can expose workers to heavy loads of very fine silica, which causes acute silicosis.<!--c:silicosis-c034--> Respiratory failure follows when the dyspnea of acute silicosis rapidly becomes disabling.<!--c:silicosis-c035--> The functional defect is severely restrictive.<!--c:silicosis-c036-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it found?** Simple silicosis is usually recognized on radiographs.<!--c:silicosis-c037--> Progressive massive fibrosis is the radiographic finding of masses over 2 cm in a lung already marked by simple silicosis.<!--c:silicosis-c038--> Acute silicosis reduces lung volume, and the radiograph shows diffuse linear fibrosis.<!--c:silicosis-c039-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How does it evolve, and what goes wrong?** Simple silicosis seldom causes real breathing impairment.<!--c:silicosis-c040--> In progressive massive fibrosis, disability results from lung tissue lost inside the nodules.<!--c:silicosis-c041--> The more silica the lung holds, the more likely this fibrosis is.<!--c:silicosis-c042--> Other forms of silicosis take decades to advance; the acute form takes only a few years.<!--c:silicosis-c043--> Compared with the general population, patients with silicosis have far more tuberculosis.<!--c:silicosis-c044--> Tuberculosis is especially frequent in acute silicosis and where it is highly prevalent in the population.<!--c:silicosis-c045--> Tuberculosis has become less common in the general population, yet silicosis patients are still at higher risk.<!--c:silicosis-c046-->

## High-yield summary

- **Silica** kills the macrophages that engulf it and releases fibrogenic factors.<!--c:silicosis-c049-->
- The **silicotic nodule** is a whorled collagen nodule, usually 2 to 4 mm.<!--c:silicosis-c050-->
- **Polarized light** shows birefringent silicate needles inside the nodule.<!--c:silicosis-c051-->
- **Progressive massive fibrosis** means masses over 2 cm, from merged nodules, mostly in the upper zones.<!--c:silicosis-c052-->
- **Tuberculosis** occurs far more often in silicosis.<!--c:silicosis-c053-->
- In **acute silicosis** there are no nodules, and alveoli fill with eosinophilic material (silicoproteinosis).<!--c:silicosis-c054-->
- **Eggshell calcification** can edge the hilar nodes.<!--c:silicosis-c055-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is simple silicosis disabling?** Usually not: it seldom impairs breathing, whereas progressive massive fibrosis can be disabling.<!--c:silicosis-c056-->

**Does every silicosis show silicotic nodules?** No. Acute silicosis has none, and shows eosinophilic alveolar material instead.<!--c:silicosis-c057-->

**Does the tuberculosis risk fade as tuberculosis becomes rarer?** No. The link outlasts the fall in tuberculosis rates.<!--c:silicosis-c058-->

**Is coal workers' lung just silicosis?** No. Anthracosilicosis can arise when coal dust carries silica, which is fibrogenic; carbon alone is harmless.<!--c:silicosis-c059-->
