## Definition and epidemiology

**What is diabetic nephropathy?** Diabetic glomerulosclerosis is the kidney arm of the small-vessel disease of diabetes, and it ends in proteinuria and progressive renal failure.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c001--> It occurs in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c002--> About 25% of diabetic patients develop it.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c003--> In the United States it is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease, behind about 40% of cases.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Who is at risk?** In type 2 diabetes the risk is higher in black patients and in Native Americans.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c005--> The setting is diabetes itself: the glomerular lesion is the local piece of small-vessel damage found all over the body.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c006-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does diabetes damage the glomerulus?** In diabetes, small vessels make more basement membrane material, driven by the altered metabolism.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c007--> Oxidative stress and non-enzymatic sugar attachment to blood and matrix proteins, notably in the GBM and mesangium, may injure podocytes and add matrix.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c008--> The number of podocytes falls early in the disease.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c009-->

## Gross morphology

**Which gross lesion goes with diabetic nephropathy?** Papillary necrosis becomes more likely when vessels narrow and the medulla receives less blood.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c010--> [[pyelonephritis|Pyelonephritis]] is favored by the same vascular change.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c011-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What changes appear in the glomerulus, and in what order?** Early on, the mesangial matrix expands, the GBM thickens and the glomerulus enlarges.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c012--> A slight rise in mesangial cells can come with the extra matrix.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c013--> In established disease the whole glomerulus shows a uniformly thick GBM and widespread mesangial matrix expansion.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c014--> Sclerotic Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodules are added to this picture.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c015--> Leaked plasma proteins can form rounded "capsular drops" sandwiched between the parietal epithelium and the Bowman capsule.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c016--> Along the capillary loops, they can also collect under the endothelium as "hyaline caps".<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c017--> Tubular basement membranes are thickened.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c018-->

**What happens to the vessels?** Hyaline arteriolosclerosis results from sclerosis and plasma protein insudation in both the afferent and the efferent arteriole.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c019--> Arteriosclerosis of the renal vessels is usually widespread as well.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c020-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which method?**

**How it is studied:**
- Once the disease causes symptoms, light microscopy shows an expanded mesangial matrix above all, together with a thick GBM.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c021-->
- By electron microscopy, the lamina densa can thicken 5 to 10 times.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c022-->
- By electron microscopy, the nodules hold the largest increase of mesangial matrix.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c023-->
- By electron microscopy, lipid fragments lie inside the dense hyaline masses.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c024-->
- By immunofluorescence, the GBM gives a continuous linear signal from trapped plasma proteins such as IgG and albumin.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c025-->
- Nonenzymatic glycosylation may explain why proteins stick nonspecifically to the thickened GBM, which produces this linear signal.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c026-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present and evolve?** Overt proteinuria appears 10 to 15 years after diabetes begins.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c027--> This heavy proteinuria frequently produces nephrotic syndrome.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c028--> With time the disease progresses to renal failure.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c029-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What is the earliest sign?** Microalbuminuria, meaning a little extra protein in the urine, shows up first.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c030-->

## Complications and prognosis

**Can progression be slowed?** Tight glucose control slows established disease and makes it less likely to start.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c031--> A lower-protein diet and hypertension treatment also slow its course.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c032-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |

| Diabetic nephropathy | Thick GBM, diffuse mesangial expansion and sclerotic nodules; plasma proteins trapped in a linear pattern.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c033--> | Light microscopy and immunofluorescence |
| [[membranous-nephropathy]] | Capillary loops show a granular IgG rim; from stage II, basement membrane spikes appear between the subepithelial deposits.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c034--> | Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy |

## High-yield summary

- The lesion is **diabetic glomerulosclerosis**, one part of a small-vessel disease.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c036-->
- The first changes are a **thicker GBM** and a **larger mesangial matrix**.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c037-->
- Established disease adds **Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodules**.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c038-->
- Afferent and efferent arterioles show **hyaline arteriolosclerosis**.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c039-->
- Poor medullary blood supply raises the risk of **papillary necrosis** and pyelonephritis.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c040-->
- The earliest sign is **microalbuminuria**; overt proteinuria follows after 10 to 15 years.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c041-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Are Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodules the first lesion?** No. Mesangial expansion, a thick GBM and an enlarged glomerulus come first; nodules belong to established disease.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c042-->

**Why does immunofluorescence show IgG along the diabetic GBM?** Because the thickened GBM adsorbs plasma proteins nonspecifically.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c043-->

**Is the first sign heavy proteinuria?** No. The first sign is microalbuminuria; overt proteinuria comes 10 to 15 years after onset.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c044-->

**Is papillary necrosis unrelated to the glomerular disease?** No. Narrowed vessels cut the blood supply to the medulla, which makes papillary necrosis more likely.<!--c:diabetic-nephropathy-c045-->

## Mnemonics
