## Definition and epidemiology

**What is the difference between grade and stage?** Grade tells how closely the tumor cells and architecture resemble the tissue of origin.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c001--> Stage tells how far the tumor has spread in the patient.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c002--> Survival figures are grouped by stage at diagnosis, and stage often decides the surgery and further treatment.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

Not applicable.

## Pathogenesis

**Why would differentiation predict behavior?** The more a tumor resembles its tissue of origin, the less aggressive it tends to be, though this is not universal.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c004--> Low grade therefore means well differentiated, and high grade means poorly differentiated.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c005--> A tumor with many mitoses, or with atypical ones, is likely growing fast.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c006--> Because grading relies on the observer's judgment, it can be only semiquantitative.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c007-->

## Gross morphology

**Can grade be guessed from the naked-eye appearance?** Ulcerated bladder masses that invade the wall tend to be high grade; small papillary mucosal growths, low grade.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c008-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the pathologist look at to assign a grade?** Grade rests on two things: how anaplastic the cells look and how many are proliferating.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c009--> Uneven cell size and shape signal a higher degree of anaplasia.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c010--> A poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma has few glands and looks unlike normal tissue; a well-differentiated one is gland-rich.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c011--> Grades usually number three or four, and a higher number means a more malignant tumor.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c012--> In lung squamous carcinoma, grade 1 still makes keratin pearls; grade 3 cells are hardly recognizable as squamous.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c013-->

**Which systems are used for specific tumors?**
- Breast: Nottingham grade 1, 2 or 3 sums three scores: how much the nuclei vary, how often cells divide and how well glands form.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c014-->
- Bladder: papillary urothelial tumors have three grades: low-grade carcinoma, high-grade carcinoma and the milder low-malignant-potential neoplasm.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c015--> In high-grade papillary carcinoma the epithelium is disorganized and the nuclei are strongly hyperchromatic and pleomorphic.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c016-->
- Prostate: the Gleason system, illustrated in the reference with grades 3, 4 and 5.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c017-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** grade comes from examining tumor architecture and cytology.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c018-->
- A pathologist must examine the nodes removed at surgery to tell whether they hold metastasis.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c019-->
- Injected dye and radioactive tracer reveal the sentinel node, where metastasis is most likely to sit.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c020-->
- When the sentinel node contains no tumor, the surgeon can safely leave out axillary dissection.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c021-->
- Routine slides can miss tumor cells in a node; cytokeratin immunostaining makes the epithelial ones visible.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c022-->
- A metastasis that stains for PSA by immunohistochemistry is likely to come from the prostate.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c023-->
- Immunohistochemistry on the breast tumor shows whether it carries estrogen and progesterone receptors.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c024-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does stage change treatment?** Ta, Tis and T1 bladder tumors, which spare the muscle, are usually watched closely after endoscopic removal and bladder instillations.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c025--> Muscle-invasive (T2) bladder tumors call for radical cystectomy, as do some advanced ones.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c026--> Radiation is one option for prostate tumors confined to the gland (T1, T2); surgery and ablation are others.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c027--> Radiation plus androgen deprivation is preferred in T3 prostate cancer, since about half of patients already have pelvic node spread that surgery cannot cure.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c028--> In breast cancer, sentinel node biopsy lowers the lymphedema and nerve damage risk of axillary dissection.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c029-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is a TNM stage built?** In TNM, M records distant metastasis, N the regional node spread, and T the size and local extent of the primary tumor.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c030--> A T3N2M0 breast cancer has no detected metastasis (M0), moderate axillary node involvement (N2) and a large primary tumor (T3).<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c031--> Criteria for T, N and M differ between tumor types and can be intricate.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c032--> Hematologic malignancies are staged with systems of their own.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c033--> Stage I to IV, sometimes subdivided, condenses T, N and M into one label.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c034-->

**What does a stage group look like in practice?**
- A lung tumor is stage I only if it is small and neither nodes nor distant sites are involved.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c035-->
- What mainly separates lung stage II from stage III is the extent of regional node involvement.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c036-->
- Any lung tumor with distant metastasis is stage IV.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c037-->
- In colorectal cancer, a tumor that invades the submucosa is T1; one that enters the muscularis propria without crossing it is T2.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c038-->
- T3 is spread into soft tissue around the colon or rectum; T4 breaks through the serosa or involves neighboring organs.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c039-->
- The T stage of a bladder cancer is set by how deep it invades the bladder wall.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c040-->
- Tumor size in the breast is measured on the invasive component alone.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c041--> Whatever the size, a breast tumor is T4 if it ulcerates through the skin or invades the chest wall.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c042-->
- Staged Tis, bladder carcinoma in situ stays flat, with an intact basement membrane and no invasion.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c043-->

## Complications and prognosis

**Which predicts outcome better, grade or stage?** Stage beats grade for forecasting course and guiding therapy, because it shows what the tumor really does in this patient.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c048--> A low cytologic grade does not guarantee an indolent tumor.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c049--> Stage outweighs every other factor in predicting prognosis in lung cancer.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c050--> In breast cancer, axillary node metastasis strongly influences prognosis.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c051--> Grade 2 or 3 breast cancer carries significantly worse survival than grade 1.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c052--> Prognosis in invasive urothelial carcinoma depends on how deeply the tumor invades the bladder wall or beyond.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c053--> By stage, 5-year survival in prostate cancer falls from about 90% (T1, T2) to 40% (T3) and 10% (T4).<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c054-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Grade | Describes how abnormal the cells and architecture look, from well to poorly differentiated.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c055--> | Histologic and cytologic examination |
| Stage | Describes extent: nodes, distant sites, size.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c056--> | TNM work-up, node pathology |
| [[carcinoma-in-situ]] | Can be high grade yet is stage Tis, because it does not invade.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c057--> | Biopsy showing an intact basement membrane |

## High-yield summary

- **Grade** is differentiation under the microscope; **stage** is spread in the patient.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c058-->
- **TNM** stands for primary tumor, regional nodes and distant metastasis.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c059-->
- **Stage** usually predicts outcome better than grade.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c060-->
- The **Nottingham** system grades breast cancer from 1 to 3.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c061-->
- A negative **sentinel node** lets the surgeon skip axillary dissection.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c062-->
- **Tis** is carcinoma in situ, noninvasive.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c063-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a high grade mean an advanced stage?** No. Grade is cell appearance and stage is spread, and a flat high-grade carcinoma in situ can be stage Tis.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c064-->

**Is a low-grade tumor always harmless?** No. Grade does not always match behavior, so some low-grade tumors turn out very aggressive.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c065-->

**Is TNM the same for every cancer?** No. The definitions of T, N and M change from one tumor type to another.<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c066-->

## Mnemonics

**G**rade = **G**lass (what you see in the microscope); **S**tage = **S**pread (how far it has gone).<!--c:tumor-grading-and-staging-c067-->
