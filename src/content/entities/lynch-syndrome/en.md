## Definition and epidemiology
**What is Lynch syndrome?** It is an autosomal dominant inherited cancer syndrome, also called hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c001--> About 3% to 5% of all colorectal cancers arise in this syndrome.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c002-->

## Etiology and risk factors
**Which gene is faulty?** The disease arises when one DNA mismatch repair (MMR) gene carries a germline mutation.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c003--> Most often the affected genes are *MSH2* (chromosome 2p) and *MLH1* (3p).<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c004--> Mutations of *MSH6* (2p) and *PMS2* (7p) are rarer.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c005-->

## Pathogenesis
**Why does one inherited defect lead to cancer?** The inherited mutation hits one allele of one MMR gene, so only one working copy remains.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c006--> Repair fails once a somatic "second hit" damages that remaining allele.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c007--> Chance errors in copying DNA are now left unfixed.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c008--> Microsatellites are repeated DNA stretches that are error-prone when copied, so unrepaired mistakes pile up there and the genome shows microsatellite instability (MSI).<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c009--> Unrepaired mutations accumulate and disable genes of two kinds: other MMR genes, and genes that govern growth and differentiation.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c010-->

## Gross morphology
Not applicable.

## Microscopic morphology
**What do the tumors look like?** Mucin production and signet ring cells are more frequent in Lynch tumors than in sporadic ones.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c011--> A solid, medullary growth pattern is also more frequent.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c012--> Lymphocytes are abundant inside these tumors, which also provoke Crohn-like lymphocytic reactions.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c013-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings
**How is mismatch repair deficiency detected?**
- **Immunohistochemistry (IHC)**: staining the tumor for MMR proteins shows their levels.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c014-->
- **MSI testing** of the tumor also reveals the deficiency.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c015-->
- **Sequencing** of the MMR genes can reveal it as well.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c016-->
- Once a specific mutation is found, it serves as the target when other family members are evaluated.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c017-->

## Clinical presentation
**Who is the typical patient?** Cancer tends to present early in life.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c018--> Adenomas are few, hence the term "nonpolyposis".<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c019--> Proximal to the splenic flexure is where about 70% of the colorectal tumors arise.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c020--> Anemia from iron loss may be the first sign, since right-sided tumors can bleed quietly without obstructing.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c021-->

**Which other cancers occur?** Endometrial and ovarian cancers are among the extracolonic tumors.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c022--> Gastrointestinal tumors outside the colon, and pancreatobiliary tumors, also occur.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c023--> The urinary tract and CNS can be involved as well.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c024--> Muir–Torre syndrome is the Lynch variant with sebaceous skin tumors, benign or malignant.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c025-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging
**Whom should we test?** The revised Bethesda guidelines raise suspicion when any one of these is met:
- Colorectal cancer (CRC) diagnosed before age 50.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c026-->
- Two or more tumors in the same person, whether CRCs or other Lynch-related tumors.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c027-->
- High-MSI morphology in a CRC found before age 60.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c028-->
- CRC plus an affected first-degree relative (CRC or another Lynch-related tumor), when a cancer came before 50 or an adenoma before 40.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c029-->
- CRC in a family with two or more affected relatives (CRC or another Lynch-related tumor), at any age.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c030-->

## Complications and prognosis
**What risk continues after the first cancer?** A patient can develop several colorectal cancers, at the same time or later.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c031-->

## High-yield summary
- A **germline mutation of an MMR gene**, mostly *MSH2* or *MLH1*, causes Lynch syndrome.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c034-->
- **Microsatellite instability** results from unrepaired replication errors.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c035-->
- The picture is **early-onset cancer with few adenomas**.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c036-->
- About **70%** of the colorectal tumors are proximal to the splenic flexure.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c037-->
- Among extracolonic tumors, the **endometrium** and **ovary** are involved.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c038-->
- **Muir–Torre syndrome** is Lynch syndrome with sebaceous skin tumors.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c039-->
- **MMR immunostaining** assesses protein levels in the tumor and is one way to find the deficiency.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c040-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps
**Does "nonpolyposis" mean no polyps at all?** No. These patients have few adenomas, not none.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c041-->

**Is Lynch syndrome only a colon disease?** No. It also causes cancers outside the colon.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c042-->

**Does meeting the Bethesda criteria prove Lynch syndrome?** No. The criteria only raise the possibility.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c043-->

**Are both hits inherited?** No. The first is germline; the second is a somatic mutation in the tumor.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c044-->

## Mnemonics
**LYNCH**: **L**ymphocytes in the tumor, **Y**oung patient, **N**o polyposis, **C**olon proximal, **H**eritable MMR mutation.<!--c:lynch-syndrome-c045-->
