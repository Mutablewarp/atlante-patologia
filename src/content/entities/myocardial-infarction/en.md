## Definition and epidemiology

**What is a myocardial infarct?** It is necrosis of heart muscle caused by ischemia; in type 1 infarcts the usual cause is a coronary thrombus.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c001--> On the ECG, infarcts are STEMI (ST elevation) or non-STEMI.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c002--> A spontaneous coronary thrombus is the usual trigger of type 1 infarcts, which are often STEMI.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c003--> A prolonged, marked supply-demand mismatch causes type 2 infarcts, which are mostly non-STEMI.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What can starve the myocardium of oxygen?**
- Anemia is survivable with normal coronaries, but severe atherosclerosis can turn it into infarction.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c005-->
- Fever, hyperthyroidism, hypertension, aortic stenosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy make the heart work harder and need more oxygen.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c006-->
- Vasculitis, aortic dissection and anomalous coronary origin can each obstruct coronary arteries.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c007-->
- The thicker wall and heavier workload of the left ventricle make it the usual site of infarction, much more often than the right.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c008-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does an infarct grow?** Several hours pass before necrosis, which begins in the subendocardium, reaches the epicardium.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c009--> Whether necrosis stays patchy and subendocardial or becomes transmural depends on how long the artery stays blocked.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c010--> Collateral arterial supply mostly decides how far necrosis spreads through the wall.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c011--> Beyond 20 to 30 minutes of total ischemia, injury turns irreversible and cells die in succession.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c012--> When the sarcolemma breaks down, troponins leak from the myocyte.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c013--> Reperfusion releases reactive oxygen species, which injure the sarcolemma and let calcium flood into the myocytes.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c014-->

## Gross morphology

**Where is the infarct, and how does it look as it ages?**
- Patchy inner-wall damage is typical of type 2; type 1 gives one full-thickness lesion.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c015-->
- Subendocardial infarcts lie in the deepest third to half of the wall and may form a ring.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c016-->
- Transmural infarcts go through the whole wall, so each usually maps to one large coronary artery.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c017-->
- Proximal right coronary artery occlusion causes the "inferior" infarct, of the posterior left ventricle and septum.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c018-->
- Blockage of the left anterior descending artery infarcts the anterior wall, the anterior septum and the apex.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c019-->
- The left circumflex artery, the least common culprit, infarcts the lateral wall.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c020-->
- Nothing is grossly visible for the first 12 hours.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c021-->
- Pallor of the cut surface appears by 24 hours.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c022-->
- At 3 to 5 days a ring of hyperemia surrounds the dead, yellowish center, so the infarct looks mottled.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c023-->
- By 2 to 3 weeks the infarct is soft and jelly-like, and lies sunken.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c024-->
- A healed infarct is a shrunken gray scar.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c025-->
- A reperfused infarct is hemorrhagic.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c026-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the microscope show, day by day?** Electron microscopy alone shows the irreversible lesion, appearing at 30 to 60 minutes: swollen mitochondria with amorphous matrix densities.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c027--> Coagulative necrosis is evident by 24 hours, with wavy fibers that take up eosin intensely.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c028--> Neutrophil numbers are highest at 2 to 3 days, at the infarct edge, where blood still flows.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c029--> At days 5 to 7, new collagen and myofibroblasts appear and edge macrophages clear dead muscle.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c030--> Scar spreads from the margin to the center, starting about day 5.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c031--> After 4 weeks the scar is dense fibrous tissue that gradually becomes more compact and less cellular.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c032--> When blood flow returns, repair is quicker and neutrophils invade the whole infarct, edge included.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c033--> Contraction band necrosis marks reperfusion: sarcomeres overcontract into eosinophilic crosswise bands.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c034-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at?**

**How it is studied:**
- Routine H&E: date the infarct from the cells present (neutrophils, macrophages, new collagen).<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c035-->
- On trichrome, scar collagen stains blue-green and stops abruptly where living myocardium begins.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c036-->
- The earliest ischemic changes cannot be seen without an electron microscope.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c037-->
- Blood tests: cardiac troponin or MB-CK rises in serum when myocardium has died.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c038-->

No immunohistochemical or molecular test is supported.

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** Abrupt crushing chest pain, sweating and nausea are typical; the pain can pass for indigestion.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c039--> Patients with a denervated transplanted heart, and diabetics whose autonomic nerves are impaired, often have infarcts without symptoms.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c040-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What confirms the diagnosis?** STEMI shows ST elevation on the ECG, usually followed by Q waves, whereas non-STEMI shows ST depression.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c041--> Troponin I or T, specific for heart muscle, is the marker measured most often.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c042--> Biomarker levels must be seen rising or falling in step with the clinical course for the diagnosis to stand.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c043--> STEMI is never without a biomarker rise; demand-related unstable angina has none.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c044-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What goes wrong after an infarct, and what limits the damage?**
- Mortality within 30 days is now below 5%, versus 30% in the 1950s.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c045-->
- Almost every infarct patient has an arrhythmia at some point.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c046-->
- If the left ventricle fails early after an infarct, a large part of the muscle has usually been lost.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c047-->
- Cardiogenic shock tends to follow early loss of at least 40% of the left ventricle, with up to 90% mortality.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c048-->
- The wall is weakest on days 2 to 7, and free wall rupture is likeliest then.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c049-->
- After such a rupture, blood fills the pericardium and tamponade usually kills.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c050-->
- Only transmural infarcts can rupture the free wall.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c051-->
- Septal rupture creates a left-to-right shunt, and its size sets the prognosis.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c052-->
- In a true aneurysm, the wall is weakened yet still continuous, and it balloons outward.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c053-->
- A false aneurysm is a contained rupture; its wall is pericardium and scar, with no ventricular muscle.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c054-->
- Pieces of mural thrombus can embolize and cause stroke; autopsy finds such a thrombus over the infarct in a third to half of fatal cases.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c055-->
- Pericarditis complicates 10% to 20% of transmural infarcts.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c056-->
- An autoimmune mechanism is probably behind Dressler syndrome, a pericarditis that appears 2 to 10 weeks after infarction.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c057-->
- To salvage ischemic myocytes, blood flow must be restored: by bypass grafting, by percutaneous coronary intervention with stenting, or by thrombolysis.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c058-->
- Little ischemic muscle can still be saved once 12 hours have passed.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c059-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What else gives ischemic chest pain or ischemic damage?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Unstable angina (demand-related) | Ischemic symptoms without necrosis.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c060--> | Serial troponin |
| [[ischemia-reperfusion-injury]] | Stunned myocardium: contraction is depressed for hours after reperfusion, without necrosis.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c061--> | Troponin; echocardiography |
| Ischemic cardiomyopathy | Left ventricular failure without an obvious infarct.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c062--> | Histology: diffuse fibrosis |

## High-yield summary

- A **transmural** infarct follows coronary thrombosis and is often STEMI.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c063-->
- A **subendocardial** infarct is patchy and usually non-STEMI.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c064-->
- **Troponin** I or T is the standard blood marker.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c065-->
- **Neutrophils** peak at 2 to 3 days.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c066-->
- **Contraction bands** and hemorrhage point to reperfusion.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c067-->
- **Rupture** peaks between days 2 and 7.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c068-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a normal-looking heart early on exclude an infarct?** No. Gross inspection of the heart shows nothing for the first 12 hours.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c069-->

**Does every infarct have a coronary thrombus?** No. Type 2 infarcts come from supply-demand imbalance, and thrombosis is less common in subendocardial infarcts.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c070-->

**Is a reperfused infarct pale?** No. Reperfusion makes it hemorrhagic.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c071-->

**Do only large infarcts rupture?** No. Rupture usually strikes transmural infarcts of modest size.<!--c:myocardial-infarction-c072-->
