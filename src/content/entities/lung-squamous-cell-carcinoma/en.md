## Definition and epidemiology

**What is lung squamous cell carcinoma, and how common is it?** Among the major forms of lung cancer, small cell carcinoma, large cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma stand beside squamous cell carcinoma.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c001--> In the United States, about one lung cancer in five is of this type, which makes it the second most frequent.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c002--> Men are affected more often than women.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What injures the bronchial lining enough to start it?** Squamous metaplasia is a common outcome when the bronchial lining repairs itself after an injury such as cigarette smoking.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c004-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does a bronchus end up with a squamous cancer?** As in the cervix and skin, bronchial squamous metaplasia can pass through dysplasia and in situ carcinoma before it invades.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c005--> After injury, pluripotent basal cells drive the repair; squamous metaplasia is the frequent result.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c006-->

## Gross morphology

**Where in the lung does the tumor sit, and what does it look like?**
- Major and segmental bronchi are the usual site of origin, so the tumor typically sits in the center of the lung.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c007-->
- Only about 10% start in the periphery.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c008-->
- The ulcerated lesion breaks out of the bronchus and invades the nearby lung.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c009-->
- Central cavitation is frequent.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c010-->
- An endobronchial mass is how a central tumor appears in some patients.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c011-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What do the tumor cells look like?** Basaloid, keratinizing and nonkeratinizing are the three histologic subtypes.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c012--> Keratinization or intercellular bridges are obvious in many of them.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c013--> Keratin pearls, typical of well-differentiated tumors, are pink keratin cores ringed by squamous cells like onion layers.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c014--> Isolated keratinizing cells stand out with deeply pink, glassy cytoplasm.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c015--> The bridges between cells seen in some well-differentiated tumors are desmosomal contacts.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c016--> With no keratin, a very poorly differentiated squamous tumor is easily mistaken for large cell or small cell carcinoma.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c017--> The basaloid type is recognized by peripheral palisading of small cells with a moderate amount of pink cytoplasm.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c018-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- Immunohistochemistry: in nonkeratinizing and basaloid tumors, widespread p40 positivity helps confirm squamous origin.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c019-->
- **How it is studied:** this card does not cover specimen types or sampling, so only the reading of the slide is given here.
- On the slide, the clues to squamous differentiation are keratin pearls, intercellular bridges and single-cell keratinization.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c020-->
- A lung cancer takes its type from its most differentiated area, but any small cell component overrides that.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c021-->
- Grading goes by the area that is least differentiated.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c022-->
- Molecular: FGFR inhibitors are under clinical testing, and FGFR1 amplification has been reported in squamous cell carcinoma.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c023-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Why does exact subtyping matter?** The lump term "non–small cell carcinoma" is discouraged, since newer drug treatments make it essential to name the exact subtype.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c024--> The old name "bronchogenic carcinoma" is dropped, because roughly one primary lung cancer in four does not clearly start in a bronchus.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c025-->

## Complications and prognosis

**Is there a molecular marker of poor outcome?** Many non–small cell lung cancers lack PTEN, a tumor suppressor; its absence goes with worse outcome and drug resistance.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c026-->

## High-yield summary

- Look for a **central** origin: major or segmental bronchi.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c029-->
- Smoking injury leads to **squamous metaplasia**, then dysplasia, carcinoma in situ and invasion.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c030-->
- **Keratin pearls** and intercellular bridges mark well-differentiated tumors.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c031-->
- Diffuse **p40** supports nonkeratinizing and basaloid squamous carcinoma.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c032-->
- **Central cavitation** is frequent.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c033-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Are all squamous lung cancers central?** No. Most are central, but about 10% arise in the periphery.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c034-->

**Does a tumor with no keratin pearls rule out squamous carcinoma?** No. Some very poorly differentiated squamous tumors lack keratinization; diffuse p40 helps to recognize the nonkeratinizing ones.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c035-->

**Is a tumor of large poorly differentiated cells with squamous foci a large cell carcinoma?** No. The squamous foci set the type, so it counts as a poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma.<!--c:lung-squamous-cell-carcinoma-c036-->
