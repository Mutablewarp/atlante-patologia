## Definition and epidemiology

**What is a pulmonary embolism?** A pulmonary embolism (PE) is the blockage of a pulmonary artery by material carried in from the veins, usually a clot.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c001--> A thromboembolus is clot material that travels in the blood and lodges in a vessel far from where it formed.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c002--> More than half of autopsies find pulmonary thromboemboli.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c003--> PE is potentially fatal.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Where do the emboli come from, and who is at risk?** The deep veins of the legs are the origin of about 90% of PEs.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c005--> The iliofemoral veins are where most lethal emboli begin.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c006--> About half of the patients whose emboli come from the leg veins show no sign of DVT.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c007--> Emboli can also arise in the pelvic veins or the right heart.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c008--> Older age and obesity raise the risk of PE after surgery.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c010--> After long bed rest, the first time up can free a piece of a clot that stasis had formed.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c011--> Rarely, a fatal PE follows a venous clot that formed while sitting still on a flight.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c012-->
## Pathogenesis

**What happens when the embolus reaches the lung?** A PE presents differently by timing (acute or chronic), embolus size and patient health.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c015--> Both lungs lose flow when a large saddle embolus sits astride the main pulmonary artery's split.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c016--> When a massive PE suddenly blocks right ventricular outflow, the right ventricle fails acutely.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c017--> With the right ventricle failing, left ventricular output drops sharply and blood pressure falls suddenly.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c018--> Shock and immediate death often follow when over half the pulmonary arteries are suddenly blocked.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c019--> Death can also follow smaller emboli lodged in several secondary branches.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c020--> Small emboli are seldom lethal; they usually come to rest in peripheral pulmonary arteries.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c021--> The lung is shielded from ischemia by two blood supplies, pulmonary and bronchial.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c022--> So 75% of small emboli leave the lung uninfarcted.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c023--> Roughly 15% to 20% of PEs do cause a [[pulmonary-infarction]].<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c024--> Infarction usually needs one supply already impaired, as in chronic lung disease or congestive heart failure.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c025--> Small emboli release vasoactive substances, which cause reflex vasoconstriction and bronchoconstriction.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c026--> Pulmonary hypertension can result from silent small emboli that keep arriving from distant veins and slowly shrink the vascular bed.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c027--> CTEPH is the pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure that follow a build-up of mostly silent emboli in small lung arteries.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c028--> Paradoxical embolism, a venous embolus blocking a systemic artery after passing an incompletely closed foramen ovale, mostly arises with a right-to-left shunt.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c029-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the lung look like?** The saddle embolus sits at the branching point of the main pulmonary artery and is exposed by opening the vessel.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c030--> A lung infarct has its base on the pleura, making it pyramidal.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c031--> These infarcts are typically hemorrhagic: the bronchial artery still delivers blood to the dead tissue.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c032--> In CTEPH, a platelet-rich plaque in the arteries of both lungs can be taken out intact at surgery.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c033-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What changes are seen under the microscope?** An older infarct has a pale center, once its blood has been resorbed.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c034--> Healing ends in a fibrous scar: the rim of the infarct fills with granulation tissue, which then organizes.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c035--> In partial infarction the lung regenerates without scarring: collateral flow keeps it alive and its supporting framework survives the bleeding and cell death.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c036--> After organization, a thromboembolus may persist only as fibrous cords on the inner wall of a pulmonary artery.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c037--> A web is a fibrous strand across a small pulmonary artery, left by an organized embolus.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c038-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does PE present?** An acute PE may be small and silent.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c041--> Others have only brief shortness of breath and rapid breathing.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c042--> Hemoptysis and pleuritic chest pain accompany an infarct.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c043--> Fluid in the pleural space is frequent and often bloody.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c044--> Sudden death through cardiovascular collapse is also possible.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c045--> A few patients with emboli but no infarct have hypotension plus chest pain and respiratory symptoms.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c046--> Small calf thrombi are usually silent.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c047--> The Homan sign refers to calf pain linked with forcing the foot upward at the ankle.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c048-->
## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which imaging findings help?** A segmental artery in chronic PE can show a dark, partly blocking thrombus on contrast CT.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c050--> Partial infarction heals without a scar, even though clinical and imaging findings suggest a true infarct.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c051-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What is the outcome?** A small embolus resolves fully only with clot lysis and enough lung reserve, vascular and bronchial, for its load.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c052--> About half of the emboli are gone or organized by 8 weeks on imaging, and the vessels narrow only slightly.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c053--> In hospital, a patient who looks on track to recover can die suddenly of a massive PE.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c054--> Anticoagulation is the main treatment of DVT with symptoms; clot-lysing drugs suit only some patients.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c055-->

## High-yield summary

- About 90% of PEs come from the **deep leg veins**.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c065-->
- A **saddle embolus** blocks flow to both lungs.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c066-->
- **Massive PE** kills through acute **right ventricular failure** and a sudden fall in cardiac output.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c067-->
- A lung infarct is **pyramidal** and **hemorrhagic**; most small emboli spare the lung thanks to its **two arterial supplies**.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c068-->
- **Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension** follows recurrent, mostly silent small emboli.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c069-->
- **Paradoxical embolism** crosses a patent foramen ovale into systemic arteries.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c070-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does every PE cause an infarct?** No. The lung's two blood supplies mean most small emboli leave it uninfarcted.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c071-->

**Is a pulmonary infarct pale?** Not at first. The infarct is hemorrhagic because bronchial artery flow keeps reaching the necrotic area.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c072-->

**Will the legs always show DVT when the emboli come from the leg veins?** No. About half of the patients whose emboli come from the leg veins show no sign of DVT.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c073-->

**Is every PE dramatic?** No. Small and chronic emboli can be silent, yet repeated ones can end as pulmonary hypertension.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c074-->

**Must a deadly embolus be large?** No. A number of smaller emboli in secondary branches can kill as well.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c075-->

## Mnemonics

**LEGS:** **L**eg veins are the source, **E**mbolus size decides the outcome, **G**iant ones saddle across the main artery, **S**mall ones spare the lung thanks to its dual supply.<!--c:pulmonary-embolism-c076-->
