## Definition and epidemiology

**What is bacterial pneumonia?** Bacteria inflame the lung tissue until it turns solid (consolidation).<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c001-->

Lobar pneumonia solidifies one entire lobe.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c002-->
Bronchopneumonia is patchy: the solid areas hug the airways, in one lobe or in several.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c003-->
Today the two terms matter little clinically.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c004-->
Organism-based grouping is preferred: the germ often dictates the picture and the therapy.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c005-->
By setting, pneumonia is opportunistic (defective immunity), nosocomial (arising in hospital) or community-acquired (outside hospital, no primary immune disorder).<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c006-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Where do the bacteria come from?** Aspiration of secretions is the usual route: the bacteria already live in the patient's oropharynx and nasopharynx.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c007-->
Blood spread, inhalation from the environment and, rarely, extension from a neighboring site are the other routes.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c008-->
Smoking and alcoholism weaken host defenses, as do chronic illnesses such as diabetes.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c009-->

**Which organisms matter?**
- Lobar pneumonia has traditionally been caused by *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (pneumococcus).<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c010-->
- *Klebsiella pneumoniae* is the only other bacterium that commonly produces a lobar pattern.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c011-->
- Risk factors for Klebsiella pneumonia: diabetes, chronic lung disease, and being a middle-aged man, often an alcoholic.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c012-->
- *S. aureus* often infects the lung on top of influenza or another viral airway infection.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c013-->
- Group A *Streptococcus pyogenes* pneumonia typically follows a viral infection, in debilitated patients.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c014-->
- *Legionella pneumophila* lives in water; outbreaks came from contaminated cooling towers and similar sources.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c015-->
- AIDS and immunosuppressive drugs have made gram-negative pneumonia, chiefly from *E. coli* and *P. aeruginosa*, more common.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c016-->
- Even without immunosuppression, abdominal or urogenital surgery can let blood-borne *E. coli* seed the lung.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c017-->
- Cystic fibrosis, burns and immunocompromise are the usual settings of Pseudomonas pneumonia.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c018-->

**What is the role of aspiration?** Mouth anaerobes such as *Bacteroides* are aspirated when swallowing fails, e.g., in anesthesia or seizures.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c019-->

## Pathogenesis

**How do pneumococci reach the alveoli?** Pneumococci live in the nasopharynx and multiply in the bronchial secretions that a viral upper respiratory infection stimulates.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c020-->
Inflammation begins once watery secretions wash the bacteria into the alveoli.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c021-->
Alcohol intoxication or anesthesia can impair the epiglottic reflexes, so pneumococci slip into the airway.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c022-->

**Why does the capsule matter?** Alveolar macrophages can ingest pneumococci only after opsonization, because the capsule resists phagocytosis.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c023-->

**What are the stages of lobar pneumonia?**
1. Edema and congestion: protein- and bacteria-laden fluid floods the alveoli.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c024-->
2. Red hepatization: neutrophils and blood flood the alveoli, and the congested lung turns firm and liver-like.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c025-->
3. Gray hepatization: congestion eases but the lung stays firm; neutrophils break down and macrophages clear the debris.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c026-->
4. Resolution: the lung slowly returns to normal as the exudate is removed.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c027-->

With antibiotics, lobar pneumonia now tends to involve several lobes, each only partly.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c030-->

**How does bronchopneumonia differ?** In bronchopneumonia the small bronchioles are inflamed, and neutrophils spill into the alveoli beside them.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c028-->
It usually arises in the dependent, posterior lung, mostly in terminally ill patients.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c029-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the lung look like?** In lobar pneumonia a whole lobe is solid, while the neighboring lobe stays aerated.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c031-->
In bronchopneumonia each solid patch surrounds a bronchus or bronchiole, and the patches lie apart.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c032-->
Thick capsules make Klebsiella-infected lung look mucoid when cut.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c033-->
The enlarged lobe pushes its fissure outward, toward the healthy lung.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c034-->
Klebsiella also tends toward necrosis and abscesses.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c035-->
Staphylococcal pneumonia leaves the lung studded with small abscesses.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c036-->
When an abscess drains into an airway, inhaled air can inflate it into a thin-walled cyst (pneumatocele).<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c037-->
Lungs in fatal *S. pyogenes* pneumonia are waterlogged with bloody edema, not dry and hepatized.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c038-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What fills the alveoli?** In pneumococcal pneumonia, neutrophils plus the occasional macrophage.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c039-->
Legionella exudate contains fibrin and varies: neutrophils dominate in some cases, macrophages in others.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c040-->
Pseudomonas packs vessel walls, causing a vasculitis that can infarct the lung.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c041-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** under the microscope, the alveolar contents give the stage: edema with bacteria, then neutrophils with red cells, then macrophages with debris.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c042-->
- Legionella is hard to demonstrate without silver or immunofluorescent stains, despite being gram-negative.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c043-->

The reference pages give no detail on sampling, electron microscopy or molecular tests.

## Clinical presentation

**How does pneumococcal pneumonia present?** Fever and chills mark an abrupt onset.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c044-->
Chest pain is common, from pleural involvement.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c045-->
Altered alveolar blood gives the sputum its characteristic "rusty" look.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c046-->
Symptoms improve fast on modern therapy, but the X-ray lags several days behind.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c047-->

**And with other organisms?** Klebsiella pneumonia may be more dangerous, though its onset is gentler.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c048-->
Streptococcal pneumonia strikes abruptly: coughing blood, shortness of breath and fever, with frequent cyanosis.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c049-->
Legionella pneumonia often begins abruptly; abdominal pain, surprisingly, may accompany fever and muscle aches.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c050-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What does imaging show?** On imaging, pneumococcal pneumonia shows opaque lobes or segments, because exudate fills the alveoli.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c051-->
Legionella films are inconsistent, but a focal alveolar pattern, sometimes in both lungs, is typical.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c052-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What can follow pneumococcal pneumonia?**
- Infection spreads easily to the pleura, so pleuritis (often painful) is frequent.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c053-->
- Pleural effusion: frequent, but it usually goes away.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c054-->
- Empyema: an infected effusion becomes pus, and healing can leave extensive fibrosis.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c055-->
- Early bacteremia, with possible endocarditis or meningitis.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c056-->
- After splenectomy, this bacteremia is frequently fatal.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c057-->
- Organizing pneumonia (rare): granulation tissue plugs the alveoli.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c058-->
- Carnification (also rare): the lobe shrinks and hardens with fibrosis.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c059-->
- Lung abscess, which is unusual.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c060-->

**And with other organisms?** Klebsiella can cause a bronchopleural fistula.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c061-->
Tension pneumothorax can follow rupture of a staphylococcal pneumatocele into the pleura.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c062-->
Necrotizing pneumonia from anaerobes frequently ends in lung abscess.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c063-->
Infarction after pulmonary artery branch thrombosis is what produces lung gangrene.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c064-->
It is an emergency, treated by lung resection.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c065-->

## High-yield summary

- **Bronchopneumonia** forms patchy foci around airways, unlike **lobar pneumonia**, which solidifies a whole lobe.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c071-->
- **Pneumococcus** is the traditional agent of lobar pneumonia.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c072-->
- Stages: edema/congestion, **red hepatization**, **gray hepatization**, **resolution**.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c073-->
- **Klebsiella** gives mucoid lung, a bulging fissure and abscesses, mostly in alcoholic men.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c074-->
- ***S. aureus*** gives abscesses and pneumatoceles, after influenza or in cystic fibrosis.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c075-->
- **Aspiration** of oral anaerobes causes necrotizing pneumonia, abscess and gangrene.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c076-->
- Complications: **pleuritis**, **empyema**, **bacteremia**, **organizing pneumonia**.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c077-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is "community-acquired" a synonym of "lobar"?** No. It names the setting (outside hospital), though it is loosely used for lobar pneumonia.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c078-->

**Is gray hepatization the same as resolution?** No. The lung is still firm; resolution comes when the exudate is removed.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c079-->

**Is pneumococcus the only cause of lobar pneumonia?** No. Klebsiella is the only other frequent one.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c080-->

**Does streptococcal (*S. pyogenes*) pneumonia show hepatization?** No. The lungs are heavy with bloody edema instead.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c081-->

**Do the bacteria usually arrive by blood?** No. Most come from the throat by aspiration.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c082-->

**Is bronchopneumonia a disease of healthy people?** No. It mainly hits the terminally ill, and deaths from it are still common.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c083-->

## Mnemonics

**Early Reds Go Rest** gives the four stages in order: Edema, Red, Gray, Resolution.<!--c:bacterial-pneumonia-c084-->
