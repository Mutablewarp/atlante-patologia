## Definition and epidemiology

**What is cholelithiasis?** It is the presence of stones, one or many, in the gallbladder or the bile ducts outside the liver.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c001--> Cholesterol is the main component of about three quarters of stones in industrialized countries.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c002--> All others are pigment stones, built on calcium bilirubinate.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c003--> Many cause no symptoms.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c004--> Biliary colic follows a stone stuck in the cystic or common duct.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c005--> Before menopause, cholesterol stones are three times commoner in women than men.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c006-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which factors favor cholesterol stones?**
- Estrogen from pregnancy or oral contraceptives raises the risk.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c007-->
- Obesity is a major risk factor.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c008-->
- Family history, age and some ethnic groups, such as Native Americans, raise the risk.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c009-->
- Diabetes and similar high-cholesterol states favor stones, as does a rich diet.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c010-->
- Disturbed bile acid recycling, as in Crohn disease, favors stones.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c011-->
- Moderate alcohol intake lowers the risk.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c012-->

**What predisposes to pigment stones?**
- Chronic hemolysis and cirrhosis predispose to black stones, but most often no cause is found.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c013-->
- Bacterial cholangitis, chiefly with E. coli, almost always accompanies brown stones.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c014-->
- In Asia, biliary infestation by Ascaris lumbricoides or Clonorchis sinensis accounts for nearly all brown stones.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c015-->
- Western cases are rare and follow a bile duct catheter or chronic obstruction, as in [[primary-sclerosing-cholangitis|sclerosing cholangitis]].<!--c:cholelithiasis-c016-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does a cholesterol stone form?**
- Bile keeps water-insoluble cholesterol dissolved in micelles with bile acids and lecithin.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c017-->
- Lithogenic bile is oversaturated with cholesterol, through excess cholesterol or too few bile acids, so crystals form.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c018-->
- Excess gallbladder mucus speeds precipitation, and biliary proteins can nucleate crystals.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c019-->
- If the gallbladder contracts poorly, bile stagnates and forms sludge, a step toward stones.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c020-->
- Estrogens may explain the female excess: more cholesterol and fewer bile acids leave the liver.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c021-->
- Progesterone slows gallbladder emptying, so stasis gives crystals more time to form.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c022-->
- Obesity brings hyperinsulinism; insulin drives liver cholesterol synthesis, which may explain the extra cholesterol in bile.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c023-->

**And how do pigment stones form?**
- Calcium bilirubinate forms when hepatocytes release extra unconjugated bilirubin; a mucin-type core probably seeds it.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c024-->
- Brown stones are promoted when bacterial enzymes, notably β-glucuronidase, deconjugate bilirubin.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c025-->

## Gross morphology

**What do the stones look like?**
- Cholesterol stones, solitary or numerous, reach 4 cm and have a tan-yellow color and a round or faceted shape.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c026-->
- Black pigment stones are glassy, irregular and smaller than 1 cm.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c027-->
- Most gallstones sit in the gallbladder, but brown stones prefer the bile ducts; they are spongy and laminated.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c028-->
- Hydrops (mucocele) is a swollen gallbladder full of clear mucus, caused by a blocked cystic duct.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c029-->

## Clinical presentation

**When do gallstones hurt?**
- Stones that stay in the gallbladder can cause no trouble for many years.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c030-->
- Not every stone that enters the cystic duct causes severe colic, but many do.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c031-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How are gallstones found and treated?**
- Ultrasonography readily detects gallstones, even though most are not radiopaque.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c032-->
- The treatment of choice is laparoscopic cholecystectomy.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c033-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What can go wrong?**
- Fewer than 20% of people with silent stones develop pain or complications within 15 years.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c034-->
- Obstruction of the cystic or common bile duct by a stone underlies most gallstone complications.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c035-->
- Stone impaction in the cystic duct can trigger acute cholecystitis, and recurrences end in chronic cholecystitis.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c036-->
- Secondary infection can develop in a gallbladder distended by hydrops.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c037-->
- A stone in the common duct (choledocholithiasis) can cause pancreatitis, cholangitis or obstructive jaundice.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c038-->
- Gallstones top the causes of acute pancreatitis in non-drinkers.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c039-->
- A large stone passing into the small intestine can obstruct it (gallstone ileus).<!--c:cholelithiasis-c040-->
- In 90% of acute cholecystitis, gallstones are present.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c041-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What can be confused with gallstone disease?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[cholecystitis]] | Acute form: inflammation, usually after outlet blockage.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c042--> | Chronic form: ultrasonography shows a shrunken, thick-walled gallbladder with stones.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c043--> |
| Cholesterolosis | Strawberry gallbladder: lipid-laden macrophages under the mucosa dot it yellow; seldom symptomatic.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c044--> | Histology: foamy macrophages distend the mucosal folds.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c045--> |

## High-yield summary

- Industrialized countries have mostly **cholesterol** stones; **pigment** stones are relatively commoner in the tropics and Asia.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c046-->
- **Black** stones go with hemolysis and cirrhosis; **brown** stones go with bacterial cholangitis and sit in the ducts.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c047-->
- Cholesterol stone risk: **female sex, estrogen, obesity, age**, family history.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c048-->
- **Ultrasonography** is the detection test, because most stones are not radiopaque.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c049-->
- Complications: **hydrops, cholecystitis, choledocholithiasis, gallstone ileus, pancreatitis**.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c050-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Will a plain X-ray show gallstones?** Usually not: most are not radiopaque.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c051-->

**Do gallstones always cause symptoms?** No, they may stay silent for years.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c052-->

**Are brown and black stones the same?** No. Brown follow bile duct infection; black follow hemolysis or cirrhosis.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c053-->

## Mnemonics

**Black = Blood, Brown = Bugs:** black stones follow hemolysis, brown stones follow bacteria and worms.<!--c:cholelithiasis-c054-->
