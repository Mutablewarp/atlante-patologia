## Definition and epidemiology

**What is atherosclerosis?** The intima of large and medium arteries slowly turns into a plaque of lipid and fibrous tissue, infiltrated by inflammatory and muscle cells.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c001--> As atheromas (plaques) grow over decades, they narrow the lumen.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c002--> Over half of all deaths in the West trace back to its ischemic damage in heart, brain and limbs.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which factors raise the risk?** Hypertension and smoking are among the major risk factors.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c004--> Obesity and a strong family history add risk.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c005--> Because other risk factors build up with time, age and male sex probably act as markers of infarction risk.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c006--> Total cholesterol predicts risk poorly, as lipoproteins can be harmful or protective.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c007--> The more a person smokes, the worse the coronary and aortic disease.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c008--> Mildly raised plasma homocysteine adds risk independently.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c009--> A high lipoprotein(a) level makes myocardial infarction more likely.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c010--> Metabolic syndrome raises cardiovascular risk.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c011--> Mutations in the LDL receptor gene cause familial hypercholesterolemia, with premature atherosclerosis ([[familial-hypercholesterolemia]]).<!--c:atherosclerosis-c012--> Atherosclerosis is polygenic: many gene variants each contribute, and rare inherited lipid disorders are the exception.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does a plaque form?**
- Plaques start where wall shear is low and erratic, such as at branches and bends.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c014-->
- Injury of the endothelium (for instance from hypertension or diabetes) lets LDL bring lipid into the intima.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c015-->
- Macrophages get into the intima by sticking to endothelium where oxidized LDL has activated adhesion molecules.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c016-->
- Lipid-laden macrophages that die leave their fat behind.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c017-->
- Macrophage growth factors draw smooth muscle cells into the lesion.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c018-->
- Oxidized LDL may be a target of an autoimmune reaction, as the T cells inside plaques suggest.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c019-->
- Ischemic necrosis of the poorly nourished deep intima creates the necrotic core.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c020-->
- Signals from platelets, macrophages and the core itself make new vessels grow into the plaque.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c021-->
- Smooth muscle cells in the fibrous cap shield the lumen from the thrombogenic core.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c022-->

**Why can a plaque stay silent?** Until a plaque fills about half the lumen, outward wall remodeling keeps it open.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c023--> A small plaque can still rupture in this stage.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c024-->

## Gross morphology

**What do the lesions look like?** Children and adults both show fatty streaks: yellow, barely raised lipid stripes in the intima.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c025--> In adults the abdominal aorta is worst affected; children's streaks favor the thoracic aorta.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c026--> Whether a streak progresses depends on other factors, though it is a probable precursor.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c027--> Another possible forerunner sits where arteries branch: the intimal cell mass, a pale, lipid-free thickening of the wall.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c028--> A simple plaque is a yellowish, raised patch of the intima with crisp margins.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c029--> One-sided rather than ring-like plaques are common in coronary and cerebral arteries.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c030--> Late plaques coalesce into bigger lesions in muscular arteries.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c031--> The abdominal aorta and iliac arteries are the most frequent severe sites, followed by the proximal coronary arteries.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c032-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What is seen under the microscope?** A fatty-streak foam cell is a macrophage or a smooth muscle cell that has loaded up on fat, macrophages holding the most.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c033--> Early plaques lie in the intima beneath the endothelium and hardly reach the media.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c034--> The cap is mostly smooth muscle cells in connective tissue, with scattered immune cells and foam cells.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c035--> Foreign-body giant cells and cholesterol crystals may join the core debris.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c036--> Healthy coronaries rarely show new vessels sprouting from the vasa vasorum, but plaques have many.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c037--> Necrotic zones calcify, and so can other parts of the plaque.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c038-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at?**
- **How it is studied:** under the microscope, look for foam cells, a fibrous cap, a necrotic core and cholesterol clefts.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c039-->
- Old intraplaque hemorrhage leaves hemosiderin-laden macrophages.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c040-->
- The kidney is the usual site where cholesterol emboli show up as needle-shaped clefts in small arteries.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c041-->
- The reference pages name no special stain, IHC or molecular test.

## Clinical presentation

**What does atherosclerosis cause?** How long the disease has lasted, and the site and caliber of the vessel involved, decide the consequences.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c042--> A clot on a plaque can abruptly plug a muscular artery, infarcting its territory.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c043--> A kidney with a plaque-narrowed renal artery shrinks; chronic ischemia atrophies any organ whose supply falls slowly.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c044-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which tests matter?** LDL cholesterol is the lipid value used for treatment decisions.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c045--> Streptokinase or tissue plasminogen activator can dissolve certain occlusive thrombi.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c046--> The reference pages give no imaging criteria.

## Complications and prognosis

**Which complications matter?**
- **Rupture:** a clot forms in the lumen after the cap tears, because the core, rich in tissue factor, is strongly thrombogenic and meets the blood.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c047-->
- **Vulnerable plaque:** a thin fibrous cap over a large lipid core is more likely to rupture.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c048-->
- Inflammation and fewer smooth muscle cells are also linked to plaque rupture.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c049-->
- Stenosis below 50% is typical of plaques that go on to rupture.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c050-->
- **Thrombosis and embolism:** pieces of a mural thrombus can lodge farther downstream.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c051-->
- **Hemorrhage:** a plaque can swell and narrow the lumen further when its fragile new vessels rupture and bleed.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c052-->
- **Calcification:** it involves osteoblast-like differentiation of cells in the plaque.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c053-->
- **Aneurysm:** the abdominal aorta is the typical site, where a plaque reaching the media weakens the wall.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c054-->
- Such an aneurysm may rupture, or its thrombus may embolize.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c055-->
- **Cholesterol emboli:** an ulcerated plaque can shed atheromatous debris.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c056-->
- **Restenosis:** within 3 to 6 months of a successful balloon angioplasty, 30% to 40% of arteries narrow again.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c057-->
- Restenosis comes from intimal hyperplasia, where smooth muscle cells multiply and lay down matrix.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c058-->
- Antiproliferative drug-eluting stents curb this overgrowth.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c059-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What looks similar?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Fatty streak | Flat yellow intimal patch with foam cells, seen in children.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c060--> | H&E |
| Intimal cell mass | Lipid-free; smooth muscle and connective tissue form a pale thickening at arterial forks.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c061--> | H&E |
| [[aortic-aneurysm]] | Wall thinning from loss of elastin and collagen breakdown, which can complicate a plaque.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c062--> | H&E |
| Cholesterol embolus | Needle-like spaces in a small artery plugged by ulcerated-plaque debris.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c063--> | H&E |

## High-yield summary

- A **fatty streak** with **foam cells** may precede a plaque, which has a **necrotic core** and a **fibrous cap**.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c064-->
- **Oxidized LDL** and macrophages start the lesion.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c065-->
- **Remodeling** hides the plaque until it fills about half of the lumen.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c066-->
- The **abdominal aorta** is the most frequent severe site.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c067-->
- A **vulnerable plaque** has a thin cap and a large lipid core.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c068-->
- **Restenosis** after angioplasty comes from intimal hyperplasia.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c069-->
- **LDL cholesterol** guides treatment.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c070-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is a mild stenosis a safe plaque?** No. Rupture mostly occurs on plaques that narrow the lumen by less than 50%.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c071-->

**Is a fatty streak always the start of a plaque?** Not necessarily. Other factors decide whether it progresses.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c072-->

**Is total cholesterol the treatment target?** No. LDL cholesterol is used, since total includes protective lipoproteins.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c073-->

**Is the thoracic aorta the main adult site?** No. In adults the abdominal aorta is more affected.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c074-->

**Is infarction caused only by slow narrowing?** No. Clotting on a ruptured plaque can suddenly block the artery.<!--c:atherosclerosis-c075-->
