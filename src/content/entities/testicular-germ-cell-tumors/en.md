## Definition and epidemiology

**How are they sorted?** Most testicular tumors start from germ cells; sex cord stromal cells give rise to the next group.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c001--> Invasive germ cell tumors divide into seminoma and nonseminomatous tumors.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c002--> Teratoma, choriocarcinoma, yolk sac tumor and embryonal carcinoma make up the nonseminomatous group.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c003--> Seminoma is the most common single type.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c004--> Most are diagnosed in men aged 25 to 45 years.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c005--> Classification follows the cell of origin.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c006--> The key split is whether germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS) comes first: adult seminomas and nonseminomatous tumors have it, while spermatocytic tumor, prepubertal teratoma and infantile yolk sac tumor do not.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c007-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes them?** The cause is unknown; internal and external influences may raise susceptibility.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c008-->
- Incidence varies by geography and ancestry: high in Scandinavia, and higher in Americans of European than of African origin.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c009-->
- Moving to another country does not alter the pattern, unlike in prostate cancer.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c010-->
- Faulty gonad development, as in gonadal dysgenesis or cryptorchidism, raises the risk.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c012-->

**Why does cryptorchidism matter?** In cryptorchidism, a testis (or both) stays out of the scrotum from birth.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c014--> It raises the risk of testicular cancer many times over.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c015--> Risk falls as the testis sits lower: abdominal highest, inguinal intermediate, high scrotal lowest.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c016--> Even the normally descended opposite testis carries extra risk.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c017--> Surgically bringing the testis down into the scrotum (orchiopexy) leaves the cancer risk unchanged.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c018-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does it start?** Proposed first step: gonocytes (primordial germ cells) fail to ripen, under internal or environmental influence.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c019--> Next comes GCNIS: immature tumor germ cells kept inside the seminiferous tubules.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c020--> Most germ cell tumors after puberty gain 12p, the short arm of chromosome 12, wholly or in part.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c021--> A common form is i(12p), an isochromosome that adds 12p while losing 12q.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c022--> GCNIS carries i(12p) too.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c023--> Intratubular seminoma or embryonal carcinoma may form intermediate lesions between GCNIS and invasion.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c024--> Seminoma and embryonal carcinoma hold pluripotent stem cells that may form somatic or extraembryonic tissue.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c025--> The result can be a choriocarcinoma, yolk sac tumor, teratoma or mixed germ cell tumor.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c026-->

**Which tumors skip the in situ stage?** Spermatocytic tumor, prepubertal teratoma and infantile yolk sac tumor are thought to arise directly from an altered germ cell, with no in situ stage.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c027--> Yolk sac tumors, teratomas and midline germ cell tumors outside the gonad may come from primordial germ cells that strayed during fetal testis development.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c028-->

## Gross morphology

**What is seen on section?** On section, seminoma is an even tan, firm and clearly set off from the surrounding testis.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c029--> Larger seminomas can show necrosis or hemorrhage, though infrequently.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c030--> Some nonseminomatous tumors are solid, others partly cystic; size and shape vary.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c031--> Red areas are hemorrhage, yellow necrosis and white viable tumor.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c032-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What do cells look like?**
- Only some tubules are affected in GCNIS; those lack sperm and have a thickened basement membrane.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c033-->
- GCNIS cells are large, with prominent nucleoli.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c034-->
- All seminoma cells look alike: polygonal, and the nucleus sits centrally and is vesicular.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c036-->
- Seminoma looks like ovarian dysgerminoma.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c037-->
- Embryonal carcinoma shows crowded, overlapping nuclei, large and dark, with hardly any cytoplasm between them.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c038-->
- Embryonal carcinoma may form solid sheets, cords, glands or papillae.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c039-->
- Teratoma draws on all three germ layers, and adult-type and fetal-type tissue often sit side by side.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c040-->
- Epidermoid and dermoid cysts are teratoma variants containing only ectoderm.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c041-->
- Sarcoma, adenocarcinoma or squamous carcinoma can arise when a somatic tissue in a teratoma transforms.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c043-->
- Choriocarcinoma is a single-type tumor in which only malignant cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast are left.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c044-->
- Yolk sac tumor is diagnosed from glomeruloid Schiller-Duval bodies plus several growth patterns.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c045-->
- Mixed germ cell tumors combine more than one component, for example seminoma with a nonseminomatous tumor.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c046-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What is examined?**
- **How it is studied:** GCNIS is found on testicular biopsy, for example one done for infertility.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c047-->
- On routine H&E, look for sperm-free tubules lined by large, clear, atypical cells.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c048-->
- Immunohistochemistry: GCNIS cells carry OCT3/4 in the nucleus and PLAP and CD117 on the surface.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c049-->
- Nuclear OCT3/4 staining reliably identifies GCNIS.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c050-->
- PLAP and OCT3/4 are positive in both seminoma and embryonal carcinoma.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c051-->
- Embryonal carcinoma is CD30 and cytokeratin positive and CD117 negative.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c052-->
- Molecular: isochromosome 12p is the cytogenetic marker of these tumors.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c053-->
- Special stains and electron microscopy: the reference pages give no data.

## Clinical presentation

**How do they present?**
- A testicular mass is the usual presentation of nonseminomatous tumors.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c054-->
- Nonseminomatous tumors spread more readily and farther than seminomas, and they also grow faster.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c055-->
- The periaortic abdominal lymph nodes are the first site of metastasis.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c057-->
- Bilateral cryptorchidism always ends in azoospermia and infertility.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c058-->
- Yolk sac tumor accounts for most testicular neoplasms in early childhood.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c059-->
- In older prepubertal boys, testicular tumors are most often benign teratomas.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c060-->
- Only dysgenetic gonads give rise to seminoma before puberty.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c061-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it diagnosed?**
- Prophylactic orchiectomy is indicated when a testicular biopsy shows GCNIS.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c062-->
- Almost every invasive germ cell tumor has extensive GCNIS next to it.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c063-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens after GCNIS?** Invasion follows at a pace nobody can predict.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c065--> By 5 years, half of men with GCNIS have invasive cancer.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c066--> Before invasion starts, GCNIS may sit unchanged for years in infertile men with past cryptorchidism.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c067--> Surgery with chemotherapy can cure most testicular tumors.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c068-->

## Differential diagnosis

**How do the main groups differ?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[seminoma]] | Plentiful clear or pale pink cytoplasm; cells look alike, polygonal.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c069--> | Reacts with OCT3/4 and PLAP.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c070--> |

## High-yield summary

- **GCNIS** is the precursor of adult seminoma and nonseminomatous tumors.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c074-->
- **Spermatocytic tumor, prepubertal teratoma and infantile yolk sac tumor** arise without GCNIS.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c075-->
- **i(12p)** is the cytogenetic hallmark.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c076-->
- **Cryptorchidism** raises the risk, and orchiopexy does not remove it.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c077-->
- **Seminoma** is the most common type: look-alike polygonal cells with plentiful pale or clear cytoplasm.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c078-->
- **Nonseminomatous tumors** spread farther and more readily than seminoma and also grow faster.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c079-->
- **Mixed germ cell tumors** combine more than one component.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c080-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does every germ cell tumor start from GCNIS?** No. Spermatocytic tumor, prepubertal teratoma and infantile yolk sac tumor have no in situ phase.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c083-->

**Does orchiopexy remove the cancer risk?** No. The risk persists after the testis is moved into the scrotum.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c084-->

**Is only the undescended testis at risk?** No. The normally descended opposite testis is also at higher risk.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c085-->

**Is a mixed germ cell tumor a single cell type?** No. It combines more than one tumor type.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c086-->

**Do seminoma and embryonal carcinoma stain the same way?** Only partly. Both react with PLAP and OCT3/4; only embryonal carcinoma expresses CD30 and cytokeratins.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c087-->

**Is every teratoma harmless?** No. A somatic component can transform into a cancer.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c088-->

## Mnemonics

**POK:** **P**LAP, **O**CT3/4 and **K**IT (CD117) mark GCNIS cells.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c089-->

**SPY** (tumors with no GCNIS): **S**permatocytic tumor, **P**repubertal teratoma, **Y**olk sac tumor of infancy.<!--c:testicular-germ-cell-tumors-c090-->
