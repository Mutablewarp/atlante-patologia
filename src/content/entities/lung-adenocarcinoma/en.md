## Definition and epidemiology

**What is lung adenocarcinoma, and how common is it?** Adenocarcinoma is now the top lung cancer subtype in most countries, ahead of squamous cell carcinoma.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c001--> Of invasive lung malignancies in the United States, 36% are adenocarcinomas.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c002--> It is more frequent in women than in men.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c003--> The usual site is the lung periphery.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c004-->

<aside class="edition">The classification was revised recently. The old term "bronchioloalveolar carcinoma" was dropped: five distinct entities had been lumped under it.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c005--> Pathologists no longer use "mixed subtype adenocarcinoma" as a diagnosis.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c006--> Check which edition a textbook or report follows before comparing terms and numbers.</aside>

## Etiology and risk factors

**Who gets it?** Among nonsmokers, no lung cancer type is more common.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c007--> Scars beside the tumor are a desmoplastic reaction to it, not old tuberculosis or infarct scars from which it grows, as once believed.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c008--> The reference pages used for this card do not cover the driver genes (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, KRAS, BRAF) or PD-L1.

## Pathogenesis

**How does it develop?** Atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH) is a lesion that probably gives rise to adenocarcinoma.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c009--> In AAH, atypical epithelial cells proliferate along the alveolar septa, forming a well-demarcated lesion that is usually under 5 mm.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c010--> Invasive adenocarcinoma is thought to follow AAH and AIS, a stepwise route that parallels the colonic adenoma-carcinoma sequence.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c011--> More advanced lesions carry more accumulated mutations, which supports the sequence.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c012--> Whether every AAH progresses, and whether every adenocarcinoma follows this route, is not settled.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c013-->

## Gross morphology

**What does it look like to the naked eye?** The pleura over a peripheral tumor may pucker, because of the pleural fibrosis and subpleural scars that often accompany it.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c014--> Some invasive adenocarcinomas grow large enough to replace a whole lobe, but most are irregular masses of 2 to 5 cm.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c015--> The cut surface of a nonmucinous tumor is grayish white.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c016--> Depending on how much mucin it makes, a mucinous tumor looks shiny or jelly-like.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c017--> In the central lung, a tumor may invade bronchial cartilage, growing mostly within the bronchial lumen.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c018--> AIS and MIA measure 3 cm or less, and their tan, vague outline can blend into adjacent lung.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c019-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Which growth patterns does the pathologist see?**
- AIS: lepidic growth, meaning the tumor cells spread only over alveolar walls that were already there, without invasion.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c020-->
- Minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA): a lepidic tumor with invasive foci of 5 mm or less, no necrosis and no pleural or lymphovascular invasion.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c021-->
- Mucin is usually absent in AIS and MIA, and their cells are club cells, type II pneumocytes or both.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c022-->
- Cuboidal tumor cells line the alveolar walls when the tumor is nonmucinous.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c023-->
- In the rare mucinous AIS and MIA, the columnar cells carry mucin in the apical cytoplasm.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c024-->
- With a mucinous tumor, a metastasis from elsewhere must be ruled out.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c025-->

**And in invasive tumors?** Only about 5% of adenocarcinomas are AIS or MIA; most are invasive.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c026--> A typical invasive tumor is a mix of several growth patterns, not a uniform lesion.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c027--> Classification depends on which pattern predominates: lepidic, acinar, papillary, micropapillary or solid.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c028--> No category of invasive adenocarcinoma is commoner than the acinar-predominant one.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c029-->
- Lepidic-predominant: lepidic growth is the main pattern, with an invasive area.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c030-->
- Acinar: glands of regular shape, formed by cuboidal or columnar cells.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c031-->
- Papillary: fibrovascular cores covered by one layer of tumor cells.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c032-->
- Micropapillary: lacking any fibrovascular core, the small tufts of tumor cells can look suspended in spaces.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c033-->
- Solid: a poorly differentiated tumor that shows mucin or TTF-1.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c034-->
- Invasive mucinous: the apical cytoplasm of tall columnar cells holds mucin.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c035-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** in a fully removed tumor, the dominant growth pattern is what predicts prognosis.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c036-->
- On histology, decide which growth pattern predominates, because classification rests on it.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c037-->
- Immunohistochemistry: TTF-1, a pneumocyte marker, supports adenocarcinoma in a solid tumor.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c038-->
- Mucin stains: mucicarmine, or PAS after diastase digestion, can show mucin in a solid tumor.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c039-->
- Biopsy sampling, electron microscopy and molecular tests (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, KRAS, BRAF, PD-L1) are not covered by the reference pages used here.

## Clinical presentation

**Who is typically affected, and where is the tumor?** Women are affected more often than men, and the tumor is the leading type in nonsmokers.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c040--> The reference pages used here do not describe symptoms.

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What does imaging show, and how is it told from large cell carcinoma?** On imaging, either AIS or MIA can present as several nodules, or as one peripheral nodule that looks like ground glass.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c041--> A small solid component may be seen in MIA; AIS, by contrast, is pure ground glass.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c042--> To tell a solid adenocarcinoma from large cell carcinoma, look for TTF-1 expression, mucin on special stains, or both.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c043-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How good is the outcome?** The predominant pattern predicts outcome once the tumor has been completely resected.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c044--> Survival at 5 years is 100% once AIS has been resected.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c045--> MIA has the same favorable prognosis.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c046-->

<aside class="edition">These figures come from one edition of the reference and its classification; confirm them in a current source.</aside>

- Stage I, lepidic-predominant: 5-year disease-free survival above 90%.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c047-->
- Stage I, acinar or papillary: 80% to 90%.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c048-->
- Stage I, solid or micropapillary: 60% to 80%, the worst group.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c049-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Atypical adenomatous hyperplasia | Small, well-demarcated precursor lesion, usually under 5 mm.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c050--> | H&E |
| Adenocarcinoma in situ | Purely lepidic growth, no invasion.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c051--> | H&E |
| Minimally invasive adenocarcinoma | Lepidic growth with an invasive focus of 5 mm or less and no necrosis.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c052--> | H&E, measuring the invasive focus |
| Large cell carcinoma | Negative for TTF-1, p40 and mucin, and without squamous or glandular differentiation: a poorly differentiated carcinoma diagnosed only by exclusion.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c053--> | TTF-1, p40, mucin stains |
| [[lung-metastases\|Metastasis from another site]] | Must be excluded when the tumor is mucinous.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c054--> | Search for another primary |

## High-yield summary

- **AAH** is the probable precursor of lung adenocarcinoma.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c055-->
- **AIS** is purely **lepidic** and non-invasive.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c056-->
- **MIA** has an invasive focus of 5 mm or less and the same favorable prognosis as AIS.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c057-->
- Invasive tumors are named by their **predominant pattern**: lepidic, acinar, papillary, micropapillary, solid.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c058-->
- **Acinar** is the most common predominant pattern.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c059-->
- In stage I, **lepidic** has the best outlook and **solid** and **micropapillary** the worst.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c060-->
- **TTF-1** and mucin stains separate solid adenocarcinoma from large cell carcinoma.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c061-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is "bronchioloalveolar carcinoma" still a valid diagnosis?** No. Five distinct entities were hidden under that name, so it was dropped.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c062-->

**Do old scars cause lung adenocarcinoma?** No. Scars beside the tumor are a desmoplastic response to it.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c063-->

**Is it a cancer of smokers only?** No. In nonsmokers it is the leading type.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c064-->

**Does a small invasive focus in a lepidic tumor make the prognosis worse?** Not in MIA, which has the same favorable prognosis as AIS.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c065-->

**Is a poorly differentiated solid tumor that makes mucin a large cell carcinoma?** No. TTF-1 expression or mucin makes it a solid adenocarcinoma.<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c066-->

## Mnemonics

**LAPSM** gives the patterns from best to worst outlook: "Lepidic, Acinar, Papillary, then Solid and Micropapillary: Lungs Are Perhaps Saved More."<!--c:lung-adenocarcinoma-c067-->
