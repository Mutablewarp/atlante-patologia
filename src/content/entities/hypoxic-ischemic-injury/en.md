## Definition and epidemiology

Ischemia is a fall or stop in blood flow to a tissue, so its cells receive too little oxygen to work normally.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c001--> Ischemia deprives cells not only of oxygen but also of nutrients such as glucose, and waste such as CO2 is no longer carried away.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c002--> Hypoxic–ischemic injury is one continuous spectrum: brief deprivation causes reversible damage, while prolonged deprivation ends in irreversible injury and cell death.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c003--> Of all forms of necrosis, ischemic necrosis matters most in practice: it is the lesion behind both myocardial infarction and stroke, which combined top the list of causes of death in Western countries.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

This card centres on ischemia. Most often the cause is blocked arterial inflow, for example occlusion of a coronary artery.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c005--> Occlusion of a vein, not only of an artery, can also cause an infarct, and the resulting infarct is red (hemorrhagic).<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c071--> In the heart, ischemia can also arise without a fixed occlusion, when blood supply falls short of oxygen demand for a long time, as in hypotension, severe anemia, arrhythmias or coronary spasm.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c072-->

- **High-demand, low-reserve cells:** cardiac myocytes and neurons spend a lot of energy and keep little in store, so they are especially vulnerable.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c006-->
- **Duration of ischemia:** the longer the interruption, the more likely the injury becomes lethal.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c007-->

## Pathogenesis

Cells keep steep ion gradients across their membranes: Na+ and Ca2+ are far more concentrated outside, K+ inside.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c008--> Holding these gradients needs pumps, exchangers, an intact plasma membrane and a steady supply of ATP.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c009--> Ischemia attacks the ATP supply first. Some of the following steps run in parallel rather than strictly in sequence.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c010-->

1. **ATP falls.** Without oxygen, mitochondria cannot oxidize pyruvate, so aerobic ATP production stops.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c011-->
2. **Acidosis.** Pyruvate is instead reduced to lactate by anaerobic glycolysis, and lactate lowers cytosolic pH.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c012--> Glycogen stores are consumed in the process.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c013-->
3. **Sodium loading.** With little ATP the Na+/K+ pump falters and Na+ builds up inside the cell.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c014--> Acidosis then switches on the normally silent Na+/H+ exchanger, which expels H+ but brings in still more Na+.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c015--> Water follows the retained Na+, producing hydropic swelling.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c016-->
4. **Calcium overload.** High intracellular Na+ drives the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger to import Ca2+, and the ATP-dependent calcium pump can no longer remove it.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c017--> Normal cytosolic Ca2+ is about 10^-7 M against roughly 10^-3 M outside, so any breach lets calcium flood in.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c018-->
5. **Membrane breakdown.** Calcium activates phospholipase A2 (PLA2), which degrades membrane phospholipids into free fatty acids and lysophospholipids.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c019--> Lysophospholipids act like detergents on membranes and further disturb ion channels and pumps.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c020--> Calcium-activated proteases cut the links between cytoskeleton and plasma membrane, so the membrane blebs.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c021-->
6. **Mitochondrial damage.** A failing electron transport chain makes more reactive oxygen species (ROS) while antioxidant defenses are impaired.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c022--> ROS peroxidize cardiolipin, a mitochondria-specific phospholipid rich in unsaturated fatty acids, which further cripples ATP production.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c023--> Ischemia holds the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) open; cytochrome c leaks out, ATP synthesis drops further and apoptosis may be triggered.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c024-->
7. **Death.** It comes once the cell has lost the ability to keep its own metabolism running.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c025-->

**Point of no return.** Pathologists cannot pinpoint the exact moment when a reversible injury turns irreversible.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c026--> In prolonged ischemia, membrane damage becomes severe enough that restoring blood flow no longer rescues the cell.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c027--> Once the plasma membrane is breached, calcium pours in down its steep gradient, and this flood is a decisive step in ischemic death.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c028-->

## Gross morphology

The tissue-level result is an infarct.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c029--> In the heart, an infarct from persistent occlusion is pale and becomes grossly visible only after about 12 hours, whereas a reperfused infarct is hemorrhagic.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c030-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Reversible phase (light and electron microscopy).**

- Hydropic swelling: an enlarged cell with pale cytoplasm and a normally placed nucleus.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c031-->
- Dilated endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes detached from the rough ER.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c032-->
- Swollen mitochondria with a less dense matrix and fainter cristae, typical of ischemia, which recover if the cell survives.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c033-->
- Plasma membrane blebs, which can detach without killing the cell.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c034-->
- Separation of fibrillar and granular components of the nucleolus.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c035-->

**Irreversible phase (coagulative necrosis).**

- Cytoplasm turns more eosinophilic because RNA, which binds hematoxylin, is rapidly degraded.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c036-->
- Nuclear changes: **pyknosis** (small, dense nucleus), **karyorrhexis** (fragmentation) and **karyolysis** (fading chromatin).<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c037-->
- On electron microscopy, swollen mitochondria now also contain calcium deposits, and the plasma membrane shows breaks.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c038-->
- Cell outlines are preserved at first, before enzymatic lysis.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c039-->
- In reperfused myocardium, massive Ca2+ entry produces **contraction band necrosis**: thick transverse eosinophilic bands of hypercontracted sarcomeres.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c040-->

## Clinical presentation

- **Heart:** transient ischemia causes angina pectoris; if it persists, myocytes die and a myocardial infarction results.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c041-->
- **Brain:** occlusion of a cerebral artery causes coagulative necrosis that rapidly liquefies, sometimes leaving a lifelong cyst.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c042-->
- The contents spilled by dead cells, including enzymes and inflammatory mediators, set off inflammation and can injure surviving cells nearby.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c043-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

When myocytes die, their proteins escape into the blood: an increase in serum cardiac troponin (I or T) or MB-CK signals that heart muscle has died.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c044--> Cardiac troponin is the most widely used marker because it is highly specific for heart muscle.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c045--> A diagnosis of myocardial infarction needs the troponin level to rise and/or fall in step with a matching clinical event.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c073-->

## Complications and prognosis

Outcome depends on how long ischemia lasts.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c046--> Myocardium deprived of flow for less than 30 minutes can recover fully once perfusion returns.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c047-->

- **Short ischemia:** reperfusion restores normal structure and function.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c048-->
- **Intermediate ischemia:** cells are still alive but die during reperfusion ([[ischemia-reperfusion-injury]]); in the heart this can account for up to half of final infarct size.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c049-->
- **Long ischemia:** cells die during the ischemic period itself, whatever happens next.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c050-->

**Ischemic preconditioning:** repeated short, sublethal episodes (for example recurrent angina) make tissue more resistant, limiting necrosis after a later prolonged episode.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c051--> One mediator is HIF-1α, the transcription factor that drives the response to low oxygen: the genes it induces protect energy stores, restrain calcium build-up and dampen oxidant formation.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c052-->

| [[coagulative-necrosis]] | Irreversible end point: eosinophilic cytoplasm with pyknosis, karyorrhexis or karyolysis.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c054--> | Nuclear changes on H&E; serum markers in the heart |
| [[apoptosis]] | Single cells amid viable tissue, nuclear condensation within an intact membrane, no inflammation.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c055--> | Pattern of single-cell death without inflammation |
| [[ischemia-reperfusion-injury]] | Injury added when oxygen returns, driven by a burst of ROS and worsened calcium overload.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c056--> | Clinical setting of restored flow |

## High-yield summary

- First event: **ATP depletion** from loss of oxidative phosphorylation.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c057-->
- **Anaerobic glycolysis** produces lactate, so the cytosol becomes **acidic**.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c058-->
- **Na+/K+ pump** failure plus the **Na+/H+ exchanger** load the cell with Na+, so water enters (**hydropic swelling**).<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c059-->
- Na+ excess drives the **Na+/Ca2+ exchanger**, causing **calcium overload**.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c060-->
- Ca2+ activates **phospholipase A2** and **proteases**, which destroy membranes and cytoskeletal anchors.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c061-->
- Mitochondria: **ROS**, **cardiolipin peroxidation**, sustained **MPTP** opening, **cytochrome c** release.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c062-->
- Irreversibility is signalled by **severe membrane damage** and **massive calcium entry**.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c063-->
- Morphologic end point: **coagulative necrosis** (followed by **liquefaction** in the brain).<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c064-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- Cell swelling, mitochondrial swelling and membrane blebs are still reversible; nuclear changes and mitochondrial calcification signal death.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c065-->
- Reperfusion is not always harmless: it saves reversibly injured cells but can kill others.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c066-->
- The Na+/H+ exchanger is not a cause of acidosis; it is a defense against it that worsens the Na+ load.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c067-->
- The eosinophilia of necrotic cells reflects loss of RNA (blue staining), not a gain of red-staining material.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c068-->
- Restoring flow cannot rescue a cell whose membranes are already severely damaged: reperfusion helps only before the point of no return.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c069-->

## Mnemonics

**ALICE** (original), the ischemic cascade in order: **A**TP falls, **L**actate and acidosis, **I**ons shift (Na+ in, water follows), **C**alcium overload, **E**nzymes (PLA2 and proteases) break membranes.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c070-->
