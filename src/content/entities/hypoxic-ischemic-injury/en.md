## Definition and epidemiology

Ischemia is a fall or stop in blood flow to a tissue, so its cells receive too little oxygen to work normally.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c001--> When flow stops, wastes such as CO2 are no longer removed, and the cells lose glucose and other fuels as well as oxygen.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c002--> Hypoxic–ischemic injury is one continuous spectrum: brief deprivation causes reversible damage, while prolonged deprivation ends in irreversible injury and cell death.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c003--> Of all forms of necrosis, ischemic necrosis matters most in practice: it is the lesion behind both myocardial infarction and stroke, which combined top the list of causes of death in Western countries.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

This card centres on ischemia. Most often the cause is blocked arterial inflow, for example occlusion of a coronary artery.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c005--> Occlusion of a vein, not only of an artery, can also cause an infarct, and the resulting infarct is red (hemorrhagic).<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c071--> In the heart, ischemia can also arise without a fixed occlusion, when for a long time flow stays below what the muscle needs, as during coronary spasm or a severe fall in blood pressure.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c072--> Oxygen can also run short while blood flow is normal (hypoxia without ischemia), when the blood carries less of it: in anemia, or in carbon monoxide poisoning, where CO displaces oxygen from hemoglobin.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c074-->

- **High-demand, low-reserve cells:** cardiac myocytes and neurons spend a lot of energy and keep little in store, so they are especially vulnerable.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c006-->
- **Duration of ischemia:** the longer the interruption, the more likely the injury becomes lethal.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c007-->

## Pathogenesis

Cells keep steep ion gradients across their membranes: Na+ and Ca2+ are far more concentrated outside, K+ inside.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c008--> Keeping these gradients requires plenty of ATP and a plasma membrane that is still whole.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c009--> Ischemia attacks the ATP supply first.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c075--> Some of the following steps run in parallel rather than strictly in sequence.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c010-->

1. **ATP falls.** Without oxygen, mitochondria cannot oxidize pyruvate, so aerobic ATP production stops.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c011-->
2. **Acidosis.** Pyruvate is instead reduced to lactate by anaerobic glycolysis, and lactate lowers cytosolic pH.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c012--> Glycogen stores are consumed in the process.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c013-->
3. **Sodium loading.** With little ATP the Na+/K+ pump falters and Na+ builds up inside the cell.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c014--> Acidosis then switches on the normally silent Na+/H+ exchanger, which expels H+ but brings in still more Na+.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c015--> Water follows the retained Na+, producing hydropic swelling.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c016-->
4. **Calcium overload.** High intracellular Na+ drives the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger to import Ca2+, and the ATP-dependent calcium pump can no longer remove it.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c017--> Normal cytosolic Ca2+ is about 10^-7 M against roughly 10^-3 M outside, so any breach lets calcium flood in.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c018-->
5. **Membrane breakdown.** Calcium activates phospholipase A2 (PLA2), which degrades membrane phospholipids into free fatty acids and lysophospholipids.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c019--> Lysophospholipids act like detergents on membranes and further disturb ion channels and pumps.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c020--> Calcium-activated proteases cut the links between cytoskeleton and plasma membrane, so the membrane blebs.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c021-->
6. **Mitochondrial damage.** A failing electron transport chain makes more reactive oxygen species (ROS) while antioxidant defenses are impaired.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c022--> Rich in unsaturated fatty acids and found only in mitochondria, cardiolipin is an easy target for ROS; once it is peroxidized, the electron transport chain makes even less ATP.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c023--> ATP falls further, and apoptosis may begin, once ischemia holds the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) open: cytochrome c is then lost from the electron transport chain.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c024-->
7. **Death.** It comes once the cell has lost the ability to keep its own metabolism running.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c025-->

**Point of no return.** Pathologists cannot pinpoint the exact moment when a reversible injury turns irreversible.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c026--> In prolonged ischemia, membrane damage becomes severe enough that restoring blood flow no longer rescues the cell.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c027--> Once the plasma membrane is breached, calcium pours in down its steep gradient, and this flood is a decisive step in ischemic death.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c028-->

## Gross morphology

The tissue-level result is an infarct.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c029--> In the heart, an infarct from persistent occlusion is pale and becomes grossly visible only after about 12 hours, whereas a reperfused infarct is hemorrhagic.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c030-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Reversible phase (light and electron microscopy).**

- Hydropic swelling: an enlarged cell with pale cytoplasm and a normally placed nucleus.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c031-->
- Dilated endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes detached from the rough ER.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c032-->
- Swollen mitochondria, a typical change of ischemia, with a pale matrix; they return to normal if the cell survives.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c033-->
- Plasma membrane blebs, which can detach without killing the cell.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c034-->
- Separation of fibrillar and granular components of the nucleolus.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c035-->

**Irreversible phase (coagulative necrosis).**

- Cytoplasm turns more eosinophilic because RNA, which binds hematoxylin, is rapidly degraded.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c036-->
- Nuclear changes: **pyknosis** (small, dense nucleus), **karyorrhexis** (fragmentation) and **karyolysis** (fading chromatin).<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c037-->
- On electron microscopy, swollen mitochondria now also contain calcium deposits, and the plasma membrane shows breaks.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c038-->
- Cell outlines are preserved at first, before enzymatic lysis.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c039-->
- In reperfused myocardium, a flood of Ca2+ makes the sarcomeres hypercontract, and the dying myocytes show **contraction band necrosis**: dense eosinophilic bands running across the fiber.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c040-->
- In the brain, nothing shows if the patient survived only minutes to a few hours; after about 6–24 hours the dying neurons have become **red neurons**, with an intensely eosinophilic cytoplasm and a pyknotic nucleus.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c076-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Autopsy, gross examination:** an early infarct cannot yet be seen with the naked eye (see Gross morphology); staining slices of the heart with triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) can then help, since infarcted muscle has lost the dehydrogenase activity that the dye reveals, although the test misses some infarcts that histology then finds.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c077--> Living muscle stains bright red, but the reaction fades as the time since death grows; in one forensic autopsy series the test was useful only within about a day and a half after death, and it seldom showed the infarct when the patient had died less than about 9 hours after the onset.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c078-->
- **H&E:** early on, look for **wavy fibers**: the living muscle around the infarct keeps contracting and pulls on the ischemic myocytes, which can no longer contract, until they are stretched into a wavy course.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c079--> From about 12–18 hours the dead zone turns eosinophilic, yet the nuclei of the dead myocytes disappear completely only after several days.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c080--> How the cells at the border date an infarct is explained under [[coagulative-necrosis]].
- **Electron microscopy:** it alone shows the earliest stage, known in most detail from animal experiments: for up to about 20–30 minutes after flow stops completely, the myocyte can still recover, its mitochondria only mildly swollen and its sarcoplasm edematous.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c081--> Amorphous densities in the matrix of markedly swollen mitochondria mark lethal injury, reached after about 30–60 minutes.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c082-->
- **Trichrome stain for collagen** (for example Masson's trichrome): collagen turns blue-green, so the scar of a healed infarct stands out clearly.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c083-->
- **IHC and molecular tests:** the reference text used for this card describes none for recognizing ischemic injury; serum markers are covered under Diagnosis.

## Clinical presentation

- **Heart:** transient ischemia causes angina pectoris; if it persists, myocytes die and a myocardial infarction results.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c041-->
- **Brain:** occlusion of a cerebral artery causes coagulative necrosis that rapidly liquefies, sometimes leaving a lifelong cyst.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c042-->
- Surviving cells nearby can be harmed too, both by the inflammation that necrosis sets off and by the enzymes and other mediators that dead cells release.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c043-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

When myocytes die, their proteins escape into the blood: an increase in serum cardiac troponin (I or T) or MB-CK signals that heart muscle has died.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c044--> Cardiac troponin is the most widely used marker, and it points very specifically to damage of heart muscle.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c045--> The troponin level must also change, rising and/or falling, over a time course that fits a compatible clinical event: only then can myocardial infarction be diagnosed.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c073-->

## Complications and prognosis

Outcome depends on how long ischemia lasts.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c046--> Myocardium deprived of flow for less than 30 minutes can recover fully once perfusion returns.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c047-->

- **Short ischemia:** reperfusion restores normal structure and function.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c048-->
- **Intermediate ischemia:** cells are still alive but die during reperfusion ([[ischemia-reperfusion-injury]]); as much as half of a heart's eventual infarct may come from this late death.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c049-->
- **Long ischemia:** cells die during the ischemic period itself, whatever happens next.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c050-->

**Chronic, partial ischemia:** when an artery is only narrowed and the tissue stays underperfused for a long time, the usual result is atrophy rather than necrosis (see [[atrophy]]); in the heart, interstitial fibrosis develops as well.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c084-->

**Ischemic preconditioning:** repeated short, sublethal episodes (for example recurrent angina) make tissue more resistant, limiting necrosis after a later prolonged episode.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c051--> One mediator is HIF-1α, the transcription factor that drives the response to low oxygen: the genes it switches on help the cell keep its ATP and hold down calcium build-up, so that it better survives a later episode.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c052-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[hydropic-swelling]] | Reversible stage of the same process; nucleus intact.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c053--> | Normal nucleus on H&E |
| [[coagulative-necrosis]] | Irreversible end point: eosinophilic cytoplasm with pyknosis, karyorrhexis or karyolysis.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c054--> | Nuclear changes on H&E; serum markers in the heart |
| [[apoptosis]] | Single cells amid viable tissue, nuclear condensation within an intact membrane, no inflammation.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c055--> | Pattern of single-cell death without inflammation |
| [[ischemia-reperfusion-injury]] | Injury added when oxygen returns, driven by a burst of ROS and worsened calcium overload.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c056--> | Clinical setting of restored flow |

## High-yield summary

- First event: **ATP depletion** from loss of oxidative phosphorylation.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c057-->
- **Anaerobic glycolysis** produces lactate, so the cytosol becomes **acidic**.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c058-->
- **Na+/K+ pump** failure plus the **Na+/H+ exchanger** load the cell with Na+, so water enters (**hydropic swelling**).<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c059-->
- Na+ excess drives the **Na+/Ca2+ exchanger**, causing **calcium overload**.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c060-->
- Ca2+ activates **phospholipase A2** and **proteases**, which destroy membranes and cytoskeletal anchors.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c061-->
- Mitochondria: **ROS**, **cardiolipin peroxidation**, sustained **MPTP** opening, **cytochrome c** release.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c062-->
- Irreversibility is signalled by **severe membrane damage** and **massive calcium entry**.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c063-->
- Morphologic end point: **coagulative necrosis** (followed by **liquefaction** in the brain).<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c064-->
- Myocardial ischemia shows first on **electron microscopy** (minutes), then as **eosinophilia** on H&E (from about 12–18 hours) and as gross **pallor** (by about 24 hours).<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c085-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- Cell swelling, mitochondrial swelling and membrane blebs are still reversible; nuclear changes and mitochondrial calcification signal death.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c065-->
- Reperfusion is not always harmless: it saves reversibly injured cells but can kill others.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c066-->
- The Na+/H+ exchanger is not a cause of acidosis; it is a defense against it that worsens the Na+ load.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c067-->
- The eosinophilia of necrotic cells reflects loss of RNA (blue staining), not a gain of red-staining material.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c068-->
- Restoring flow cannot rescue a cell whose membranes are already severely damaged: reperfusion helps only before the point of no return.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c069-->
- A heart that looks normal at autopsy does not rule out an infarct: in the first hours the lesion can escape both the naked eye and routine H&E.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c086-->

## Mnemonics

**ALICE** (original), the ischemic cascade in order: **A**TP falls, **L**actate and acidosis, **I**ons shift (Na+ in, water follows), **C**alcium overload, **E**nzymes (PLA2 and proteases) break membranes.<!--c:hypoxic-ischemic-injury-c070-->
