## Definition and epidemiology

**What is infective endocarditis?** Bacteria cause most cases of this infection of the inner lining of the heart, which usually involves a valve.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c001--> Fungi, chlamydiae and rickettsiae are less common causes.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c002-->

**How was it classified, and how is it classified now?** Before antibiotics, nearly every patient with bacterial endocarditis died, because no cure existed.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c003--> Aggressive organisms such as *Staphylococcus aureus* caused the acute form, which struck normal valves.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c004--> Weaker organisms such as viridans streptococci caused the subacute form, typically on valves already scarred by rheumatic disease.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c005--> Today the classification rests on the anatomic site and the organism, not on the tempo.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c006-->

**Who gets it?** In children, a congenital malformation is now the most common predisposing condition, and most affected children have some underlying heart lesion.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c007--> Most adults show no known cardiac predisposition.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c008--> Among adults today, the underlying conditions seen most often are congenital heart disease and mitral valve prolapse.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c009--> Rheumatic disease, once the leading setting in adults, has become uncommon.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c010-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which conditions and procedures open the door?**
- The mechanism is unclear: *S. aureus* can infect valves that look healthy.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c011-->
- When people who inject drugs develop endocarditis, the bacteria most often come from the skin.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c012-->
- *S. aureus* is the leading organism in that group.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c013-->
- The tricuspid valve is often the one infected in drug users.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c014-->
- Prosthetic valves can become infected, and staphylococci are the commonest culprits.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c015-->
- Endocarditis can also be caused by medical care itself, when a catheter is left in a blood vessel.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c016-->
- Bacteria briefly in the blood, from dental work or elsewhere, can seed a valve.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c017-->
- Preventive antibiotics are advised for patients more likely to develop endocarditis.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c018-->
- Valve changes of aging, such as calcification, make older people increasingly susceptible.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c019-->
- Endocarditis may be commoner in diabetes and pregnancy.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c020-->
- Bicuspid aortic valves are increasingly seen as predisposing.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c021-->

## Pathogenesis

**Why does a valve become colonized?** The core problem is disturbed blood flow through a damaged valve.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c022--> The inflow side of the valve is exposed to strong pulsatile shear, and that is where the lesions arise.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c023--> Squeezed through a narrow opening, blood forms a jet and eddies that bare the valve surface.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c024--> Small sterile vegetations, easy targets for bacteria, build up where platelets and fibrin settle on the bare areas.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c025--> Inflammatory cells are drawn in by E-selectin, ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, made more by nearby endothelium.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c026--> Dental manipulation is one way microorganisms enter the bloodstream, and they can then lodge within the vegetations.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c027--> Protected there, they multiply to very high densities.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c028--> Bacterial matrix metalloproteinases start to break the valve down, which makes adjacent vegetations more likely.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c029--> Fibronectin binds bacterial surfaces and so helps matrix and cells stick to them.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c030--> Adherence is also promoted by extracellular polysaccharides, which some microorganisms produce.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c031-->

## Gross morphology

**Where are the vegetations and what do they look like?** The mitral valve, the aortic valve or both are most often affected, so the disease is mainly left-sided.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c032--> Vegetations lie on the upstream face: atrial on the mitral and tricuspid valves, ventricular on the aortic and pulmonary valves.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c033--> The contact points of the leaflets or cusps are frequent locations.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c034--> Vegetations range from thin surface coatings to bulky masses.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c035--> Destructive vegetations can erode the edge of a mitral leaflet.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c036--> Regurgitation can result when the damage perforates a leaflet.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c037--> The valve ring or the chordae tendineae may be reached when the infection spreads locally.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c038-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What is inside a vegetation?** A platelet-fibrin thrombus encloses microorganisms and cellular debris.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c039--> Beneath the vegetation, the valve itself shows edema and inflammation.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c040-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?**
- At first the symptoms are vague, for example low-grade fever and fatigue.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c041-->
- A murmur is present in nearly every patient, and it often changes over time.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c042-->
- Longer-lasting disease often shows splenomegaly and clubbed fingers.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c043-->
- In about one-third of cases, a systemic embolus is recognized during the illness.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c044-->
- Infected emboli lodge in organs such as the brain and spleen and produce infarcts or abscesses.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c045-->
- Brain abscesses and mycotic aneurysms in brain arteries may develop.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c046-->
- Septic emboli can reach the lungs when drug users develop tricuspid valve endocarditis.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c047-->

**Which complications are immune-mediated?** Focal segmental glomerulonephritis can complicate endocarditis.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c048--> Immune complexes lodge in the glomeruli and cause it.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c049--> "Flea-bitten kidneys" is the name for the patchy, hemorrhagic look of the affected organs.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c050-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it diagnosed?** The source pages give no formal diagnostic criteria, so none are stated here. Some patients have negative blood cultures.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c051-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What is the outlook?** Treatment with antibacterial drugs reduces both illness and death.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c052--> The infection stage at treatment, and the organism, partly decide outcome.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c053--> *S. aureus* endocarditis kills about one patient in four.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c054--> When infection is still active, operating to replace the ruined valve is dangerous.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c055--> Valve destruction is the usual cause of heart failure, the most frequent serious complication.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c056--> When heart failure develops, the outlook is poor.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c057--> Coronary emboli causing infarction, and myocardial abscesses, can also contribute to it.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c058-->

## High-yield summary

- **Acute** endocarditis: virulent organisms such as ***S. aureus*** on normal valves.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c064-->
- **Subacute** endocarditis: less virulent organisms such as **viridans streptococci** on abnormal valves.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c065-->
- **Vegetations** hold organisms within a platelet-fibrin thrombus and sit on the upstream side of the valve.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c066-->
- Pathogenesis starts with **endothelial damage** from turbulent flow, then sterile platelet-fibrin deposits that bacteria colonize.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c067-->
- **Embolic** complications are infarcts and abscesses in organs such as the brain and spleen.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c068-->
- **Immune** complication: glomerulonephritis from immune complexes, with **flea-bitten kidneys**.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c069-->
- **Heart failure** from valve destruction is the commonest serious complication.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c070-->
- **NBTE** is sterile and spares the valve structure, although it resembles infective endocarditis grossly.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c071-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is "acute versus subacute" the current classification?** No. It is the older, tempo-based scheme; today the classification uses the site and the organism.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c072-->

**Does endocarditis need a previously damaged valve?** No. Even a normal-looking valve can be infected, for instance by *S. aureus*.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c073-->

**Do vegetations form on the downstream side of the valve?** No. They form on the upstream side.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c074-->

**Are flea-bitten kidneys caused by infected emboli?** No. They reflect immune complex glomerulonephritis.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c075-->

**Is every vegetation on a valve infective?** No. Nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis has sterile vegetations.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c076-->

## Mnemonics

**ASSS:** **A**cute = ***A**ureus*, even on a normal valve; **S**ubacute = **S**trep (viridans) on a **S**carred valve, often rheumatic.<!--c:infective-endocarditis-c077-->
