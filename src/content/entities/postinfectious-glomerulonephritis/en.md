## Definition and epidemiology

**What is post-infectious glomerulonephritis?** Immune complexes loaded with complement lodge in the glomeruli after an infection and produce a nephritic picture.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c001-->
In children it ranks among the frequent kidney diseases.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c002-->
Where nephritogenic infections are more frequent it stays common, while in most developed countries it is becoming rarer.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which infections lead to it?** Most cases follow infection with certain group A streptococci or with staphylococci; the streptococcal type is called post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c004-->
Hepatitis B and malaria are examples of viral and parasitic infections that only rarely trigger it.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c005-->
Skin infection (pyoderma) is a particularly common starting point in hot and humid climates; the other site is the pharynx.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c006-->
Staphylococci now account for more cases than before.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c007-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does an infection damage the glomerulus?** In the post-streptococcal form, the kidney disease appears only 9 to 14 days after the infection begins.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c008-->
Complement activation and inflammation could be set off by immune complexes, made of microbial antigen and antibody, that form inside the glomeruli.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c009-->
Bacteria might release substances that switch on complement directly, which would explain deposits with complement but no immunoglobulin.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c010-->
Under these inflammatory signals, mesangial and endothelial cells multiply, and neutrophils and monocytes arrive.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c011-->
Acute diffuse proliferative glomerulonephritis is the name for this marked hypercellularity of the glomeruli.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c012-->
More than 90% of patients have low serum complement, a sign of how much complement is used up.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c013-->

## Gross morphology

Not applicable.

## Microscopic morphology

**What do the glomeruli look like in the acute phase?** Throughout they are swollen and cell-rich.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c014-->
Infiltrating neutrophils and monocytes add to proliferating mesangial and endothelial cells in making the glomerulus cell-rich.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c015-->
Crescents are uncommon.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c016-->
The interstitium also shows some edema and a light scatter of mononuclear cells.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c017-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which method?**
- **How it is studied:** a Masson trichrome section of an acute case shows glomeruli packed with neutrophils.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c018-->
- **Electron microscopy:** the hallmark hump is a dense, dome-shaped subepithelial deposit.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c019-->
- Humps never occur alone: mesangial and subendothelial deposits are always present too.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c020-->
- Hard to detect, these deposits sit beside the blood's inflammatory systems and probably contribute more to injury than the humps.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c021-->
- **Immunofluorescence:** capillary walls show granular C3 at the sites of the humps, with or without immunoglobulin.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c022-->
- In a rare variant, mostly after MRSA infection, IgA stands out in the deposits.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c023-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** Onset is abrupt, with a puffy face and high blood pressure, little urine and blood in the urine: a nephritic syndrome.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c024-->
One to 2 weeks after the infection starts, the acute illness begins.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c025-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which tests support the diagnosis?** During the acute syndrome C3 is low; it normalizes in 1 to 2 weeks.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c026-->
Rising antibody titers to streptococcal antigens may establish the diagnosis, since the organism can be gone when nephritis develops.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c027-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What is the outcome?** Within a few weeks the acute phase is over in over 9 in 10 patients.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c028-->
The neutrophils and the excess endothelial cells go first.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c029-->
The mesangial changes clear last, in most patients within a few months.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c030-->
Hematuria and especially proteinuria can last for months, even though overt nephritis fades within a few weeks.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c031-->
Years after the attack, a few patients still show an abnormal urinary sediment.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c032-->
Progressive renal failure is rare and seen mainly in adults.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c033-->

## High-yield summary

- **Post-streptococcal** infection, 9 to 14 days earlier, is the classic trigger.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c036-->
- In the acute phase the glomeruli are swollen and **hypercellular**.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c037-->
- Electron microscopy shows **subepithelial humps**.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c038-->
- Immunofluorescence shows granular **C3**, and serum C3 is low.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c039-->
- **Nephritic syndrome**: scanty bloody urine, puffy face, high blood pressure.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c040-->
- The **outcome** is usually complete recovery, with rare progression to renal failure, mainly in adults.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c041-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a negative culture rule it out?** No. Rising antibody titers to streptococcal antigens can make the diagnosis, because the organism may have cleared by the time nephritis appears.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c042-->

**Is it always streptococcal?** No. Staphylococci also cause it, and their share is increasing.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c043-->

**Do subepithelial deposits mean membranous nephropathy?** No. Membranous nephropathy has the more widespread deposits; the humps are more limited.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c044-->

**Does every patient recover completely?** Most do, but proteinuria can last for months and a few patients progress to renal failure.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c045-->

## Mnemonics

**HUMPS:** Hypercellular glomeruli, Under the epithelium, Missing complement (low C3), Pharynx or skin first, Subsides in weeks.<!--c:postinfectious-glomerulonephritis-c046-->
