## Definition and epidemiology
**What is prostatic adenocarcinoma, and who gets it?** Of prostate cancers, the commonest arises from the acini.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c001--> It mainly affects elderly men.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c002--> Many are latent and may stay silent for life.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c003--> Hence PSA blood screening is still debated.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors
**What raises the risk?** Risk rises with age.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c005--> Death rates are lowest in Japan, Greece and Mexico and highest in Scandinavia and the United States.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c006--> No group worldwide dies of it more often than Black American men.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c007--> An affected first-degree relative markedly raises risk.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c008--> Red meat and animal fat are suspected, but how they act is unknown.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c009--> BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations are also implicated.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c010-->

## Pathogenesis
**What drives the tumor, and what comes before it?** Male hormones are implicated: the tumor responds to castration, and the normal prostate depends on androgens.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c011--> Yet circulating androgens are typically not raised.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c012--> Some tumors have acquired mutations that let androgens, through the TMPRSS2 promoter, drive the ETV1 gene.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c013--> In others, the glutathione S-transferase gene (an antioxidant) is hypermethylated.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c014--> Prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN) is the accepted precursor lesion.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c016--> In PIN, basal cells become fewer and the luminal cells lining the ducts look atypical.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c017--> High-grade PIN shows crowded, enlarged nuclei with nucleoli.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c018--> It and invasive cancer lie mainly in the periphery of the gland.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c019--> Invasive carcinoma is often found when a patient whose first biopsy showed high-grade PIN is biopsied again.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c020-->

## Gross morphology
**Can the tumor be seen on the cut prostate?** Tumors are often multicentric.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c021--> The peripheral zones hold the tumor in more than 70% of cases.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c022--> Sections may show blurred, uneven yellowish-white patches.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c023--> Most look unremarkable, so the tumor is hard to spot.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c024-->

## Microscopic morphology
**What do the cancer glands look like?** Glands infiltrating the stroma, small to medium and fuzzy-edged, mark the usual acinar tumor.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c025--> Malignant glands lack the basal cells that normal glands have.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c026--> They also lose the lobular growth pattern.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c027--> A well-differentiated tumor keeps one layer of cells around each gland, and the glands stay alike and small to medium.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c028--> Solid cords of invading cells, with few or no glands, mark poor differentiation.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c029--> Cribriform and papillary patterns mark loss of differentiation.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c030--> The nuclear change seen most often is a prominent nucleolus, sometimes two.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c031--> Heavily vacuolated cytoplasm can make tumor cells resemble the clear cells of renal cell carcinoma.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c032-->

**How is the tumor graded?** The Gleason grade comes from how glands form and infiltrate, sorted into five patterns.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c033--> Patterns 1 and 2 are rarely assigned: such well-formed tumors look too much like benign glands.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c034--> Small, discrete invasive glands make up pattern 3, the most frequent.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c035--> Pattern 4 shows disordered glands: fused, cribriform or glomeruloid.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c036--> Comedo necrosis, or glandless growth as sheets, cords or single cells, defines pattern 5, the highest grade.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c037--> The Gleason score adds the predominant pattern to the next most common.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c038--> Grade groups 1 to 5 rank the risk of each score range.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c039-->
- Group 1: score 6 or less.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c040-->
- Group 2: score 3 + 4 = 7.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c041-->
- Group 3: score 4 + 3 = 7.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c042-->
- Group 4: score 8.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c043-->
- Group 5: score 9 or 10.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c044-->

<aside class="edition">Grading and staging follow the 8th edition of one textbook; cut-offs change with classification updates, so check a current source.</aside>

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings
**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** a raised serum PSA typically sends the patient to needle biopsy.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c045-->
- On H&E, look in the stroma for small glands with ill-defined borders.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c046-->
- Cancer glands have no basal cells, so they stay negative for high-molecular-weight cytokeratin and p63, which stain benign glands.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c047-->
- Benign glands lack AMACR; high-grade PIN and carcinoma express it.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c048-->
- These markers help most in difficult biopsies.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c049-->
- PSA immunostaining of a metastasis points to the prostate as its origin.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c050-->

## Clinical presentation
**How does it present, and what is PSA?** Screening with PSA detects them most often, as most are symptom-free.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c051--> A minority present with symptoms of metastasis or bladder outlet obstruction.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c052--> The prostate makes PSA, a serine protease that liquefies semen.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c053--> Neoplasia, but also hypertrophy and inflammation, raise serum PSA.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c054--> Preoperative PSA correlates with cancer volume.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c055--> Screening pairs a digital rectal examination with serum PSA.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c056--> Wider screening means more treatment, with incontinence and impotence, so guidelines keep changing.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c057-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging
**How is it followed and staged?** A serum PSA that keeps rising after therapy signals recurrence.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c058--> In osteoblastic bone metastases, osteoblasts building new bone secrete alkaline phosphatase, so serum levels climb.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c059--> Staging uses the TNM system.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c060-->
- T1: tumor not clinically detectable.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c061-->
- T2: tumor limited to the gland.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c062-->
- T3: spread through the capsule; T3b reaches the seminal vesicles.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c063-->
- T4: invasion of nearby structures, the seminal vesicles excepted.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c064-->
- N1: regional lymph node metastases.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c065-->
- M1: distant metastases.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c066-->

Clinical staging underestimates spread: microscopy upstages 60% of patients first called T2 to T3.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c067-->

## Complications and prognosis
**How does it spread, and how does it end?** The tumor arises just under the capsule, so it often invades it.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c068--> Perineural invasion is common.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c069--> Direct spread reaches the seminal vesicles in nearly every case.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c070--> Obturator nodes are the first to harbor metastases.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c072--> Metastases are commonest in lymph nodes, then bone, lung and liver.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c073--> Osteoblastic bone metastases occur above all in the pelvis, ribs and vertebral column.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c074--> Death most often results from carcinomatosis complicated by pneumonia or sepsis.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c076--> Prognosis worsens as stage, grade group and Gleason score rise.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c077--> At five years, 10% of patients with T4 survive, against 40% for T3 and 90% for T1 and T2.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c078-->

## High-yield summary
- Most tumors arise in the **peripheral zone**.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c087-->
- **High-grade PIN** is the precursor: atypical luminal cells, reduced basal layer.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c088-->
- Invasive cancer is made of **small glands without basal cells**.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c089-->
- Cancer glands are **AMACR** positive.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c090-->
- The **Gleason score** is the sum of the two most prominent patterns.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c091-->
- **Grade group 5** is a score of 9 or 10.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c092-->
- **PSA** also rises in inflammation and hypertrophy.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c093-->
- A raised PSA leads to **needle biopsy**.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c094-->
- **Osteoblastic** bone metastases raise alkaline phosphatase.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c095-->
- **T3** means spread through the capsule.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c096-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps
**Does a high PSA prove prostate cancer?** No. PSA also rises with inflammation and hypertrophy.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c097-->

**Do malignant glands keep their basal cells?** No. Benign glands stain for basal-cell markers and cancer glands do not.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c098-->

**Is every prostate cancer dangerous?** No. Many may stay silent for life.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c099-->

**Are prostate bone metastases lytic?** No. They are osteoblastic, forming new bone.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c100-->

**Are scores 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 the same?** No. Both sum to 7, but 3 + 4 is grade group 2 and 4 + 3 is group 3.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c101-->

## Mnemonics
**No base, AMACR on:** cancer glands lack the basal layer and express AMACR.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c102-->

**3 separate, 4 fused, 5 formless:** Gleason pattern 3 has separate glands, pattern 4 fused ones, pattern 5 no gland formation.<!--c:prostatic-adenocarcinoma-c103-->
