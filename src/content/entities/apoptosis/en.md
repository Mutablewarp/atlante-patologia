## Definition and epidemiology

Apoptosis is a regulated, caspase-dependent program of cell death that removes single cells or small clusters while the surrounding cells stay alive.<!--c:apoptosis-c001--> Of the programmed cell death types, processes in which pre-existing signaling circuits kill the cell, apoptosis is the best-characterized and most extensively investigated.<!--c:apoptosis-c002--> Once the program is under way it cannot be reversed, which is why it is tightly controlled.<!--c:apoptosis-c003--> The scale is enormous: in a normal adult more than one million cells die this way every second, balancing the production of new cells.<!--c:apoptosis-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Physiologic triggers**

- **Embryonic sculpting:** regression of the pronephros and mesonephros, loss of Müllerian ducts in males and Wolffian ducts in females, removal of interdigital webs, and hollowing of solid primordia into tubes.<!--c:apoptosis-c005-->
- **Deletion of autoreactive lymphocyte clones** during immune development.<!--c:apoptosis-c006-->
- **Cell turnover in renewing tissues:** enterocytes die at the villus tips and are shed into the lumen.<!--c:apoptosis-c007-->
- **Hormone withdrawal:** regression of the lactating breast after weaning and endometrial atrophy after menopause.<!--c:apoptosis-c008-->
- **Gametogenesis:** a large share of developing male germ cells and about 99% of the oocytes present at birth are eventually removed by apoptosis.<!--c:apoptosis-c009-->

**Pathologic triggers**

- **Irreparable DNA damage** (replication errors, UV light, ionizing radiation, DNA-binding chemicals), sensed mainly through p53.<!--c:apoptosis-c010-->
- **Viral infection:** the infected cell kills itself and limits viral spread.<!--c:apoptosis-c011-->
- **Cytotoxic T lymphocytes and NK cells**, which deliver granzymes through perforin channels.<!--c:apoptosis-c012-->
- **Prolonged release of Ca2+ from the endoplasmic reticulum**.<!--c:apoptosis-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**Caspases.** Caspases are cysteine proteases made as inactive procaspases and switched on in sequence.<!--c:apoptosis-c014--> About 14 are known, roughly half of them working in apoptosis.<!--c:apoptosis-c015--> Initiator caspases start the program; the executioner caspases-3, -6 and -7 finish it by cleaving many proteins, and caspase-3 is the one most often involved.<!--c:apoptosis-c016--> Caspase-3 drives the two hallmark demolitions: DNA cleavage, by switching on caspase-activated DNase (CAD), and cytoskeletal collapse, which lets the cell break into apoptotic bodies.<!--c:apoptosis-c017-->

**Extrinsic (death receptor) pathway.** A ligand such as TNFα, FasL or TRAIL binds its surface receptor; the receptor's cytoplasmic death domain recruits adaptor proteins into a death-inducing signaling complex (DISC).<!--c:apoptosis-c018--> The DISC activates procaspase-8 (and -10), which then activates the executioners.<!--c:apoptosis-c019--> TNF receptor signaling can also switch on NFκB and survival genes, so the same receptor may promote life or death.<!--c:apoptosis-c020-->

**Intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway.** Intracellular stress acts through the BCL-2 family at the outer mitochondrial membrane.<!--c:apoptosis-c021--> In a healthy cell, anti-apoptotic members (Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, Mcl-1) hold Bax and Bak in check, so the default setting is survival.<!--c:apoptosis-c022--> Stress activates BH3-only proteins (e.g., Bim, Bid, Bad), which displace the anti-apoptotic partners or activate Bax directly.<!--c:apoptosis-c023--> Freed Bax and Bak build channels in the outer membrane, through which cytochrome c, SMACs and AIF leak into the cytosol.<!--c:apoptosis-c024--> Apaf-1 bound to cytosolic cytochrome c recruits procaspase-9; the resulting complex, the apoptosome, turns it into active caspase-9, which in turn switches on the executioners.<!--c:apoptosis-c025--> SMACs neutralize the inhibitor-of-apoptosis proteins (IAPs) that would otherwise block caspases-3, -7 and -9.<!--c:apoptosis-c026--> AIF enters the nucleus and fragments DNA without caspases.<!--c:apoptosis-c027--> Opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, favored by Ca2+, ROS and NO, adds to the release of these proteins.<!--c:apoptosis-c028-->

**BCL-2 family structure.** Members share up to four BCL-2 homology domains (BH1–BH4): anti-apoptotic members carry BH1 and BH2 (often BH4), Bax and Bak carry BH1–BH3, and BH3-only members carry just BH3.<!--c:apoptosis-c029-->

**p53.** Normally p53 is kept low by Mdm2-driven ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation.<!--c:apoptosis-c030--> After DNA damage it accumulates; in the nucleus it raises transcription of Bax, NOXA and PUMA and lowers Bcl-2, Bcl-xL and Mcl-1, while in the cytosol it can activate Bax directly.<!--c:apoptosis-c031-->

**Crosstalk.** Caspase-8 cleaves Bid to truncated Bid (tBid), which carries the extrinsic signal into the mitochondrial pathway; granzyme B from cytotoxic lymphocytes also cleaves Bid.<!--c:apoptosis-c032--> Survival signals such as PI3K–Akt oppose apoptosis, for example by inactivating Bad and activating NFκB.<!--c:apoptosis-c033-->

**Removal (efferocytosis).** Dying cells release "find me" signals that attract phagocytes and expose "eat me" signals such as phosphatidylserine and calreticulin, normally confined to the inner leaflet of the membrane.<!--c:apoptosis-c034--> At the same time they lose the "don't eat me" signals of viable cells.<!--c:apoptosis-c035--> Macrophages and neighboring epithelial cells or fibroblasts engulf the fragments before the membrane leaks, so no inflammation follows.<!--c:apoptosis-c036-->

## Microscopic morphology

- Affects **single cells or small groups** scattered among viable cells, not a geographic area.<!--c:apoptosis-c037-->
- **Nucleus:** chromatin condenses (pyknosis) and the nucleus fragments (karyorrhexis), inside an intact cell membrane.<!--c:apoptosis-c038-->
- **Cell body:** the apoptotic cell shrinks, but its plasma membrane stays intact.<!--c:apoptosis-c073--> Its cytoplasm can turn eosinophilic on H&E, as in the apoptotic keratinocytes of erythema multiforme; the acidophilic bodies of viral hepatitis are apoptotic hepatocytes with intensely eosinophilic cytoplasm.<!--c:apoptosis-c074-->
- **Cytoplasm and membrane:** organelles segregate, the plasma membrane forms blebs, and the cell splits into membrane-bound apoptotic bodies, many without nuclear material.<!--c:apoptosis-c039-->
- **No inflammatory infiltrate** around the dying cells.<!--c:apoptosis-c040-->
- Classic examples: apoptotic hepatocytes in viral hepatitis and apoptotic keratinocytes in erythema multiforme.<!--c:apoptosis-c041--> In acute viral hepatitis, single dead hepatocytes become small, strongly eosinophilic **acidophil bodies**, sometimes with a pyknotic nucleus.<!--c:apoptosis-c075--> Yellow fever virus pushes some hepatocytes into apoptosis, and these are the **Councilman bodies**: separated from the surrounding liver cells, with intensely eosinophilic cytoplasm.<!--c:apoptosis-c042--> In lichen planus, apoptotic keratinocytes form round, eosinophilic **colloid (Civatte) bodies**, mixed with the inflammatory infiltrate at the dermal–epidermal junction.<!--c:apoptosis-c076-->
- **Tingible body macrophages** are macrophages packed with engulfed nuclear debris; they are a normal finding in germinal centers.<!--c:apoptosis-c077--> In Burkitt lymphoma, macrophages that have ingested apoptotic tumor cells lie scattered among the tumor cells and create a **starry-sky** pattern.<!--c:apoptosis-c078-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

- Molecular hallmarks: cytochrome c release, caspase-3 activation, DNA fragmentation by CAD, and phosphatidylserine on the outer membrane surface.<!--c:apoptosis-c043-->
- Executioner caspases cleave nuclear lamins, cytoskeletal α-fodrin and PARP.<!--c:apoptosis-c044-->

**How it is studied:**

- **Specimen and sampling:** apoptosis strikes scattered single cells, so there is no gross lesion to sample; it is a microscopic finding in ordinary sections, such as liver tissue in acute viral hepatitis or a skin biopsy in erythema multiforme.<!--c:apoptosis-c079-->
- **H&E:** look for isolated shrunken cells with dense eosinophilic cytoplasm and a dark, condensed or fragmented nucleus, surrounded by healthy cells and without inflammation.<!--c:apoptosis-c080--> H&E catches only the late stages, and because the fragments are engulfed fast, a good deal of apoptosis can pass unseen.<!--c:apoptosis-c081-->
- **Immunohistochemistry:** antibodies to cleaved (active) caspase-3, or to cleaved caspase substrates such as PARP, label apoptotic cells in the section.<!--c:apoptosis-c082-->
- **TUNEL:** the enzyme terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) adds labeled nucleotides to the free 3′ ends of broken DNA; with a peroxidase detection step, apoptotic nuclei turn brown in the section.<!--c:apoptosis-c083-->
- **Electron microscopy**, the reference standard: dense chromatin gathered under the nuclear envelope, nuclear fragments, an intact plasma membrane with surface blebs, and membrane-bound apoptotic bodies with preserved organelles, some already inside phagocytes.<!--c:apoptosis-c084-->
- **Tests on DNA and cells:** on agarose gel electrophoresis, DNA from apoptotic cells forms a ladder of fragments roughly 180 base pairs apart.<!--c:apoptosis-c085--> Annexin V binds the exposed phosphatidylserine, while a dye that enters only cells with leaky membranes, such as propidium iodide, sets necrotic cells apart.<!--c:apoptosis-c086-->

## Clinical presentation

**Too little apoptosis**

- **Cancer:** loss of p53 lets mutations accumulate, and Bcl-2 overexpression is found in some aggressive lymphomas.<!--c:apoptosis-c045--> In follicular lymphoma a translocation puts Bcl-2 under the immunoglobulin heavy-chain promoter, so B cells survive and accumulate.<!--c:apoptosis-c046-->
- **Persistent viral infection and oncogenesis:** many viruses block apoptosis; human papillomavirus inactivates p53.<!--c:apoptosis-c047-->
- **Autoimmunity:** failure to delete self-reactive lymphocyte clones.<!--c:apoptosis-c048--> In systemic lupus erythematosus, dying cells are cleared poorly; nuclear material from the apoptotic debris (DNA with histones, RNA with proteins) activates dendritic cells and autoreactive B cells through Toll-like receptors.<!--c:apoptosis-c087-->
- **Chronic myeloid leukemia:** an anti-apoptotic genetic lesion lets granulocytes pile up.<!--c:apoptosis-c049-->

**Too much apoptosis**

- **Neurodegeneration:** protein aggregates in neurons trigger apoptosis and neuronal loss.<!--c:apoptosis-c050-->
- **Male infertility:** excessive apoptosis of spermatozoa.<!--c:apoptosis-c051-->

## Complications and prognosis

- Defective efferocytosis has been linked to cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis and atherosclerosis.<!--c:apoptosis-c052-->
- If ATP runs out during prolonged mitochondrial injury, the apoptosome cannot work and the cell dies by necrosis instead.<!--c:apoptosis-c053-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[coagulative-necrosis]] | Geographic area of dead cells, cell swelling, membrane rupture and acute inflammation.<!--c:apoptosis-c054--> | Pattern on H&E: area versus single cells; inflammation present |
| [[necroptosis]] | Programmed but caspase-independent; RIP1–RIP3 necrosome and MLKL rupture membranes, so it looks like necrosis and is inflammatory.<!--c:apoptosis-c055--> | Necrotic morphology despite a programmed trigger |
| [[pyroptosis]] | Infection-driven death that depends on caspase-1; the cell swells and bursts, releasing DAMPs.<!--c:apoptosis-c056--> | Caspase-1 activation in the inflammasome |
| [[autophagy]] | Chiefly a pro-survival response to stress; its role as a killer in its own right is an open question.<!--c:apoptosis-c057--> | No nuclear fragmentation or apoptotic bodies |

## High-yield summary

- Apoptosis kills **single cells** without **inflammation**; necrosis kills **areas** with inflammation.<!--c:apoptosis-c058-->
- **Caspases** are cysteine proteases; **caspase-3** is the main executioner.<!--c:apoptosis-c059-->
- **Extrinsic** pathway: **Fas/FasL** or **TNF** receptor, **DISC**, **caspase-8**.<!--c:apoptosis-c060-->
- **Intrinsic** pathway: **BH3-only** sensors free **Bax/Bak**, **cytochrome c** is released, and the **apoptosome** (Apaf-1) activates **caspase-9**.<!--c:apoptosis-c061-->
- **Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, Mcl-1** protect; **Bax, Bak** and BH3-only proteins kill.<!--c:apoptosis-c062-->
- **tBid** links the extrinsic to the intrinsic pathway.<!--c:apoptosis-c063-->
- **p53** triggers apoptosis after irreparable DNA damage through **PUMA, NOXA** and Bax.<!--c:apoptosis-c064-->
- **Phosphatidylserine** flipped to the cell surface is an **"eat me"** signal for efferocytosis.<!--c:apoptosis-c065-->
- Beyond H&E, apoptosis is shown with **cleaved caspase-3** IHC, **TUNEL** and **electron microscopy** (the reference standard), ideally combining two methods.<!--c:apoptosis-c088-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- Pyknosis and nuclear fragmentation are not specific to necrosis; scale and inflammation separate the two.<!--c:apoptosis-c066-->
- Apoptosis is not ATP-independent: the apoptosome needs ATP, and severe ATP loss pushes the cell toward necrosis.<!--c:apoptosis-c067-->
- Bcl-2 is anti-apoptotic, whereas Bax and Bak, members of the same family, are pro-apoptotic.<!--c:apoptosis-c068-->
- TNFα does not always kill: through NFκB and PI3K it can also deliver survival signals.<!--c:apoptosis-c069-->
- Not all programmed death is apoptosis: necroptosis and pyroptosis are programmed yet lytic and inflammatory.<!--c:apoptosis-c070-->
- AIF and granzymes can drive DNA fragmentation without caspases.<!--c:apoptosis-c071-->
- A positive TUNEL or annexin V test does not prove apoptosis on its own: TUNEL also labels necrotic cells and cells repairing DNA, and annexin V also binds necrotic cells, so morphology or a second method must confirm it.<!--c:apoptosis-c089-->

## Mnemonics

**QUIET** (original): **Q**uiet single-cell death among living neighbors, without inflammation, **U**nder caspase control, **I**ntact membrane until engulfed, **E**at-me signal (phosphatidylserine) for efferocytosis, **T**wo pathways (extrinsic caspase-8, intrinsic caspase-9) meeting at caspase-3.<!--c:apoptosis-c072-->
