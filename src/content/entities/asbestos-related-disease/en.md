## Definition and epidemiology

**What is asbestos-related disease?** Inhaled asbestos can injure the lung and the pleura in two ways: reactive scarring conditions and malignant tumors.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c001--> The silicate minerals called asbestos all form thin fibers.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c002--> Scarring that spreads through the lung interstitium after these fibers are inhaled is called asbestosis.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c003--> Only heavy exposure, once common in mining and insulation, leads to asbestosis.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which fibers and which exposures cause disease?** Two mineral groups hold the six natural types of asbestos.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c005--> Most of the asbestos sold commercially is chrysotile.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c006--> Commercial use of the amphiboles, the second group, was largely limited to two members, amosite and crocidolite.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c007--> Crocidolite and the other amphiboles are much more likely than chrysotile to cause disease, although every commercial type is linked to lung disease.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c008--> Like the amphiboles, erionite causes disease; this fiber-forming zeolite is found in Turkey and nearby areas.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c009--> Asbestos was widely used in building materials and in car brakes.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c010--> Mesothelioma can also affect non-workers, such as a spouse who washed the contaminated work clothes.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c011--> Heavy workplace exposure, mainly to crocidolite and amosite, is the more frequent background of mesothelioma.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c012-->

## Pathogenesis

**How do the fibers cause fibrosis?** Fibers settle where alveolar ducts branch, deep in the lung, since they are thin even when long.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c013--> Many larger fibers escape into the interstitium, while only the smallest particles are engulfed by macrophages.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c014--> The earliest injury there is alveolitis, and more exposure means more of it.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c015--> Free fibers, which are fibrogenic themselves, and mediators released by activated macrophages together promote interstitial fibrosis.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c016--> Lymphatics may carry fibers to the pleural space, where they are thought to provoke plaque formation.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c017-->

## Gross morphology

**What do the lungs and the pleura look like?** The lower zones of the lung usually suffer most; the disease is bilateral and diffuse.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c018--> In the end stage the lung becomes a honeycomb lung.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c019--> Plaques look pearly white; their surface is either smooth or lumpy.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c020--> Plaques favor the diaphragm's dome and the low back and side of the parietal pleura.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c021--> Calcification and large size are possible, and the two sides, usually both involved, need not match.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c022--> Pleural folds several centimeters deep run into the lung in rounded atelectasis, where the pleura is fibrotic or plaque-bearing.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c023--> This lesion is clinically benign.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c024-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What do we see on the slide?** Early on, the scars sit at the edge of the acinus, around the respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c025--> Alveolar macrophages often engulf asbestos bodies, which sit in the alveolar spaces or the walls of bronchioles.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c026--> Under the light microscope an asbestos body is golden brown and beaded, since iron and protein are laid down around a transparent, non-birefringent fiber.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c027--> One macrophage cannot take up the whole fiber, so the cells wrap it in a coat of ferritin and other proteins.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c028--> The basket-weave look of a plaque comes from parallel narrow slits in dense, cell-free, hyaline fibrous tissue.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c029-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** under the light microscope, look for diffuse interstitial fibrosis together with golden-brown asbestos bodies.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c030-->
- **Special stain:** Prussian blue stains the iron in the coat of asbestos bodies, giving a strong reaction.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c031-->
- **Autopsy lung:** almost every adult lung has some asbestos bodies when the tissue is digested and concentrated.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c032-->
- The source used for this card describes no immunohistochemical, electron microscopic or molecular test.

## Clinical presentation

**What does a patient with asbestos exposure develop?** Mild chronic obstruction of airflow can accompany the restrictive defect of asbestosis.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c033--> Pleural plaques appear 10 to 20 years after exposure.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c034--> A known history of exposure is missing in many people found to have plaques at autopsy, and plaques also occur in the general population.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c035--> Within 10 years of first exposure, benign pleural effusion is often the lesion that appears.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c036--> It affects about 3% of exposed workers.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c037--> Ten years or more usually pass after first exposure before diffuse pleural fibrosis is found.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c038--> Plaques do not predict asbestosis.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c039--> Plaques are not a precursor of mesothelioma.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c040-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is each lesion recognized?** Asbestosis can be diagnosed only if the lung also shows diffuse interstitial fibrosis; asbestos bodies found by chance at autopsy do not suffice.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c041--> Benign pleural effusion is a diagnosis of exclusion: other causes must be absent and follow-up must show no malignant tumor, in a patient with an exposure history and an effusion found on radiograph or thoracentesis.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c042--> Along the back of a lower lobe, rounded atelectasis typically appears on a chest radiograph as a round or oval shadow based on the pleura.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c043--> Asbestosis is diffuse scarring within the lung interstitium; diffuse pleural fibrosis is scarring confined to the pleura.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c044-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What are the long-term risks?** Later in asbestosis, scarring is no longer confined to the area around the bronchioles.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c045--> Asbestos is firmly established as a cause of malignant mesothelioma.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c046--> Smokers who work with asbestos get lung carcinoma up to 40 to 60 times as often as nonsmokers in the general population.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c047--> Among asbestos workers who do not smoke, the evidence is thin and cannot settle the question.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c048--> Best evidence linking asbestos to lung cancer comes from patients who also have asbestosis.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c049-->

## High-yield summary

- **Ferruginous (asbestos) bodies** are golden-brown, iron-coated fibers that stain with **Prussian blue**.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c055-->
- **Asbestosis** needs **diffuse interstitial fibrosis**, not just asbestos bodies.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c056-->
- **Pleural plaques** are mostly parietal, pearly white and cell-free, with a **basket-weave** hyaline texture.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c057-->
- **Amphiboles**, **crocidolite** above all, are more harmful than chrysotile.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c058-->
- **Lung carcinoma** risk is vastly higher in asbestos workers who **smoke**.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c059-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Do pleural plaques turn into mesothelioma?** No. A plaque is not a precursor of mesothelioma.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c060-->

**Do asbestos bodies in the lung prove asbestosis?** No. Nearly every adult lung contains some.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c061-->

**Is chrysotile as dangerous as crocidolite?** No. Crocidolite is far more likely to cause disease.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c062-->

**Does asbestos clearly raise lung cancer risk in nonsmokers?** Not established. The data are limited.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c063-->

**Is diffuse pleural fibrosis the same as asbestosis?** No. It is limited to the pleura, while asbestosis scars the lung interstitium.<!--c:asbestos-related-disease-c064-->
