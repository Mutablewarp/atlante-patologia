## Definition and epidemiology

**What is membranous nephropathy?** Membranous nephropathy means immune complexes trapped in the subepithelial space, between the podocytes and the basement membrane.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c001--> It often underlies nephrotic syndrome in adults.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c002--> In the United States, white and Asian adults with primary glomerular nephrotic syndrome most often have this disease.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What triggers it, and what is a secondary cause?** In the primary form the target is a podocyte antigen, most often PLA2R.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c004--> THSD7A is another known target.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c005--> The secondary forms have an identifiable cause:
- Autoimmune: lupus (SLE) or thyroid autoimmunity.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c006-->
- Infection, such as hepatitis B or syphilis.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c007-->
- Drugs, such as penicillamine.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c008-->
- Tumors, such as lung or prostate cancer.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c009-->

The neonatal form is rare: the mother lacks neutral endopeptidase, which the baby's podocytes carry, so her antibodies against it cross the placenta.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c010-->

## Pathogenesis

**How do the deposits get under the podocytes?** There are two routes: the complexes form on the spot, or circulating complexes settle in the subepithelial zone.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c011--> The Heymann rat model shows the first route: antibodies to a kidney antigen reach the podocytes through the basement membrane, and the complexes form beneath them.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c012--> Most primary cases, about three in four, carry blood antibodies to PLA2R, a receptor that spans the podocyte membrane.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c013--> Finding the antigen together with its antibody inside the deposits points to local formation.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c014--> Complement, including the membrane attack complex, is heavily deposited and chiefly damages the podocytes.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c015-->

**Why do the secondary forms look different?** Primary disease seldom puts deposits in the mesangium, whereas secondary disease often does.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c016--> The antigen's origin may explain this: a podocyte antigen keeps complexes beneath the epithelium, while a blood-borne one, such as a hepatitis B antigen, can reach the mesangium too.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c017-->

## Gross morphology

## Microscopic morphology

**What do the glomeruli look like?** They are usually normocellular.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c018--> Whether the capillary walls are normal or thickened depends on how long the disease has lasted.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c019--> Spikes studding the podocyte side of the basement membrane show up with silver in mid-stage disease.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c020--> Immune complexes do not take up silver; the spikes are basement membrane material projecting around them.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c021--> Eventually the glomeruli become sclerotic, after the capillary lumens have narrowed.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c022--> The more scarred the glomeruli, the more tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis there is.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c023-->

**What does electron microscopy show?** Dense deposits lie in the capillary walls.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c024--> Four stages are described:
- Stage I: deposits beneath the epithelium, with a smooth basement membrane and no projections.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c025-->
- Stage II: spikes of basement membrane rise beside the deposits.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c026-->
- Stage III: basement membrane material closes around the deposits.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c027-->
- Stage IV: a thick basement membrane, with deposits that have lost density.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c028-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** on routine H&E, look for glomeruli without extra cells; wall thickness varies with disease duration.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c029-->
- Spikes show up on silver stains because these stain basement membrane material.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c030-->
- On immunofluorescence, IgG and C3 show as granules throughout the capillary walls.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c031-->
- Electron microscopy shows the deposits and their stage.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c032-->
- Serology: anti-PLA2R antibodies are found in the blood in most primary cases.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c033-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does a patient present?** Adults with nephrotic syndrome are the patients in whom it is commonly found.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c034--> The course varies widely between patients.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c035-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What tells the pathologist this is membranous nephropathy?** Granular IgG and C3 along the capillary walls, together with subepithelial dense deposits on electron microscopy, define the pattern.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c036--> Mesangial deposits suggest a secondary cause, such as lupus.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c037-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens in the long run?** Over 20 years, one in four patients experiences a spontaneous remission.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c038--> More than 65% of kidneys are still functioning 10 years on.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c039--> Kidney survival is worse in men and with proteinuria above 6 g/day.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c040--> Steroids, with or without other immunosuppressants, are used once kidney function worsens.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c041--> Children do better because their remissions more often last for good.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c042-->

## High-yield summary

- The deposits are **subepithelial immune complexes** under the podocytes.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c046-->
- About 75% of primary cases carry **anti-PLA2R** antibodies.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c047-->
- Silver stain shows **spikes**, made of basement membrane material around the deposits.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c048-->
- Glomeruli are **normocellular**, and wall thickness varies with disease duration.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c049-->
- Immunofluorescence shows **granular IgG and C3** along the capillary walls.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c050-->
- Secondary causes: **lupus and hepatitis B**, but also some drugs and cancers.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c051-->
- **Mesangial deposits** favor a secondary form.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c052-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Are the silver "spikes" immune complexes?** No. They are basement membrane material around the deposits, and the deposits themselves do not stain with silver.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c053-->

**Do all primary cases have anti-PLA2R antibodies?** No. About 75% do, and other targets such as THSD7A exist.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c054-->

**Is it a proliferative glomerulonephritis?** No. The glomeruli are usually normocellular, and the walls may be thickened.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c055-->

**Do mesangial deposits fit the primary form?** Rarely. They point more toward a secondary cause, such as lupus.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c056-->

## Mnemonics

**SPIKES** (original): **S**ubepithelial deposits, **P**LA2R antibodies, **I**gG and C3 granular, **K**idney glomeruli normocellular, **E**xclude secondary causes (SLE, hepatitis B, drugs, tumors), **S**ilver stain shows the spikes.<!--c:membranous-nephropathy-c057-->
