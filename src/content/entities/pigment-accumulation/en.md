## Definition and epidemiology

Pigments are colored substances that cells can retain; some are made in the body (endogenous) and others enter from outside (exogenous).<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c001--> The main endogenous pigments are lipofuscin, melanin and the two products of hemoglobin breakdown, hemosiderin and bilirubin; the commonest exogenous one is inhaled carbon.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c002--> Most pigment deposits are harmless signs of something that happened, but iron overload and high unconjugated bilirubin in the newborn do injure cells.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c003--> Carbon is almost universal: practically everyone who lives in a city breathes in soot from burned fossil fuel.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

- **Lipofuscin:** builds up with age in cells that no longer divide, such as neurons and cardiac myocytes, and in slowly renewing cells such as hepatocytes.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c005--> It is more obvious when an organ atrophies.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c006--> In marasmus the wasted heart and liver carry extra lipofuscin.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c007-->
- **Hemosiderin, local:** any hemorrhage; macrophages eat the extravasated red cells and keep their iron, for example in the alveoli during chronic pulmonary congestion.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c008-->
- **Hemosiderin, systemic:** more iron absorbed (hereditary hemochromatosis, some anemias) or iron given parenterally (repeated transfusions, injected iron).<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c009-->
- **Melanin:** sun exposure increases it (tanning); nevi and melanomas produce it; an inherited inability to make it is albinism.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c010-->
- **Bilirubin:** too much made (hemolysis, ineffective erythropoiesis), too little taken up or conjugated by the liver, or too little excreted into bile.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c011-->
- **Exogenous:** inhaled carbon (anthracosis); in melanosis coli, brown pigment collects in colonic macrophages of people who take plant-derived laxatives.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c012-->

## Pathogenesis

**Lipofuscin = indigestible oxidative residue.** Neither autophagy nor lysosomal enzymes can digest it, so it can only accumulate: its golden-brown granules consist of peroxidized lipid and of proteins that oxidation has damaged and cross-linked.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c013--> It may not be inert: by hampering proteasomal and lysosomal clearance it may let damaged, ROS-producing mitochondria persist, which would sustain a vicious circle of oxidant stress.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c014-->

**Iron: safe storage versus overload.** About a quarter of total body iron is held in storage form, bound to ferritin or hemosiderin.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c015--> Ferritin locks away ferric iron so that it cannot drive free-radical formation through the Fenton reaction.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c016--> Hemosiderin is partly denatured, clumped ferritin; normally it is found above all in the spleen and in the Kupffer cells of the liver.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c017--> In hereditary hemochromatosis, too little hepcidin leaves the iron exporter ferroportin unchecked, so the gut absorbs more and stores release more, and free iron floods parenchymal cells.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c018--> The resulting tissue damage most likely comes from iron-driven oxidative stress.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c019-->

**Heme to bilirubin.** Heme oxygenase opens the heme ring to biliverdin and releases carbon monoxide.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c020--> Bilirubin is the final product of heme catabolism; macrophages that clear aged red cells from the blood supply most of that heme (roughly 80%).<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c021--> Unconjugated bilirubin is fat-soluble and circulates bound to albumin; hepatocytes take it up and conjugate it with glucuronic acid in the ER, which makes it water-soluble, and then secrete it into bile, the slowest (rate-limiting) step.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c022--> Unconjugated bilirubin not bound to albumin enters the lipid-rich brain, where it is toxic.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c023--> Newborns are the patients at risk: about 70% of healthy infants go through a transient unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia (physiologic jaundice), because red cells break down faster after birth while the liver reaches adult conjugating capacity only at about two weeks of age.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c078-->

**Melanin.** Melanocytes polymerize oxidation products of tyrosine inside melanosomes and pass the granules on to surrounding cells.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c024--> Melanin absorbs ultraviolet light and so shields the skin from sun damage.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c025--> Neuromelanin, which forms as a side product when neurons make catecholamine transmitters, is the brown pigment of the neurons in certain brainstem nuclei.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c079-->

**Carbon.** Inhaled carbon is ingested by alveolar macrophages, which deliver it to the regional lymph nodes, chiefly at the lung hilum; the particles are never cleared.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c026--> Amorphous carbon alone neither kills macrophages nor causes fibrosis.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c027-->

## Gross morphology

- The changing colors of a healing bruise (ecchymosis), green and then yellow after the first purple, follow the conversion of hemoglobin from the broken-down red cells into bilirubin.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c080-->
- Long-standing pulmonary congestion leaves the lungs brown and hard: the interstitium scars and the airspaces fill with hemosiderin-laden macrophages (brown induration).<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c081-->
- In a chronically congested spleen, old hemorrhages leave small fibrotic foci encrusted with iron and calcium (Gamna–Gandy bodies).<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c082-->
- A strikingly black, anthracotic lung is not in itself evidence of disease.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c028-->
- In hereditary hemochromatosis the liver is enlarged, red-brown and micronodular-cirrhotic, and the pancreas is rust-colored and fibrotic.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c029-->
- With extrahepatic obstruction the enlarged liver turns green from retained bile; the tint persists in long-standing obstruction, even after bile secretion falls so far that the duct contents turn pale ("white bile").<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c030-->
- In Dubin–Johnson syndrome the pigment makes the liver grossly dark, even black.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c031-->
- In Parkinson disease the substantia nigra and the locus coeruleus look pale, because most of their pigmented neurons have been lost.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c083-->

## Microscopic morphology

- **Lipofuscin:** fine golden-brown granules in the cytoplasm, for example in hepatocytes of the elderly.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c032-->
- **Hemosiderin:** yellow-brown cytoplasmic granules, often in macrophages; in left heart failure, hemosiderin-laden alveolar macrophages are called heart failure cells.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c033-->
- **Iron distribution matters:** in hereditary hemochromatosis iron fills hepatocytes and bile duct epithelium, reaching Kupffer cells only late.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c034--> In transfusion-related siderosis it starts in Kupffer cells and later spills into hepatocytes.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c035-->
- **Melanin:** brown-black granules, for example in the cells of an intradermal nevus.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c036-->
- **Bile:** brownish bile pigment in dilated canaliculi (bile plugs) and in hepatocytes, first around the central vein.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c037--> If a bile duct obstruction lasts, hepatocytes swell and are diffusely stained by bile (feathery degeneration), and ruptured ductules spill bile into golden-yellow pools (bile lakes).<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c084-->
- **Carbon:** black particles inside macrophages of the lung and mediastinal lymph nodes.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c038-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Specimen:** the liver is involved in every case of hereditary hemochromatosis, and late in the disease it holds more than 0.5 g of iron per 100 g of wet tissue.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c085--> Body iron stores are judged on a smear of bone marrow aspirate stained with Perls; in healthy people both macrophages and nucleated red-cell precursors (sideroblasts) contain blue iron granules.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c086-->
- **H&E:** note the color, the size of the granules and the cell that holds them (see Microscopic morphology) before choosing a special stain such as Perls (see [[g:perls-prussian-blue]]).
- **Perls (Prussian blue)** turns hemosiderin iron blue, which separates it from the other brown pigments.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c039--> A negative iron stain is informative: the dark Dubin–Johnson pigment is iron-free and sits in enlarged lysosomes.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c040--> On a marrow smear the stain answers a clinical question: in iron-deficiency anemia both the stores and the red-cell precursors are empty, whereas in the anemia of chronic disease (anemia of inflammation) macrophages hold normal or even increased iron that excess hepcidin keeps from reaching the precursors.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c087-->
- **Polarized light:** silicate particles in silicotic nodules are birefringent needles, so polarization can show that a pigmented dust lesion in a coal miner's lung also contains silica (anthracosilicosis, see Complications and prognosis).<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c088-->
- **Immunohistochemistry:** none is needed to recognize a pigment.
- **Electron microscopy:** in hereditary hemochromatosis most of the excess iron in liver cells is ferric iron held in lysosomes.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c089--> In cholestasis the canalicular microvilli are blunted or lost, and the bile inside hepatocytes collects in large lysosomes.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c090-->
- **Molecular tests:** most hereditary hemochromatosis is caused by mutations of the **HFE** gene, above all homozygous **C282Y**.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c041--> About 90% of patients carry C282Y on both copies of HFE, yet some people with this genotype never develop iron overload.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c091--> Dubin–Johnson syndrome results from mutations in both copies of ABCC2, the gene for MRP2, a transporter that moves conjugated bilirubin across the canalicular membrane into bile.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c092-->
- **Limits:** a blue Perls reaction shows iron, not necessarily an old hemorrhage or iron overload: asbestos bodies, golden brown under the light microscope, also react strongly, because macrophages wrap the fiber in a protein layer that includes ferritin.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c093--> Lung digests taken at autopsy show asbestos bodies in nearly every adult, so a few bodies without diffuse interstitial fibrosis do not amount to asbestosis.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c094-->

## Clinical presentation

- **Lipofuscin and anthracosis:** incidental findings; anthracosis causes no disease on its own.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c042-->
- **Jaundice:** yellow skin and sclerae, visible once serum bilirubin exceeds about 2.5–3.0 mg/dL.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c043-->
- **Hereditary hemochromatosis:** late disease combines cirrhosis, diabetes, cardiac dysfunction and darkened skin; the classic label for diabetes with skin pigmentation is "bronze diabetes".<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c044-->
- **Hemosiderosis:** total stored iron rises in many organs, the heart and pancreas among them; the term itself implies that the iron-laden cells remain undamaged.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c045-->
- **Heart failure cells** point to left-sided heart failure with passive pulmonary congestion.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c046-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

- In tissue, a pigment is identified by color, by the cell that holds it and by special stains, with Perls deciding whether it is iron.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c047-->
- In established hereditary hemochromatosis serum iron is more than twice normal, transferrin saturation is markedly raised (up to 100%) and serum ferritin is greatly increased.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c048-->
- Serum ferritin also rises in acute inflammation, so a high value alone does not prove iron overload.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c049-->
- When the liver works normally, hemolysis raises chiefly the unconjugated fraction; disease of the liver cells themselves raises conjugated and unconjugated bilirubin together.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c050-->

## Complications and prognosis

- **Iron overload:** in hereditary hemochromatosis heart failure often causes death, and the risk of liver cancer is increased.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c051--> Excess stored iron may also raise cancer risk elsewhere, as with lung cancer in metal polishers with pulmonary siderosis.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c052--> Timing decides the outlook: untreated, only 6% of patients survive 10 years, whereas iron removal by repeated phlebotomy, begun before cirrhosis or diabetes, gives a normal life expectancy.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c095-->
- **Bilirubin:** kernicterus, permanent brain injury from unconjugated bilirubin, is a risk of the neonatal period; in adults the same rise is usually of little consequence.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c053--> Light turns unconjugated bilirubin into water-soluble isomers, which is why phototherapy is the routine treatment of neonatal jaundice.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c096-->
- **Carbon:** harmless alone, but when inhaled together with silica it contributes to anthracosilicosis.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c054-->
- **Lipofuscin:** probably not a mere bystander: in aging cells it may arise from oxidant stress and also amplify it.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c055-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Lipofuscin | Fine golden-brown cytoplasmic granules in long-lived cells (neurons, myocytes, hepatocytes).<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c056--> | Iron stain negative |
| [[hereditary-hemochromatosis]] | Iron in parenchymal cells (hepatocytes, bile ducts, myocytes, pancreas) with organ damage.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c057--> | Perls; serum iron, transferrin saturation, ferritin |
| Secondary (transfusional) iron overload | Iron first in Kupffer cells and macrophages, later spilling into hepatocytes; cirrhosis is rare, but massive overload (e.g. thalassemia major) damages tissues.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c058--> | Perls; transfusion history |
| [[anthracosis]] | Jet-black particles in alveolar and lymph node macrophages.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c059--> | Iron stain negative; exposure history |
| Dubin–Johnson pigment | Large, very dark granules that do not stain for iron, mostly in hepatocytes around central veins; no other histologic lesion.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c060--> | Iron stain negative; urinary coproporphyrin isomer pattern |
| Melanosis coli | Brown pigment in macrophages of the colonic mucosa in people taking plant-derived laxatives.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c061--> | Laxative history |
| Ochronosis (alkaptonuria) | Bluish-black pigment in connective tissue from homogentisic acid, with arthritis.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c062--> | Clinical setting |
| Asbestos bodies | Golden-brown beaded rods, each an asbestos fiber with an iron–protein coat, in alveoli or bronchiolar walls, often only partly taken up by macrophages.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c097--> | Perls positive; asbestosis also needs diffuse interstitial fibrosis |
| Malarial pigment | Dark pigment made by the parasite as it digests hemoglobin, lying with hemosiderin in macrophages of the enlarged spleen and liver, which turn dark gray.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c098--> | Clinical setting; parasites in red cells |

## High-yield summary

- **Lipofuscin** = "wear-and-tear" pigment: oxidized lipid and protein that **autophagy** cannot digest; increases with **age** and **atrophy**.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c063-->
- **Hemosiderin** = clumped **ferritin**; stains blue with **Perls (Prussian blue)**.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c064-->
- **Hemosiderosis** stores iron without injury; **hemochromatosis** injures **parenchymal** cells through iron-driven **ROS**.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c065-->
- **Heart failure cells** = hemosiderin-laden **alveolar macrophages** in chronic pulmonary congestion.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c066-->
- **Brown induration** of the lung and **Gamna–Gandy bodies** of the spleen: fibrosis plus **hemosiderin** left by **chronic congestion**.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c099-->
- **Bilirubin**: end product of **heme**; unconjugated bilirubin is **lipid-soluble** and can damage the newborn brain (**kernicterus**).<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c067-->
- **Melanin**: made from **tyrosine** in **melanosomes**; absorbs **UV** light.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c068-->
- **Anthracosis**: inhaled **carbon** carried by macrophages to **hilar lymph nodes**; innocuous on its own.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c069-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- **Hemosiderosis is not hemochromatosis.** The first is iron storage without cell damage; the second is iron overload with organ injury.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c070-->
- The bronze skin of hemochromatosis is mostly melanin: only about half of patients have heavy iron deposits in the skin, whereas in most the basal melanocytes, nonspecifically stimulated by iron, simply make more melanin.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c100-->
- Brown is not always iron: Dubin–Johnson pigment looks like melanin but is iron-free, and hepatocytes do not make melanin.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c071-->
- Melanosis coli is not a melanocytic lesion: the pigment is lipofuscin-like debris of colonic epithelial cells killed by apoptosis under anthraquinone laxatives, then digested by mucosal macrophages.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c072-->
- Transfusional iron seldom causes cirrhosis because it lodges mainly in macrophages, yet massive overload (e.g. thalassemia major) still damages tissues and needs chelation.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c073-->
- A raised serum ferritin can reflect acute inflammation rather than iron excess.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c074-->
- "Wear and tear" does not mean inert: lipofuscin may itself add to oxidant injury.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c075-->

## Mnemonics

**"LIMB-C" — the five pigments to know** (original): **L**ipofuscin, **I**ron (hemosiderin), **M**elanin, **B**ilirubin, **C**arbon.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c076-->

**"Brown? Ask the blue question"** (original): when a pigment is brown, run Perls; blue means iron, no blue means think lipofuscin, melanin, bile or Dubin–Johnson.<!--c:pigment-accumulation-c077-->
