## Definition and epidemiology

Coagulative necrosis is a light-microscopic pattern of cell death in which the dead cells keep their outlines for a while, so the architecture of the tissue can still be recognized.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c001--> Necrosis in general is death imposed on cells by an injury they cannot adapt to, and it usually strikes a contiguous, geographic area rather than scattered single cells.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c002--> Ischemic necrosis is the most important example and underlies myocardial infarction and stroke; it is coagulative in most organs but liquefactive in the brain.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c003--> The label goes back to an old comparison with protein set by heat: the theory behind it has been abandoned, but pathologists keep the word because it describes what they see.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

- **Ischemia** is the main cause: blockage of an end artery produces a zone of coagulative necrosis called an **infarct**.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c005-->
- Other causes of necrosis include physical trauma, burns or frostbite, toxins and infectious agents.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c006-->
- **Radiation:** a dose far beyond 10 Gy kills cells within a short time, and no histologic feature separates the result from coagulative necrosis of other origin.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c007-->
- Partial narrowing of an artery more often causes atrophy and fibrosis from chronic ischemia than outright necrosis.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c008-->

## Pathogenesis

Loss of blood supply starves the cell of oxygen and fuel; ATP falls, ion pumps fail, calcium accumulates and membranes are progressively damaged until the cell passes the point of no return (see [[hypoxic-ischemic-injury]]).<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c009--> Once the cell is dead, its cytoplasmic RNA is degraded quickly, which removes the blue (hematoxylin-binding) component of the cytoplasm.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c010--> For a time the dead cell is not digested, which is why its shape and the tissue plan persist.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c011--> Later, enzymes from the dead cells themselves and from recruited leukocytes break the cells down, especially once acute inflammation has started.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c012--> Necrotic cells release enzymes and mediators that trigger inflammation, in contrast to apoptotic cells, which are removed quietly.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c013-->

## Gross morphology

- **Pale (white) infarcts** are typical of solid organs with a single arterial supply: heart, kidney and spleen.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c014--> Their color changes fast: the swollen, dusky-red zone of the first hours becomes, by day 1–2, a soft pale-yellow area with a crisp margin, framed by a band of hemorrhage against the surviving tissue.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c015-->
- **Kidney:** on cut section the infarct is a pale triangle whose broad side faces the capsule; once healed it leaves a sunken, well-defined notch of cortical scar.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c016-->
- **Heart:** gross and microscopic changes follow a predictable clock, shown in the timeline table under complications and prognosis.
- **Red (hemorrhagic) infarcts** also contain coagulative necrosis, with blood added from neighboring vessels; they follow arterial or venous occlusion and occur in organs with a dual supply, such as the lung, or with rich collaterals, such as the small intestine, and in a reperfused heart.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c019-->
- **Dry gangrene** of a limb after arterial occlusion is in essence a large pale infarct (see [[gangrene]]).<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c020-->

## Microscopic morphology

- **Preserved outlines:** soon after death the cell borders and the tissue architecture are still visible ("ghost" cells).<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c021-->
- **Cytoplasmic eosinophilia:** on H&E the dead cytoplasm is redder than normal because RNA, which stains with hematoxylin, has been lost.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c022-->
- **Nuclear changes:** chromatin first clumps and gathers along the nuclear membrane; the nucleus then shows one or more of three changes.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c023-->
  - **Pyknosis:** the nucleus shrinks and becomes dark and dense.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c024-->
  - **Karyorrhexis:** the shrunken nucleus breaks into many fragments.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c025-->
  - **Karyolysis:** the nucleus fades as its chromatin loses staining, or it is extruded.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c026-->
- **Electron microscopy**, organelle by organelle: the plasma membrane shows blebs or breaks; mitochondria swell and take up calcium deposits; the endoplasmic reticulum is dilated and its ribosomes are dispersed; cytoskeletal filaments clump together.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c027-->
- **Myocardium:** dead myocytes cannot contract, so each systole of the surviving muscle pulls them into undulating "wavy fibers"; their intense eosinophilia is in place within the first day, whereas complete loss of nuclei takes several days.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c028-->
- **Kidney:** in an infarct every structure in the zone, glomeruli and tubules alike, undergoes coagulative necrosis.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c029-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Autopsy:** besides the infarct that caused death, the autopsy can bring silent ones to light: up to a third of nonfatal Q-wave myocardial infarcts give no symptoms and are recognized only later, on the ECG or at autopsy.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c070-->
- **Gross handling:** on transverse slices of the ventricles, the position of a transmural infarct points to the blocked artery: anterior wall, apex and front of the septum for the left anterior descending; posterior basal wall and back of the septum ("inferior" infarct) for the right coronary; lateral wall for the circumflex.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c071--> To date an infarct, sample its border, where living tissue begins and repair starts, because the center of a large infarct matures later.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c072-->
- **H&E:** compare the dead zone with the surviving tissue next to it: in the heart the infarct turns more eosinophilic after about 12–18 hours, and the cells that later reach its border (neutrophils, then macrophages and granulation tissue) tell its age, as in the timeline below.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c073-->
- **Electron microscopy** is needed for the earliest changes: during the first 20–30 minutes of total ischemia the myocyte is still reversibly injured, with sarcoplasmic edema, slight mitochondrial swelling and loss of glycogen.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c074--> After 30–60 minutes the injury is irreversible: mitochondria are markedly swollen, with disorganized cristae and amorphous dense deposits in the matrix; chromatin clumps along the nuclear membrane; the sarcolemma is broken in places.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c075-->
- **Masson trichrome:** collagen stains blue-green, so a healed infarct stands out as a compact, cell-poor scar with a sharp edge against the surviving muscle.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c076-->
- **Calcification:** old renal infarcts and necrotic papillae may calcify; the calcium salts are deep blue with hematoxylin and black with von Kossa, the more specific stain (see [[dystrophic-calcification]]).<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c077-->

## Clinical presentation

The symptoms are those of the infarcted organ.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c030-->

- **Heart:** myocardial infarction after persistent coronary occlusion.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c031-->
- **Kidney:** sudden pain in the flank or abdomen together with blood in the urine is the usual picture of an acute renal infarct; emboli cause most cases, for example from mural thrombi or infected heart valves.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c032-->
- **Renal papilla:** in analgesic nephropathy the papillae undergo coagulative necrosis and may slough, causing colic as fragments pass down the ureter.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c033-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

When the plasma membrane of dead myocytes breaks down, their contents reach the blood: cardiac troponin (I or T), CK, LDH and myoglobin.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c034--> Troponin I or T is the marker measured most, being highly specific for heart muscle; an infarct is diagnosed when its blood level goes up and/or down in step with the clinical episode.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c068--> In tissue, the diagnosis is morphologic on routine H&E: eosinophilic cells with preserved outlines whose nuclei are pyknotic, fragmented or already gone.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c035-->

## Complications and prognosis

**Timeline of a myocardial infarct** (persistent occlusion):

| Time | Naked eye | Microscope |
|---|---|---|
| First 12 hours<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c017--> | No visible lesion yet<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c017--> | — |
| About 24 hours<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c063--> | Pallor on the cut surface<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c063--> | Myocytes strongly eosinophilic, nuclear changes under way<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c036--> |
| Days 2–3 | — | Neutrophils, entering only from the perfused border, reach their peak<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c037--> |
| Days 3–5 | Yellow necrotic center inside a red hyperemic rim<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c064--> | — |
| Days 5–7 | — | Macrophages clear dead muscle; myofibroblasts lay down collagen from the edge inward<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c038--> |
| Weeks 2–3<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c018--> | Sunken, soft, gelatinous area<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c018--> | — |
| After 4 weeks (healed)<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c039--> | Firm, shrunken, gray-white scar<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c065--> | Dense fibrous tissue that grows less cellular as it matures<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c039--> |

Other outcomes:

- **Rupture:** the necrotic wall is weakest between days 2 and 7, when inflammatory proteases have digested its matrix and new collagen has not yet been laid down; free-wall rupture of a transmural infarct is most frequent in this window and usually kills by hemopericardium and tamponade.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c067-->
- The center of a large infarct may stay unreached by leukocytes for months, and dead myocytes can persist "mummified"; the age of a large infarct is best judged at its border.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c040-->
- Old necrotic areas, for example healed renal infarcts or necrotic papillae, often undergo [[dystrophic-calcification]].<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c041-->
- If the dead tissue becomes infected, the result is a septic infarct, which may turn into an abscess.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c042-->
- In the brain, coagulative necrosis is quickly followed by liquefaction, which can leave a lifelong cyst.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c043-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[liquefactive-necrosis]] | Dead tissue is dissolved into fluid (abscess, brain infarct); outlines are lost.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c044--> | Cavity with neutrophils or debris, no ghost outlines |
| [[caseous-necrosis]] | Outlines lost, replaced by granular eosinophilic debris inside granulomas; cheese-like on gross view.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c045--> | Granulomas; stain for mycobacteria |
| [[fat-necrosis]] | Affects adipose tissue, mostly after pancreatitis or trauma; released fatty acids bind calcium and form soaps, seen grossly as chalky white foci.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c066--> | Site in fat; basophilic calcium-soap deposits |
| [[apoptosis]] | Single cells or small groups among viable cells, membrane-bound fragments, no inflammation.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c046--> | Pattern: scattered single cells, no neutrophils |
| [[hydropic-swelling]] | Reversible injury: swollen pale cell with an intact nucleus.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c047--> | Normal nucleus on H&E |
| [[fibrinoid-necrosis]] | Bright eosinophilic deposit of plasma proteins in an injured vessel wall.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c048--> | Location in the vessel wall |
| Reperfused infarct ([[ischemia-reperfusion-injury]]) | Blood flow returns to an evolving infarct: it is hemorrhagic from the start, neutrophils spread through all of it instead of stopping at the edge, and contraction bands (thick, irregular cross-bands of hypercontracted sarcomeres) are widespread, whereas with persistent occlusion they are at most confined to the margins.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c069--> | Hemorrhage and contraction bands throughout the infarct |

## High-yield summary

- Hallmark: **preserved cell outlines** and tissue architecture in dead tissue.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c049-->
- **Eosinophilia** mainly reflects **loss of cytoplasmic RNA**, the hematoxylin-binding component.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c050-->
- Nuclear changes: **pyknosis** first; the shrunken nucleus may then fragment (**karyorrhexis**) or fade (**karyolysis**).<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c051-->
- Typical cause: **ischemia** of an end-artery territory, producing an **infarct**.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c052-->
- **Pale infarcts** occur in **heart, kidney and spleen**; renal infarcts are **wedge-shaped** with the base on the capsule.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c053-->
- The **brain** is the exception: ischemic necrosis there turns **liquefactive**.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c054-->
- Healing: **neutrophils**, then **macrophages**, **granulation tissue** and finally a **scar**, starting from the border.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c055-->
- Old necrotic tissue can undergo **dystrophic calcification**.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c056-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- Coagulative necrosis has nothing to do with blood clotting; the name refers to the look of heat-denatured protein.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c057-->
- A myocardial infarct in its first hours looks normal to the naked eye; absence of a gross lesion does not exclude early infarction.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c058-->
- Red infarcts are not a different type of necrosis: they are coagulative necrosis plus hemorrhage.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c059-->
- The necrosis of tuberculosis is caseous, not coagulative: inside the granuloma the cell outlines vanish, yet the debris is not dissolved and stays for a long time.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c060-->
- Pyknosis and nuclear fragmentation also occur in apoptosis; the key differences are scale (geographic area versus single cells) and inflammation.<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c061-->

## Mnemonics

**GHOST** (original): **G**eographic area of dead cells, **H**ematoxylin lost so the cytoplasm turns pink, **O**utlines and architecture preserved, **S**hrink–shatter–fade of the nucleus (pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis), **T**erritory of an end artery in a solid organ (heart, kidney, spleen).<!--c:coagulative-necrosis-c062-->
