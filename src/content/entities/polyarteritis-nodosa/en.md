## Definition and epidemiology

**What is polyarteritis nodosa?** The target is the muscular artery of medium size or smaller, and the inflammation is acute and necrotizing.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c001--> Larger arteries are occasionally involved too.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c002--> Women develop it less often than men.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What triggers it?** Animal-derived antisera and sulfonamides came into wide use in the 1940s, and the disease rose sharply in frequency at the same time, which suggested a link.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c004--> Its incidence now appears to be falling.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c005--> In people infected with hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus or HIV, lesions resembling polyarteritis nodosa may develop.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c006-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does the arterial wall get damaged?** The reference pages give no mechanism for classic polyarteritis nodosa, so none is stated here.

## Gross morphology

**What do the arteries and organs look like?** Arterial thrombosis often leads to infarcts in the organs supplied.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c007--> When larger arteries are injured, small aneurysms mostly form on branches to the brain, heart and kidney.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c008-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the lesion look like on a slide?** A small or medium-sized muscular artery shows a patchy focus of [[fibrinoid-necrosis|fibrinoid necrosis]].<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c009--> The focus may ring the whole vessel or only part of it.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c010--> Structureless pink (eosinophilic) material fills the area, formed by the dead media together with the tissue next to it.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c011--> The adventitia is usually inflamed throughout (periarteritis), and acute inflammation around the necrosis reaches the deeper wall layers too.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c012--> The cell mix varies: macrophages, plasma cells, lymphocytes and neutrophils occur, and eosinophils can be prominent.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c013-->

**What happens as time passes?** Many lesions begin to repair with time, and corticosteroids make this especially likely.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c014--> The healed vessel has a fibrotic media and clear gaps in the elastic laminae, once dead tissue and exudate have been cleared.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c015-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** on the slide, look for fibrinoid necrosis of the arterial wall with inflammation around it.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c016-->
- **Fibrin:** the necrotic material is positive on fibrin stains.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c017-->
- **Healed segments:** look for medial fibrosis and gaps in the elastic laminae.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c018-->

The reference pages do not cover specimen choice, immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy or molecular tests for this disease.

## Clinical presentation

**Why are the symptoms so variable?** The picture depends on which organs the lesions reach.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c019--> The kidneys and heart are common targets, as are the skin, mesentery and skeletal muscle.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c020--> Lesions can occur almost anywhere, for example in the gut, brain or liver.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c021--> Patients often have fever and weight loss.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c022-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which tests establish the diagnosis?** The reference pages list no key laboratory or imaging tests for this disease, so none are stated here. The tissue findings are in the microscopic section.

## Complications and prognosis

**What is the outlook?** Untreated disease was usually fatal.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c023--> Remission or cure is usual with treatment that suppresses inflammation and immunity, such as corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c024--> Fatal hemorrhage can follow if an aneurysm ruptures in a critical site.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c025-->

## High-yield summary

- Target: **muscular arteries** of medium size or smaller; larger ones only occasionally.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c033-->
- Lesion: patchy **fibrinoid necrosis** with **periarteritis**.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c034-->
- Consequence: **thrombosis** causes **organ infarcts**, and small **aneurysms** can rupture.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c035-->
- Link: PAN-like lesions complicate **hepatitis B**, hepatitis C and HIV infection.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c036-->
- Common sites: **kidneys**, **heart**, skeletal muscle, skin and mesentery; fever and weight loss are common.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c037-->
- Treatment: untreated disease was usually fatal; **corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide** usually induce remission.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c038-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is polyarteritis nodosa a disease of small vessels only?** No. Larger arteries are sometimes involved as well, besides medium-sized and smaller muscular ones.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c039-->

**Is it the same as microscopic polyangiitis?** No. Only the smallest arteries and arterioles are affected in microscopic polyangiitis, which is strongly associated with P-ANCA.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c040-->

**Is every vessel ring destroyed?** No. The wall may be involved all round or in only part of its circumference.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c041-->

**Is hepatitis B the only infection linked to it?** No. Hepatitis C virus and HIV can also be complicated by PAN-like lesions.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c042-->

**Is it still a fatal disease?** Untreated, it usually was; with corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide remission or cure is usual.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c043-->

## Mnemonics

**PAN:** **P**atchy fibrinoid necrosis, **A**rteries of medium size, **N**ecrotizing inflammation.<!--c:polyarteritis-nodosa-c044-->
