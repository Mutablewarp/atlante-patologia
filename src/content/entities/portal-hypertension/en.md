## Definition and epidemiology

**What is portal hypertension?** Blood meets resistance somewhere in the portal circuit, and pressure in the portal vein rises as a result.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c001--> Collateral routes to the systemic veins open, the spleen swells, and the kidneys and lungs may suffer.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Where can the obstruction sit?** Before the sinusoids, inside them, or after them.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c005-->
- **Presinusoidal:** cirrhosis is the commonest setting of portal vein thrombosis; tumors and infections also cause it.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c006-->
- **Sinusoidal (intrahepatic):** cirrhosis, which in Western countries causes more cases than any other disease.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c008-->
- **Postsinusoidal:** thrombosis of the hepatic veins (Budd–Chiari syndrome).<!--c:portal-hypertension-c009-->

## Pathogenesis

**Why does pressure rise in cirrhosis?** Resistance starts early: contracting stellate cells and smooth muscle narrow the way in for portal blood before fibrosis has formed.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c013--> Later, nodules squeeze the hepatic veins and portal scars choke small venules, blocking flow through the liver.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c014-->

**Why does it get worse?** Portal resistance is already climbing when widened mesenteric arteries add to the inflow.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c016--> Advanced disease is driven by low effective arterial blood volume, which produces the hepatorenal and hepatopulmonary syndromes as well as ascites.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c017-->

**How does ascites form?** The kidney holds on to sodium and water, driven by rising renin–angiotensin, sympathetic and antidiuretic hormone activity that defends arterial pressure when effective volume is low.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c018--> Fluid is pushed into the peritoneum where high sinusoidal pressure meets low albumin.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c019-->

## Gross morphology

**What do the organs look like?** In acute hepatic vein thrombosis the liver is enlarged and tight; sectioning shows a blotchy surface that bleeds.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c021--> A firm, enlarged spleen is deep red throughout on section.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c023--> Varices are widened submucosal collaterals of the lower esophagus that bulge inward.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c024-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What is seen under the microscope?** In acute Budd-Chiari syndrome, central hepatocytes die and the adjacent sinusoids balloon with trapped blood.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c027--> In prolonged congestion, scars join adjacent central zones, so nodules form around one portal tract: reverse lobulation.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c028-->

## Clinical presentation

**What does the patient develop?**
- **Varices:** they bleed into the upper gastrointestinal tract, sometimes massively, and are the leading complication.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c032-->
- **Splenomegaly:** hypersplenism with pancytopenia often develops as the spleen enlarges.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c033-->
- **Ascites:** the abdomen swells because fluid, sometimes many liters, collects in the peritoneal cavity.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c034-->
- **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis:** ascitic fluid becomes infected when gut bacteria, mainly *Escherichia coli* and *Klebsiella*, translocate.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c035-->
- **Hepatorenal syndrome** mostly arises in cirrhosis: underfilled kidneys make little urine, and creatinine and nitrogenous waste build up.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c037-->
- **Hepatopulmonary syndrome:** chest radiographs are usually normal despite progressive dyspnea from intrapulmonary shunts.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c039-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is portal pressure measured?** A balloon catheter passed from the jugular vein into a hepatic vein gives both readings; the HVPG is wedged minus free pressure.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c041--> In postsinusoidal block the HVPG is usually normal, whereas sinusoidal block usually raises it.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c042-->

**What do ascitic fluid and labs add?** In ascites caused by cirrhotic portal hypertension, the serum-ascites albumin gradient exceeds 1.1.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c044--> Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis is diagnosed when ascitic fluid holds over 250 neutrophils/μL.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c045--> Hepatorenal syndrome requires creatinine above 1.5 mg/dL that persists once diuretics are stopped and volume is restored.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c046-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How serious is it?** Up to 40% of patients may die during an acute variceal bleed.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c047--> Hepatorenal kidney dysfunction is reversible: kidney function returns after liver transplantation.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c054--> Only transplantation effectively treats hepatopulmonary syndrome.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c056-->

## High-yield summary

- **Presinusoidal, sinusoidal and postsinusoidal** blocks are the three sites of raised resistance.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c062-->
- **Budd–Chiari syndrome** is hepatic vein thrombosis and gives ascites with splenomegaly.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c064-->
- **Esophageal varices** top the list of complications.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c065-->
- Splanchnic vasodilation lowers effective arterial volume, and **ascites** and the **hepatorenal** and **hepatopulmonary syndromes** follow.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c066-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is HVPG raised in every portal hypertension?** No. It is usually normal in postsinusoidal block.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c067-->

**Is hepatorenal syndrome kidney disease?** No. The kidneys are intact and work in grafted recipients with chronic renal failure.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c068-->

## Mnemonics

**How do I remember the three sites?** PRE-CLOT, SIN-SCAR, POST-BACK: clot in the portal vein, scar in the sinusoids, back-pressure from the hepatic veins or heart.<!--c:portal-hypertension-c070-->
