## Definition and epidemiology

Necroptosis is a form of regulated necrosis: the cell activates a dedicated signaling program to kill itself, yet the result looks like ordinary necrosis.<!--c:necroptosis-c001--> It borrows part of its signaling machinery from apoptosis while keeping the necrotic appearance.<!--c:necroptosis-c002--> Its execution does not need caspases, which is why it still works when caspases are blocked.<!--c:necroptosis-c003--> It is counted with apoptosis and autophagy among the main forms of programmed cell death, although the role of autophagy as a killer is debated.<!--c:necroptosis-c004--> It also has normal roles, in adult tissues (for instance at the bottom of the intestinal crypts) and during development, above all at the growth plate of bone.<!--c:necroptosis-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

- **Death receptors:** most often a ligand such as TNFα or Fas ligand sets it off by binding a receptor of the TNF superfamily, for example TNFR1.<!--c:necroptosis-c006-->
- **Other immune receptors:** Toll-like receptors and interferon receptors can also trigger it.<!--c:necroptosis-c007-->
- **Viral infection:** the infected cell can still kill itself by this caspase-free route when the virus makes caspase inhibitors, as many viruses do.<!--c:necroptosis-c008-->
- **Blocked apoptosis:** necroptosis serves as a backup route to death when the apoptotic program cannot run, a point of interest for tumor cells that have switched apoptosis off.<!--c:necroptosis-c009-->
- **Metastasis:** circulating tumor cells cross the vessel wall more easily when TGFβ released by platelets drives endothelial cells into necroptosis.<!--c:necroptosis-c010-->

## Pathogenesis

**Building the necrosome.**

1. With TNFR1, the engaged receptor recruits the adaptor TRADD.<!--c:necroptosis-c011-->
2. The **necrosome** forms next: a filament-shaped signaling complex of RIP1 (RIPK1) and RIP3 (RIPK3), the receptor-interacting proteins that TRADD recruits.<!--c:necroptosis-c012-->
3. RIP3 is phosphorylated and becomes the hub from which several damaging paths branch out.<!--c:necroptosis-c013-->

**Executing the cell.**

- **MLKL:** the plasma membrane and the membranes of organelles lose their integrity when MLKL, switched on by the necrosome, inserts into them.<!--c:necroptosis-c014-->
- **Calcium:** partly via CaMKII, RIP3 raises the Ca2+ level in the cytosol; the extra calcium turns on calcium-dependent enzymes such as calpains, which break down cell components.<!--c:necroptosis-c015-->
- **Lysosomes:** these enzymes make lysosomal membranes leaky, so hydrolases reach the cytosol and digest macromolecules.<!--c:necroptosis-c016-->
- **Mitochondria:** once injured by calpain, they set iron free and can no longer make enough ATP.<!--c:necroptosis-c017-->
- **Iron and ROS:** the freed iron drives the Fenton and Haber–Weiss reactions, and the reactive oxygen species they produce oxidize the cell's macromolecules, DNA included.<!--c:necroptosis-c018-->
- **AIF:** DNA breakdown in the nucleus, and with it pyknosis, is started by apoptosis-inducing factor, which leaves the damaged mitochondria and moves into the nucleus.<!--c:necroptosis-c019-->
- **End point:** energy collapses, the cell takes on a necrotic appearance and spills damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that call in inflammation.<!--c:necroptosis-c020-->

**The switch with apoptosis.**

- Caspase-8, an initiator caspase of extrinsic apoptosis, cleaves RIP1 and thereby stops the necrosome from forming.<!--c:necroptosis-c021-->
- Whether a cell takes the apoptotic or the necroptotic route depends on several overlapping layers of control.<!--c:necroptosis-c022-->
- The same TNF receptor can also send survival signals through NFκB, so its outcome depends on context.<!--c:necroptosis-c023-->

## Microscopic morphology

- By light microscopy, a necroptotic cell looks exactly like a necrotic one: a pyknotic nucleus in a swollen cell whose plasma membrane is breaking up.<!--c:necroptosis-c024-->
- An inflammatory reaction follows the cell death.<!--c:necroptosis-c025-->
- Apoptosis looks different: no inflammatory infiltrate gathers, the nucleus breaks into pieces and the cell surface forms blebs.<!--c:necroptosis-c026-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **H&E:** in a routine section necroptotic cells look like necrotic ones (see Microscopic morphology), so H&E can show a necrotic type of death but cannot prove that it was necroptosis.<!--c:necroptosis-c050-->
- **What proves it:** in its 2018 recommendations, the Nomenclature Committee on Cell Death defines each form of regulated cell death by its molecular machinery rather than by its appearance; necroptosis is the form that depends on RIP3 and MLKL.<!--c:necroptosis-c051-->
- **Immunostaining:** RIP3 activates MLKL by phosphorylating it, so phosphorylated MLKL flags cells on their way to necroptotic death; in humans it has been shown in drug-induced liver injury, where liver biopsies stained with a monoclonal antibody specific for the phosphorylated form are positive.<!--c:necroptosis-c052--> When checked against knockout cells, some popular antibodies to RIP1, RIP3, MLKL or their phosphorylated forms proved unfit for immunofluorescence.<!--c:necroptosis-c053-->
- **Experimental tests:** necrostatin-1, an inhibitor of RIP1, blocks necroptosis triggered through TNFR1, and necroptosis also fails when MLKL is removed genetically or inhibited with necrosulfonamide.<!--c:necroptosis-c054-->

## Clinical presentation

Necroptosis has no clinical picture of its own; it contributes to the mechanism of several diseases:

- **Viral infection:** killing the infected cell without caspases limits the spread of the virus.<!--c:necroptosis-c027-->
- **Inflammatory disease:** it is involved in Crohn disease and pancreatitis.<!--c:necroptosis-c028-->
- **Myocardial infarction:** it is active during the post-infarction inflammatory phase.<!--c:necroptosis-c029-->
- **Cancer:** tumor cells that have lost apoptosis can still be eliminated through this caspase-independent route.<!--c:necroptosis-c030-->

## Complications and prognosis

Beyond the loss of the cell itself, necroptosis causes inflammation: triggered by the DAMPs that ruptured cells release, it can, as in any necrosis, also injure the living cells around the dead ones.<!--c:necroptosis-c031-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[apoptosis]] | Caspase-driven; it causes no inflammation, and the blebbing cell splits up into apoptotic bodies.<!--c:necroptosis-c032--> | Morphology and caspase dependence |
| [[coagulative-necrosis]] | Accidental, unprogrammed death from external injury, usually ischemia; it looks the same under the microscope.<!--c:necroptosis-c033--> | Pathway markers such as phosphorylated MLKL, not H&E |
| [[pyroptosis]] | Inflammatory death driven by caspase-1 activated in the inflammasome after intracellular sensors detect microbial molecules.<!--c:necroptosis-c034--> | Caspase-1 versus RIP1/RIP3–MLKL |
| [[autophagy]] | Mainly a survival response to stress, based on lysosomal self-digestion; an independent killing role has not been established.<!--c:necroptosis-c035--> | Survival process versus membrane rupture |

## High-yield summary

- Necroptosis = **programmed necrosis**: a signaled death with **necrotic morphology** and **inflammation**.<!--c:necroptosis-c036-->
- Triggers: **TNFR1** and other **death receptors**, **Toll-like receptors**; **viral infection**, where **viral caspase inhibitors** unmask it.<!--c:necroptosis-c037-->
- Via TNFR1: **TRADD** → **RIP1 + RIP3** = **necrosome** → **MLKL** breaches the membranes.<!--c:necroptosis-c038-->
- Downstream damage: **Ca2+/CaMKII → calpains**, lysosomal rupture, **iron-driven ROS**, **AIF** → DNA breakdown, **bioenergetic crisis**.<!--c:necroptosis-c039-->
- **Caspase-8 cleaves RIP1** and so blocks necroptosis.<!--c:necroptosis-c040-->
- Disease links: **Crohn disease**, **pancreatitis**, **post-infarction inflammation**, viral infection and cancer.<!--c:necroptosis-c041-->
- Normal roles: **bone growth plate** and **deep intestinal crypts**.<!--c:necroptosis-c042-->
- Proof: **H&E** shows only necrosis; the pathway itself is revealed by markers such as **phosphorylated MLKL**.<!--c:necroptosis-c055-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- **"Programmed" does not mean "apoptotic":** necroptosis is programmed, but it needs no caspases and it is inflammatory.<!--c:necroptosis-c043-->
- **H&E cannot separate it from accidental necrosis;** the two differ in mechanism, not in appearance.<!--c:necroptosis-c044-->
- **Pyroptosis is a relative, not a synonym:** pyroptosis runs on caspase-1 in the inflammasome, whereas necroptosis bypasses caspases.<!--c:necroptosis-c045-->
- **Not only pathologic:** it occurs normally at the growth plate and in intestinal crypts.<!--c:necroptosis-c046-->
- **Blocking caspases does not guarantee survival:** caspase-8 restrains necroptosis, so when caspases are shut down, as viral inhibitors do, the cell can still die by this route.<!--c:necroptosis-c047-->

## Mnemonics

**"Two RIPs and a rip"** (original): RIP1 and RIP3 build the necrosome, and MLKL rips the membrane open.<!--c:necroptosis-c048-->

**"Caspase-8 cuts the cord"** (original): active caspase-8 cuts RIP1 and the cell leaves by the apoptotic exit; when caspases are silenced, the necroptotic exit opens.<!--c:necroptosis-c049-->
