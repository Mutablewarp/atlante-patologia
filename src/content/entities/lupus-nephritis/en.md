## Definition and epidemiology

**What is lupus nephritis?** It is inflammation of the kidney caused by immune complexes deposited in the glomeruli of patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c001-->
Of all the complications SLE can bring, nephritis figures among the most frequent.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c002-->
Seventy percent of SLE patients end up with kidney involvement.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c003-->
Kidney disease is frequently what makes SLE most dangerous to health and life.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Who gets lupus nephritis, and what drives the autoimmunity?** Women of reproductive age are the group most often affected by SLE.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c005-->
Compared with white patients, those who are Hispanic, Asian or Black generally have more severe disease.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c006-->
Hyperactive B cells make autoantibodies against many antigens, nuclear ones such as DNA among them.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c007-->
When apoptosis is faulty and clearance slow, leftover chromatin can supply nuclear antigens.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c008-->

## Pathogenesis

**How do immune complexes injure the glomerulus?** Glomerular complexes can be delivered by the blood, assembled on site (in situ), or arise by both routes.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c009-->
Antibodies that bind tightly form complexes in the circulation, which settle in the mesangium and under the endothelium.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c010-->
In the subepithelial zone, complexes form on the spot from antibodies of low affinity.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c011-->
Charge attracts double-stranded DNA and nucleosomes to the basement membrane and mesangial matrix, where they can anchor complexes formed in place.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c012-->
Inflammation begins when complexes in the glomerulus switch on complement.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c013-->

Where the complexes sit decides the damage:

- Compared with subendothelial deposits, mesangial ones provoke less inflammation.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c014-->
- Because subendothelial complexes sit where blood-borne inflammatory mediators can reach them, they are more likely to start inflammation.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c015-->
- Subepithelial deposits cause proteinuria, yet the glomerulus does not become overtly inflamed.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c016-->

Outside the glomeruli, complexes lodge in the interstitium and around the tubules, which may explain the tubulointerstitial inflammation.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c017-->

## Microscopic morphology

**How do the classes of lupus nephritis differ?**

- **Class I, minimal mesangial:** light microscopy shows no change, because the complexes are confined to the mesangium.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c018-->
- **Class II, mesangial proliferative:** the mesangium becomes hypercellular and its matrix expands, to a varying degree, from complexes still confined there.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c019-->
- **Class III, focal:** complexes under the endothelium and in the mesangium trigger inflammation and cell proliferation in fewer than 50% of glomeruli.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c020-->
- **Class IV, diffuse:** more than half of the glomeruli carry the class III lesion.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c021-->
- Within class IV, IV-G marks mostly global glomerular damage and IV-S marks mostly segmental damage.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c022-->
- Segmental necrosis is more prominent in class IV-S, which tends to carry fewer complexes.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c023-->
- **Class V, membranous:** most complexes sit on the subepithelial side.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c024-->
- Class V can occur together with class III or IV.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c025-->
- **Class VI, advanced sclerosing:** advanced chronic disease.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c026-->

<aside class="edition">The classification changed between editions, and the cut-offs above come from one edition of the reference. Check them against the current scheme.</aside>

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**

- **How it is studied:** the specimen is a kidney biopsy from the patient with lupus.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c027-->
- Immunofluorescence: band-like or granular staining marks subendothelial deposits, while subepithelial complexes appear granular.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c028-->
- Nearly all cases carry C1q, C3, IgA and IgM alongside IgG, which is usually the strongest.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c029-->
- Over half of cases also stain granularly in tubular basement membranes and interstitial vessels.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c030-->
- On electron microscopy, dense deposits can be found beneath the epithelium, beneath the endothelium and in the mesangium.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c031-->
- In classes I and II the deposits are chiefly mesangial.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c032-->
- Subepithelial deposits are usually scattered in classes III and IV-G, which also have mesangial and subendothelial deposits.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c033-->
- Class V has many subepithelial deposits.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c034-->
- Electron microscopy can detect mesangial complexes even in pure class V.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c035-->
- A high interferon level is reflected by tubuloreticular inclusions in endothelial cytoplasm, which electron microscopy finds in 80% of cases.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c036-->
- Among kidney diseases, these inclusions are chiefly a feature of lupus nephritis and HIV-associated nephropathy.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c037-->
- Special stains and molecular tests: the reference pages for this card do not describe any.

## Clinical presentation

**What does the patient have in each class?**

- Classes I and II: mild hematuria and proteinuria.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c038-->
- Class III: moderate nephritis.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c039-->
- Class IV: severe nephritis.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c040-->
- Class V: nephrotic syndrome.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c041-->
- Class VI: chronic renal failure.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c042-->

Which lesion the kidney has determines how the patient presents and fares.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c043-->
Symptoms shift together with the lesion when a patient moves to another class, which can happen over time and sometimes because of treatment.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c044-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which tests settle the diagnosis?** A kidney biopsy confirms lupus glomerulonephritis and gives its class, activity and chronicity.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c045-->
Complement is consumed by the immune complexes, so serum complement is often low (hypocomplementemia).<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c046-->

## Complications and prognosis

**Which classes do worst?** The worst outcomes belong to classes III and IV.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c047-->
Therapy is most aggressive here, typically immunosuppressants plus high-dose steroids.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c048-->
End-stage renal disease develops within 5 years in fewer than 20% of class IV patients.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c049-->

## High-yield summary

- **Class III** is focal (fewer than 50% of glomeruli); **class IV** is diffuse (more than 50%).<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c053-->
- **Class V** is membranous: subepithelial deposits and **nephrotic syndrome**.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c054-->
- **Classes III and IV** have the poorest prognosis.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c055-->
- Immunofluorescence shows **IgG** with IgA, IgM, C3 and **C1q** all present.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c056-->
- **Tubuloreticular inclusions** on electron microscopy reflect high interferon.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c057-->
- **Subendothelial** deposits drive inflammation; **subepithelial** deposits drive proteinuria.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c058-->
- The biopsy grades **activity** and **chronicity**.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c059-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a normal light-microscopy biopsy rule out lupus nephritis?** No. Class I looks normal by light microscopy but still has mesangial complexes.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c060-->

**Is class IV just a milder class III?** No. It is the more extensive one, with more than 50% of glomeruli involved.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c061-->

**Does a patient stay in one class?** No. The class can change over time, sometimes because of treatment.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c062-->

**Is IgG the only immunoglobulin on immunofluorescence?** No. Lupus deposits nearly always contain IgA and IgM as well, plus C3 and C1q.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c063-->

**Does class V mean subepithelial deposits only?** No. Mesangial complexes are present even in pure class V.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c064-->

## Mnemonics

**Two, two, one, one.** Classes I and II stay in the mesangium, III and IV inflame the glomerulus, V is membranous, VI is the scar.<!--c:lupus-nephritis-c065-->
