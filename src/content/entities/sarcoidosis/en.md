## Definition and epidemiology

**What is sarcoidosis?** Most often it is the lung that is involved in this granulomatous disease, whose cause remains unknown.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c001--> Beyond the lung, it often reaches the heart, eyes, skin and lymph nodes.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c002-->

Men and women of every region are affected, but ethnic origin strongly shifts the risk.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c003--> In North America, Black people carry a much higher risk than white people.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c004--> Prevalence is also higher in Scandinavia than in France or Poland.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c005--> Young adults are the typical patients.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c006-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes sarcoidosis?** The cause is still unknown.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c007--> Much of the evidence points to a defect of immune regulation.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c008--> Even with highly sensitive techniques, no infectious agent has ever been found.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c009--> Immune-gene variants, inborn or acquired, may steer which T-cell response prevails.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c010-->

## Pathogenesis

**Why do granulomas form?** Helper T cells respond too strongly to foreign or self antigens, a view most experts share.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c011--> Macrophages drawn to organs packed with helper T cells and their lymphokines help build the noncaseating granulomas (see [[granulomatous-inflammation]]).<!--c:sarcoidosis-c012--> Uninvolved tissue has twice as many CD4+ as CD8+ cells, whereas in organs with granulomas the ratio is 10:1.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c013--> Why helper cells build up is unknown; failing suppressor cells may leave their growth unchecked.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c014--> Active disease typically shows hyperglobulinemia; helper T cells switch on B cells indiscriminately.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c015-->

## Gross morphology

**Which organs are involved?** The lungs and hilar lymph nodes are hit most often, and either can be diseased alone.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c016--> Larger nodules occur in a few patients, but the typical chest radiograph shows a diffuse reticulonodular infiltrate.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c017--> The base of the septum between the ventricles is where granulomas most heavily injure heart muscle.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c018-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the biopsy show?** A sarcoid granuloma packs epithelioid macrophages with multinucleated giant cells and has no caseous necrosis.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c019--> Granulomas lie in the interstitium, favoring the bronchovascular bundles and the lobular septa and pleura.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c020--> A bronchoscopic biopsy often succeeds because granulomas commonly lie in the airway wall beneath the lining.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c021--> Necrosis of any real extent is uncommon.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c022--> In the interstitium, chronic inflammation is usually inconspicuous.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c023--> Open lung biopsies often show vessels invaded by granulomas (granulomatous vasculitis).<!--c:sarcoidosis-c024-->

Both asteroid bodies (stellate crystals) and Schaumann bodies (laminated calcium) are frequent findings, yet neither identifies sarcoidosis.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c025--> Onion-skin rings of collagen can enclose the giant cells as a granuloma turns fibrotic, usually starting at its edge.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c026--> In severe cardiac sarcoidosis, the myocardium ends up scarred and depleted of myocytes, with granulomas running through it.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c027-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**

- **How it is studied:** granulomas are often found in samples taken by transbronchial biopsy with a fiberoptic bronchoscope.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c028-->
- Several noncaseating granulomas in a mediastinal node taken at mediastinoscopy sometimes provide the diagnosis.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c029-->
- Under the microscope, look for compact noncaseating granulomas, sometimes with asteroid bodies.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c030-->
- A larger proportion of CD4+ T lymphocytes is often found in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c031-->
- On a gallium-67 scan, granulomatous areas light up because activated macrophages take up the tracer.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c032-->

The reference pages used here describe no special stain, immunohistochemical or molecular test for sarcoidosis.

## Clinical presentation

**How does sarcoidosis present?** Steroids work very well in acute disease, which begins suddenly and often clears without treatment.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c033--> The chronic form creeps in slowly and tends to last or worsen.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c034--> The respiratory complaints are chiefly cough and breathlessness.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c035--> A chest film taken for another reason sometimes reveals the disease in a patient with no symptoms.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c036--> The textbook chest film shows both hila enlarged by nodes, sometimes with lung infiltrates too.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c037-->

Women are the main patients when the skin is involved in the form of erythema nodosum.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c038--> Black patients tend toward more serious uveitis, and their skin and lacrimal gland disease is worse.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c039--> Outside the chest, the central nervous system, uvea, liver, spleen and lymph nodes are often involved.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c040-->

Granulomas at the septal base endanger the AV conduction pathways, which are largely located there.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c041--> Patients may die suddenly, or develop heart block or ventricular tachyarrhythmias.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c042--> The heart is frequently affected and occasionally is the sole site.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c043--> Heart granulomas at autopsy far outnumber cases with cardiac symptoms.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c044-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is the diagnosis made?** Sarcoidosis has no specific laboratory marker.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c045--> Serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) is often raised when the disease is active.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c046--> Urinary calcium, measured over 24 hours, is frequently increased.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c047--> With supporting clinical and radiologic findings, such data make the diagnosis highly probable.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c048--> Chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis also has noncaseating granulomas; subtle cases need clinical and radiographic correlation.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c049-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What is the outlook?** Most patients with lung sarcoidosis keep no important damage, so the outlook is good.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c050--> In most of them the lung disease clears up.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c051--> Resolution is less likely with disease outside the chest, especially in bone and skin, or with older age.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c052--> Death from the disease is uncommon, although some patients never go into remission or relapse repeatedly.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c053--> Respiratory failure and cor pulmonale are the late consequences when progressive scarring turns the lung into a honeycomb.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c054--> Corticosteroids work well when the disease is active.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c055-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What else produces granulomas or similar lung and heart changes?**

| [[myocarditis]] | Some cases of granulomatous myocarditis are infections, with mycobacteria or certain fungi as the agents.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c058--> A rapidly fatal course and numerous giant cells without any granulomas distinguish giant cell myocarditis.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c059--> | Heart histology with clinical correlation |
| [[idiopathic-pulmonary-fibrosis]] | Fibrosis is worst in the lower lobes and beneath the pleura, and it is patchy.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c060--> In sarcoidosis the interstitium is not much scarred.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c061--> | On CT, many patients show honeycombing and reticular opacities at the lung edge, mostly low and posterior.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c062--> |

## High-yield summary

- The lesion is the **noncaseating granuloma** of epithelioid macrophages and giant cells.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c063-->
- The **lung and hilar nodes** are the classic targets, with **bilateral hilar adenopathy** on the chest film.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c064-->
- Extrapulmonary sites include **skin**, **eye** (uveal tract) and **heart**.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c065-->
- In the heart, granulomas at the septal base cause **heart block** and ventricular arrhythmias.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c066-->
- The mechanism is an exaggerated **CD4+ helper T-cell** response, with hyperglobulinemia.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c067-->
- **No test is specific**: serum **ACE** and urine calcium are often raised.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c068-->
- **Acute** disease remits spontaneously and responds to steroids; **chronic** disease tends to persist.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c069-->
- The prognosis is usually favorable, and **corticosteroids** treat active disease.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c070-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Do sarcoid granulomas show caseous necrosis?** No. They are noncaseating, and significant necrosis is uncommon.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c071-->

**Are asteroid and Schaumann bodies specific for sarcoidosis?** No. They are common in it, but most other granulomatous diseases can show them too.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c072-->

**Is sarcoidosis only a lung disease?** No. The lung is hit most often, but it is a systemic disease: nodes, skin, eye and heart are frequently involved too.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c073-->

**Does a biopsy with granulomas prove sarcoidosis?** No. Other granulomatous diseases, such as chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis, look similar, so the biopsy must be read with clinical, radiologic and laboratory data.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c074-->

## Mnemonics

**HILAR:** Hilar nodes, Immune (CD4+ helper T cells), Lung first, ACE raised, Resolves in most patients.<!--c:sarcoidosis-c075-->
