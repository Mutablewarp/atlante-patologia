## Definition and epidemiology

**What is cholecystitis?** When the gallbladder outlet is blocked, as is typical, the whole organ becomes inflamed: this is acute cholecystitis.<!--c:cholecystitis-c001--> No other gallbladder disease is as common as chronic cholecystitis, a long-lasting inflammation of the organ.<!--c:cholecystitis-c002--> Stones are found in 90% of acute cases.<!--c:cholecystitis-c003--> Acute cholecystitis without stones is called acalculous and makes up the rest.<!--c:cholecystitis-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What sets off cholecystitis?** Acalculous acute cases occur with [[polyarteritis-nodosa]], gallbladder infection with *Salmonella typhosa*, sepsis or severe trauma.<!--c:cholecystitis-c005--> Obstruction of bile flow normally comes first, and bacterial infection follows it.<!--c:cholecystitis-c006--> Gallstones ([[cholelithiasis]]) accompany chronic cholecystitis in nearly every case.<!--c:cholecystitis-c007--> Repeated bouts of acute cholecystitis can also lead to it.<!--c:cholecystitis-c008-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does a stone injure the gallbladder wall?** The gallbladder lining may secrete phospholipase when a stone lodges in the cystic duct.<!--c:cholecystitis-c009--> This enzyme makes lysolecithin, a toxin acting on cell membranes, out of lecithin.<!--c:cholecystitis-c010--> Once the mucus layer is lost, concentrated bile salts act on the exposed cells like a detergent.<!--c:cholecystitis-c011--> Excess cholesterol in the bile can injure the epithelium.<!--c:cholecystitis-c012--> After repeated acute attacks, the likely mechanism is lasting epithelial damage from irritation and chemicals.<!--c:cholecystitis-c013--> Cholesterolosis arises when the bile carries more cholesterol than it can hold in solution.<!--c:cholecystitis-c014-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the removed gallbladder look like?**
- Acute: fibrinous exudate covers a congested outer surface.<!--c:cholecystitis-c015-->
- Acute: the mucosa looks vivid red or purple, and edema makes the wall thicker.<!--c:cholecystitis-c016-->
- Acute: a stone is often stuck in the cystic duct, and stones are usually present in the lumen.<!--c:cholecystitis-c017-->
- Rarely, a fully blocked cystic duct traps bacteria, and the gallbladder fills with turbid pus (empyema).<!--c:cholecystitis-c018-->
- Chronic: past acute attacks leave fibrous adhesions on the outer surface, and the wall is thick and firm.<!--c:cholecystitis-c019-->
- Chronic: about half of cases have coliform bacteria in the bile, which often also holds gravel or sludge.<!--c:cholecystitis-c020-->
- A porcelain gallbladder is a calcified wall, which long-standing inflammation can cause.<!--c:cholecystitis-c021-->
- Cholesterolosis: yellow flecks dot the mucosa, giving the "strawberry gallbladder".<!--c:cholecystitis-c022-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What is seen on the slide?** In acute disease, acute and chronic inflammatory cells infiltrate the wall, which is conspicuously edematous and hemorrhagic.<!--c:cholecystitis-c023--> Once bacteria invade, pus often forms in the wall.<!--c:cholecystitis-c024--> Gangrenous cholecystitis is the severe form, in which the lining is necrotic over a wide area instead of showing only patchy ulcers.<!--c:cholecystitis-c025--> In chronic disease, Rokitansky–Aschoff sinuses extend into a wall that is scarred and inflamed throughout.<!--c:cholecystitis-c026--> Sometimes the chronic mucosa is intact, but it tends to be atrophic with patchy ulcers.<!--c:cholecystitis-c027--> In cholesterolosis, cholesterol-filled macrophages swell the mucosal folds, their small nuclei lying at the cell edge.<!--c:cholecystitis-c028-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** the specimen is the gallbladder removed by cholecystectomy.<!--c:cholecystitis-c029-->
- On routine H&E in acute disease, look for mucosal ulceration, wall edema and hemorrhage, and mixed acute and chronic inflammation.<!--c:cholecystitis-c030-->
- On routine H&E in chronic disease, look for a fibrotic, inflamed wall with Rokitansky–Aschoff sinuses.<!--c:cholecystitis-c031-->
- The source pages describe no special stain, immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy or molecular test for these lesions.

## Clinical presentation

**How do patients present?** In acute cholecystitis, most patients first seek care for pain in the right upper abdomen.<!--c:cholecystitis-c032--> Biliary colic has occurred earlier in most of these patients.<!--c:cholecystitis-c033--> A stone in the common bile duct, or edema of that duct, causes mild jaundice in about 20% of patients.<!--c:cholecystitis-c034--> The attack usually settles within a week.<!--c:cholecystitis-c035--> Rigors, a high white count, fever and pain that will not go away mean the disease is progressing and surgery is needed.<!--c:cholecystitis-c036--> Even though the mucosa heals, the gallbladder keeps working poorly once inflammation resolves and the wall turns fibrotic.<!--c:cholecystitis-c037--> Episodic pain in the right hypochondrium is characteristic of chronic disease.<!--c:cholecystitis-c038--> Many patients also report vague abdominal symptoms, which may not come from the gallbladder.<!--c:cholecystitis-c039--> Cholesterolosis is ordinarily asymptomatic.<!--c:cholecystitis-c040-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which test settles the diagnosis?** Stones within a thickened, shrunken gallbladder are what ultrasound shows, and it is the preferred test for chronic cholecystitis.<!--c:cholecystitis-c041--> Cholecystectomy is the definitive treatment.<!--c:cholecystitis-c042-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What can go wrong in severe acute disease?** Bacteria in the gallbladder can lead to perforation, a serious complication.<!--c:cholecystitis-c043--> Bile peritonitis can result when bile escapes into the peritoneal cavity.<!--c:cholecystitis-c044--> After perforation, inflammatory adhesions more often confine the spill by forming a pericholecystic abscess.<!--c:cholecystitis-c045--> A cholecystenteric fistula may arise when gallbladder contents eat through into a hollow organ.<!--c:cholecystitis-c046-->

## High-yield summary

- Gallstones underlie **90%** of acute cholecystitis; the rest is **acalculous**.<!--c:cholecystitis-c052-->
- A stone in the cystic duct may lead to **phospholipase** release and **lysolecithin** injury of the mucosa.<!--c:cholecystitis-c053-->
- Severe acute disease can show **empyema** and **gangrenous** necrosis; bacterial infection can lead to **perforation**.<!--c:cholecystitis-c054-->
- Chronic cholecystitis shows a thick fibrotic wall with **Rokitansky–Aschoff sinuses** and may end as **porcelain gallbladder**.<!--c:cholecystitis-c055-->
- **Cholesterolosis** (strawberry gallbladder) is foamy macrophage accumulation and is usually silent.<!--c:cholecystitis-c056-->
- Chronic disease is diagnosed by **ultrasound** and treated by **cholecystectomy**.<!--c:cholecystitis-c057-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Do all cases of acute cholecystitis have stones?** No: acalculous cases exist, linked to polyarteritis nodosa, *Salmonella typhosa*, sepsis and trauma.<!--c:cholecystitis-c058-->

**Does infection start the process?** Usually not: blockage of bile flow comes first and bacteria arrive later.<!--c:cholecystitis-c059-->

**Is cholesterolosis a painful inflammation?** No: it is macrophage accumulation in the submucosa and usually produces no symptoms.<!--c:cholecystitis-c060-->

**What is a porcelain gallbladder?** A wall calcified after long-standing inflammation.<!--c:cholecystitis-c061-->

**Do vague abdominal complaints prove chronic cholecystitis?** No: such symptoms are common, but whether the gallbladder causes them is uncertain.<!--c:cholecystitis-c062-->

## Mnemonics

**EGP: "Every Gallbladder Pops."** Empyema, Gangrene and Perforation are the severe outcomes of acute disease.<!--c:cholecystitis-c063-->
