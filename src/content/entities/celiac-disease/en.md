## Definition and epidemiology

**What is celiac disease?** Gluten in wheat, rye and barley provokes an immune reaction in people with a susceptible genotype, and absorption suffers.<!--c:celiac-disease-c001--> Of the gluten proteins, gliadin seems to be the main one responsible.<!--c:celiac-disease-c002--> Many patients with type 1 diabetes also have celiac disease.<!--c:celiac-disease-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which genes and exposures are involved?** Family studies showed that the disease has a genetic basis.<!--c:celiac-disease-c004--> Nearly all patients carry HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8.<!--c:celiac-disease-c005--> A patient who lacks both alleles almost certainly does not have celiac disease, although carrying them does not by itself guarantee it.<!--c:celiac-disease-c006--> Environmental factors besides gluten may contribute as well.<!--c:celiac-disease-c007-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does gluten damage the gut?**
- Antigen-presenting cells carrying DQ2 or DQ8 show altered gliadin pieces to T cells and so activate them.<!--c:celiac-disease-c008-->
- Activated T cells in the gut lining release cytokines, which injure it.<!--c:celiac-disease-c009-->
- Complement activation triggered by deposited antigen–antibody complexes is a further possible cause of injury.<!--c:celiac-disease-c010-->
- Serologic tests detect circulating antibodies, which B cells secrete with help from the activated T cells.<!--c:celiac-disease-c011-->

## Gross morphology

Not applicable.

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the small-bowel mucosa look like?** Villous change varies; the mucosa is more inflamed, with extra intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs).<!--c:celiac-disease-c012--> The worst cases combine hyperplastic crypts with villi that have disappeared entirely or nearly so.<!--c:celiac-disease-c013--> Damage to the surface epithelium strips its cells of their brush border.<!--c:celiac-disease-c014--> Crypts deepen and villi shrink because epithelial cells die early and crypt mitoses rise to replace them.<!--c:celiac-disease-c015--> Extra IELs concentrated at the villus tips may be the only change; at the other extreme the mucosa is flat, with many grades in between.<!--c:celiac-disease-c016--> The distal ileum is rarely affected, whereas the proximal small bowel shows the strongest changes.<!--c:celiac-disease-c017--> Patients with celiac disease essentially always have more IELs.<!--c:celiac-disease-c018--> A raised IEL count also occurs with NSAID use and H. pylori gastritis, so it does not identify celiac disease by itself.<!--c:celiac-disease-c019-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and which tests help?**
- **How it is studied:** on histology, look for more IELs, shorter villi and deeper, hyperplastic crypts.<!--c:celiac-disease-c020-->
- **Serology:** the anti-tissue transglutaminase (tTG) antibody is now the test of choice.<!--c:celiac-disease-c021-->
- **Serology:** the antiendomysial antibody is the other important test.<!--c:celiac-disease-c022-->
- **Serology:** by sensitivity and specificity, antigliadin antibody lags well behind the other two.<!--c:celiac-disease-c023-->

The reference gives no special stain, immunohistochemical or molecular test beyond these.

## Clinical presentation

**How does a patient present?** Typical complaints are diarrhea and abdominal discomfort.<!--c:celiac-disease-c024--> Steatorrhea is a feature of advanced disease, when the gut copes badly with dietary fat.<!--c:celiac-disease-c025--> Intestinal bacteria act on the lipid and give the stool a foul smell, and the fat also makes it tend to float.<!--c:celiac-disease-c026--> Severe late malnutrition shows as edema from low albumin, together with weight and muscle loss.<!--c:celiac-disease-c027--> In advanced disease, patients may be short compared with their siblings, and osteoporosis can develop.<!--c:celiac-disease-c028--> Better diagnosis and treatment mean that this classic picture is now seen less often.<!--c:celiac-disease-c029--> Iron-deficiency anemia that does not respond to oral iron may be the only sign, or the patient may have no symptoms.<!--c:celiac-disease-c030-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What is needed to make the diagnosis?** No single line of evidence suffices: clinical features, serology and histology must be put together.<!--c:celiac-disease-c031--> A placebo effect of diet change is known, so feeling better on a new diet does not prove the diagnosis.<!--c:celiac-disease-c032-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens over time?** A gluten-free diet is usually an effective treatment.<!--c:celiac-disease-c033--> Because gluten is present almost everywhere in food, keeping to the diet can be hard.<!--c:celiac-disease-c034--> Patients with long-standing disease are at higher risk of small-bowel adenocarcinoma and of enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL).<!--c:celiac-disease-c035--> The size of this risk is unclear.<!--c:celiac-disease-c036-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What else causes malabsorption with a damaged small-bowel mucosa?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Tropical sprue | Found only in particular parts of the world.<!--c:celiac-disease-c037--> The whole small bowel is often affected, down to the ileum.<!--c:celiac-disease-c038--> Look for IELs mainly in the crypts, not at the villus tips.<!--c:celiac-disease-c039--> | Antibiotics relieve it.<!--c:celiac-disease-c040--> |
| Autoimmune enteropathy | Circulating antibodies against the enterocytes themselves.<!--c:celiac-disease-c041--> Goblet and Paneth cells are usually lost, and IELs stand out less than in celiac disease.<!--c:celiac-disease-c042--> Stomach and colon are often involved too.<!--c:celiac-disease-c043--> | Immunosuppression is required, and removing gluten does not help.<!--c:celiac-disease-c044--> |
| Whipple disease | Infection by Tropheryma whippelii, with bacteria-filled foamy macrophages that swell the villi.<!--c:celiac-disease-c045--> | PAS-positive foamy macrophages; PCR is replacing this method.<!--c:celiac-disease-c046--> |
| Small bowel bacterial overgrowth | Most cases follow poor gut motility, which lets colon-type anaerobes multiply in the small bowel.<!--c:celiac-disease-c047--> | Hydrogen and methane breath test.<!--c:celiac-disease-c048--> |

## High-yield summary

- Nearly all patients carry **HLA-DQ2 or DQ8**; with neither, the disease is very unlikely.<!--c:celiac-disease-c049-->
- **Gliadin**, a gluten protein, seems to be the main trigger.<!--c:celiac-disease-c050-->
- Histology shows more **IELs**, **crypt hyperplasia** and, in marked cases, **villous atrophy**, mainly in the proximal small bowel.<!--c:celiac-disease-c051-->
- The serologic test of choice is **anti-tTG** antibody.<!--c:celiac-disease-c052-->
- Sometimes the only sign is **iron-deficiency anemia** that oral iron fails to correct.<!--c:celiac-disease-c053-->
- Long-term risks are **EATL** and small-bowel **adenocarcinoma**.<!--c:celiac-disease-c054-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Do more IELs prove celiac disease?** No. NSAIDs and H. pylori gastritis also raise IELs, though nearly every celiac patient has them.<!--c:celiac-disease-c055-->

**Does carrying HLA-DQ2 or DQ8 mean the patient has celiac disease?** No. Carrying either allele does not guarantee the disease, but having neither makes it very unlikely.<!--c:celiac-disease-c056-->

**Does improvement on a gluten-free diet confirm the diagnosis?** Not by itself: a placebo effect of diet change is known.<!--c:celiac-disease-c057-->

**Is antigliadin antibody the best serologic test?** No. It lags behind tTG and antiendomysial antibody in sensitivity and specificity.<!--c:celiac-disease-c058-->

## Mnemonics

**FLAT:** Flat villi, Lymphocytes in the epithelium, Antibody to tTG, T-cell lymphoma risk.<!--c:celiac-disease-c059-->
