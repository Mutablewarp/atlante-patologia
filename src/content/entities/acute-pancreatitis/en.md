## Definition and epidemiology

**What is acute pancreatitis?** Abnormal release of pancreatic digestive enzymes drives the disease, and inflammation is only part of the picture.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c001--> The leaked enzymes digest the pancreas and the tissues around it.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c002--> The mild form spares most acinar cells and usually the rest of the body; the changes are stromal edema and acute inflammation.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c003--> Massive necrosis with hemorrhage defines the severe form, which is sometimes fatal and can bring systemic effects.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c004--> Repeated attacks can lead on to chronic pancreatitis.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What triggers an attack?** Gallstones sometimes block the pancreatic duct and set it off.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c006--> Gallstones are found in roughly 45% of people with acute pancreatitis.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c007--> Yet a stone lodged at the ampulla of Vater is found in only a few of them, and why stones cause pancreatitis is still unclear.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c008-->

**And alcohol?** About one-third of cases occur in people who abuse alcohol chronically.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c009--> Only a minority of heavy drinkers ever develop the disease.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c010--> After heavy drinking, the sphincter of Oddi can swell or go into spasm.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c011--> Pancreatic juice flow also rises indirectly, because alcohol first boosts small-bowel secretion.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c012-->

**What are the other causes?**
- Viruses such as cytomegalovirus, coxsackievirus and mumps.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c013-->
- Drugs, for example diuretics and azathioprine.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c014-->
- Surgical manipulation, ERCP and blunt abdominal trauma.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c015-->
- Ischemia from thrombosis, vasculitis or shock.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c016-->
- Hyperlipidemia, probably through leaked lipase splitting triglycerides into cell-damaging fatty acids.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c017-->
- Hypercalcemia of any cause.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c018-->
- Cases with no identifiable cause (idiopathic) rank third in frequency.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c019-->
- Pancreatic or ampullary tumors can obstruct the ducts, and anatomic anomalies such as pancreas divisum can leave them insufficient.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c020-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does the gland end up digesting itself?** Enzymes that leak out of the cells in active form digest the gland and nearby tissue; the leak follows injury to acinar cells or blockage of the ducts.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c021--> Normally the acinar cell is shielded from its own enzymes by inhibitors, by inactive precursors, and by storage inside membrane-bound compartments.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c022--> This protection is clearly incomplete.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c023--> Trypsin does not kill cells directly; it is the central trigger that activates other proenzymes, such as prophospholipase A2 and proelastase.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c024--> Suspected trigger of early proenzyme activation: backflow of enzyme through the duct wall when a blocked or narrowed duct builds up pressure.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c025--> Fat-cell triglycerides split by lipase release fatty acids, which bind calcium and magnesium to form soaps.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c026--> Extra peripancreatic fat may explain why obesity raises risk, mainly of severe disease: leaked lipase finds more fat to digest.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c027-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the pancreas look like?** Early on, the gland is swollen with fluid and engorged with blood.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c028--> Crumbly bloody foci develop fast, starting as pale gray spots on day one.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c029--> In severe cases a huge retroperitoneal hematoma replaces most of the gland as the foci enlarge and multiply; little pancreatic tissue stays recognizable.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c030--> Outside the gland, the fat and the nearby mesentery show yellowish-white necrotic patches.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c031--> Calcium and magnesium soaps progressively stiffen the dead fat: soft early, chalky later.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c032--> In some patients, so much calcium is bound in soaps that blood calcium falls and neuromuscular irritability results.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c033-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What is seen on the slide?** Acute inflammation often accompanies the two chief findings, acinar cell necrosis and fat necrosis.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c034--> Usually the necrosis is scattered in patches; it seldom spreads through the whole gland.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c035--> In some patients an acute episode heals with uneven scarring and occasional calcification, the picture of chronic pancreatitis.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c036-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** on routine H&E, look for acinar necrosis, fat necrosis and acute inflammation.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c037-->
- Serum amylase and lipase are the diagnostic test; the reference describes no special stain, IHC or molecular test for this disease.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c038-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does a patient present?** Nausea and vomiting come with severe pain in the epigastrium, which radiates to the upper back.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c039--> Within hours, circulatory collapse and shock can follow.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c040--> Long, severe shock can lead to ARDS and acute kidney failure within the first week.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c041-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which test confirms it?** Raised amylase and lipase in the blood, documented within 24 to 72 hours, make the diagnosis.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c042--> Enzymes spill from damaged acinar cells into the blood and the retroperitoneum in the first phase of the illness.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c043-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What can go wrong?** Some survivors develop a pseudocyst: a pocket of enzyme-rich fluid and debris with a connective-tissue wall.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c044--> The duodenum and other adjacent structures may be squeezed, sometimes blocked, by a pseudocyst that grows.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c045--> A pseudocyst that gets infected can form an abscess.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c046--> Peritonitis, chemical, septic or both, is the consequence of the uncommon rupture of a pseudocyst.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c047--> Gut-derived gram-negative bacteria can infect the dead pancreatic tissue, an occasional event that sharply raises mortality.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c048--> Severe disease can overwhelm the kidneys, lungs and clotting, and cause shock.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c049--> Scarring after repeated episodes may end in chronic pancreatitis.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c050-->

## High-yield summary

- Acute pancreatitis is **autodigestion** by leaked pancreatic enzymes.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c054-->
- **Trypsin** activation is the central step, switching on prophospholipase A2 and proelastase.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c055-->
- **Gallstones** and **alcohol** head the list of causes; idiopathic cases rank third.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c056-->
- **Hemorrhage** marks the severe form; **fat necrosis** with calcium soaps is also typical.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c057-->
- **Serum amylase and lipase** confirm the diagnosis.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c058-->
- A **pseudocyst** can enlarge, become infected as an abscess, or rupture.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c059-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is acute pancreatitis a plain inflammation of the gland?** No. It is driven by enzyme release, and inflammation is only part of the picture.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c060-->

**Are most patients with acute pancreatitis carrying a stone stuck at the ampulla?** No. Such impacted stones are found in only a few patients.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c061-->

**Does trypsin itself kill the acinar cell?** No. Its role is indirect: it switches on other proenzymes.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c062-->

**Can blood calcium fall in acute pancreatitis?** Yes. Calcium is used up in the soaps of fat necrosis.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c063-->

**Does every heavy drinker get pancreatitis?** No. Only a minority do.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c064-->

## Mnemonics

**GALA** for causes worth remembering: Gallstones, Alcohol, Lipids (hyperlipidemia), and Abdominal injury or ERCP.<!--c:acute-pancreatitis-c065-->
