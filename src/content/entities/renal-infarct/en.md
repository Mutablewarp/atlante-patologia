## Definition and epidemiology

**What is a renal infarct, and how does it differ from cortical necrosis?** An embolus lodged in an artery, usually an interlobar or arcuate one, accounts for most renal infarcts.<!--c:renal-infarct-c001--> Necrosis of one or a few zones after an artery is blocked is called an infarct.<!--c:renal-infarct-c002--> Cortical necrosis is widespread ischemic death of the cortex, whole or partial.<!--c:renal-infarct-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What blocks the renal vessels?** A bigger occluded vessel means a bigger infarct.<!--c:renal-infarct-c004-->
- Emboli can come from ulcerated, complex atheromas of the aorta.<!--c:renal-infarct-c007-->
- Infected valves in bacterial endocarditis also shed emboli.<!--c:renal-infarct-c006-->
- Mural thrombi that form over a myocardial infarct or in atrial fibrillation can embolize.<!--c:renal-infarct-c005-->

Less often, thrombosis on atherosclerosis or arteritis occludes a renal artery branch.<!--c:renal-infarct-c008--> Narrowing of small renal artery branches by HUS, scleroderma or malignant hypertension can starve the tissue of blood.<!--c:renal-infarct-c009--> Sickle cell anemia infarcts the kidney mainly at the papillae.<!--c:renal-infarct-c010--> Hemorrhagic infarction can follow renal vein thrombosis: in adults with septic thrombophlebitis or hypercoagulability, and in infants with severe dehydration.<!--c:renal-infarct-c011--> Cortical necrosis can complicate shock from volume loss or endotoxin.<!--c:renal-infarct-c012--> The textbook trigger is a placenta that separates prematurely late in pregnancy.<!--c:renal-infarct-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**Why is the whole kidney rarely lost, and the cortex hit but not the medulla?** Collateral flow usually saves the kidney, so a blocked main renal artery rarely infarcts all of it.<!--c:renal-infarct-c014--> In shock, irreversible cortical necrosis follows reversible prerenal or intrarenal ischemia.<!--c:renal-infarct-c015--> Outer cortical vessels lie downstream of the medullary vasa recta on the juxtamedullary efferent arteriole.<!--c:renal-infarct-c016--> When outer cortical vessels close (spasm, clots, microangiopathy), the cortex dies and the medulla lives.<!--c:renal-infarct-c017-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the cut kidney look like?** A typical renal infarct is a pale wedge whose broad side faces the capsule.<!--c:renal-infarct-c018--> A rim of hemorrhage surrounds acute infarcts.<!--c:renal-infarct-c019--> Once healed, an infarct leaves a depressed cortical scar with sharp borders.<!--c:renal-infarct-c020--> The dead areas of cortex may be scattered patches or fused together.<!--c:renal-infarct-c021--> In extensive disease the cortex is pale and necrotic throughout.<!--c:renal-infarct-c022--> Capsular collaterals sustain a thin subcapsular rim of cortex, and medullary collaterals one at the corticomedullary junction.<!--c:renal-infarct-c023--> In survivors, the dead areas may calcify.<!--c:renal-infarct-c024-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What happens to the tissue over time?** In an infarct, no structure escapes coagulative necrosis.<!--c:renal-infarct-c025--> The reaction runs from acute inflammation through granulation tissue to fibrosis.<!--c:renal-infarct-c026--> Healed infarcts are fibrous scars: mild chronic inflammation, atrophic tubules, and glomeruli reduced to ghosts.<!--c:renal-infarct-c027--> Dystrophic calcification can develop in long-standing infarcts.<!--c:renal-infarct-c028--> Repeated sublethal ischemia can leave tubular atrophy and fibrosis in viable tissue at the margin of a healed infarct.<!--c:renal-infarct-c029-->

**And in cortical necrosis?** In the worst areas coagulative necrosis takes all parenchymal elements.<!--c:renal-infarct-c030--> Necrosis invariably includes the proximal tubules, along with most distal tubules.<!--c:renal-infarct-c031--> Many proximal tubules of the surviving cortex beside the dead zone are ischemically injured; glomeruli and distal tubules mostly escape.<!--c:renal-infarct-c032-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** a renal biopsy may be needed to diagnose cortical necrosis.<!--c:renal-infarct-c033-->
- Special stains, IHC and molecular tests: none described in the source.

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** Hematuria and severe flank or abdominal pain usually mark an acute infarct.<!--c:renal-infarct-c034--> A kidney infarcted in its entirety no longer works.<!--c:renal-infarct-c035--> Early on, severe cortical necrosis may mimic acute tubular injury clinically.<!--c:renal-infarct-c036-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is cortical necrosis confirmed?** Diagnosis may need a biopsy or a renal arteriogram.<!--c:renal-infarct-c037-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How do patients do?** Irreversibility is more frequent in cortical necrosis than in acute tubular injury.<!--c:renal-infarct-c038--> Recovery depends on how much cortex is lost.<!--c:renal-infarct-c039--> Survivors commonly develop hypertension.<!--c:renal-infarct-c040-->

| Renal infarct (focal) | Wedge-shaped, with its base on the capsule, rather than diffuse.<!--c:renal-infarct-c043--> | Wedge on the cut surface.<!--c:renal-infarct-c052--> |

## High-yield summary

- An embolic infarct is a **pale wedge** with its base on the capsule.<!--c:renal-infarct-c044-->
- **Cortical necrosis** follows shock, classically after the placenta separates prematurely in late pregnancy.<!--c:renal-infarct-c045-->
- Cortical necrosis spares the **medulla**, since the vasa recta arise upstream of the outer cortical vessels.<!--c:renal-infarct-c046-->
- Thin **subcapsular rims** of cortex survive because of collateral supply.<!--c:renal-infarct-c047-->
- Both lesions are **coagulative necrosis** and may calcify.<!--c:renal-infarct-c048-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is cortical necrosis just a big infarct?** No. Cortical necrosis is widespread and often follows shock; an infarct is a limited zone below a blocked artery.<!--c:renal-infarct-c049-->

**Does a blocked main renal artery usually infarct the whole kidney?** No. Collateral vessels usually spare the kidney, so total infarction is rare.<!--c:renal-infarct-c050-->

## Mnemonics

**How can I recall the infarct?** WEDGE: Wedge with base on the capsule, Emboli from heart or aorta, Dead coagulative tissue, Gone pale, hemorrhagic border, End-stage depressed scar.<!--c:renal-infarct-c051-->
