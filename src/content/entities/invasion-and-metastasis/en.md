## Definition and epidemiology

**What do invasion and metastasis mean?** Invasion is growth of a malignant tumor into the tissue around it.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c001--> Metastasis is malignant cells settling at a distant site with no continuity with the primary tumor.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c002--> Surgery can usually remove the primary tumor, so invasion and spread cause most cancer deaths.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c003--> Invasion and metastasis lie behind over 90% of cancer deaths.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c004--> Carcinoma in situ is always curable: its basement membrane still holds it inside the epithelium.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c005--> Cancers of cells that sit on no basement membrane, hepatocytes for instance, have no in situ stage.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c007-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What pushes a tumor cell to invade?** Signals from the tumor cells and from nearby non-tumor cells switch on an invasion program.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c008--> Stroma and tumor reinforce each other: the cells the tumor calls in help it invade more.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c011-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does a cell get from the primary tumor to a new organ?** A tumor spreads through successive steps, each needing the tumor cells and their neighbors to act differently.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c012--> The cascade runs from local escape (basement membrane, connective tissue), through blood or lymph, to a new organ (exit, micrometastasis), where cells lie dormant or grow.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c014-->

**What happens at the primary site?** In EMT, epithelial cells lose their grip on neighbors and gain the ability to migrate through tissue.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c015--> Snail, Slug, Zeb1 and Twist drive it.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c017--> Loss of E-cadherin frees cells to move, because this adhesion glycoprotein ties epithelial cells together.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c018--> Undetectable metastases are often present at diagnosis, since EMT frequently starts early.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c019--> In invasive tumors the balance between MMPs and their inhibitors (TIMPs) is lost, so excess protease activity clears a path through the matrix.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c022--> Tumor-associated macrophages release urokinase, which turns plasminogen into plasmin; plasmin then attacks type IV collagen in the basement membrane.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c023-->

**How do cells enter and survive in the circulation?** Lymphatic capillaries have no basement membrane, so tumor cells penetrate them readily.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c025--> In blood, tumor cells risk NK-cell killing, shear stress and anoikis (apoptosis after loss of matrix anchors).<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c026--> Platelets wrapped around tumor cells hide them from NK cells.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c027-->

**How do they leave and settle?** Tumor cells leave the blood by attaching to the vessel lining and crossing it, which is easier when the lining turns leaky.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c028--> Circulating tumor cells exit at premetastatic niches, sites prepared by mediators from the tumor, not at random.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c029--> Bone marrow has a ready-made niche beside osteoblasts, and arriving tumor cells can push the blood stem cells out of it.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c030--> Tumors can also create a niche where none existed, by reshaping matrix and recruiting supporting cells and new vessels.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c031--> Most cells a tumor sheds never seed a metastasis that matters clinically; the process is highly wasteful.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c032--> Cancer stem cells are generally thought to be the only cells able to found metastases.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c033--> A micrometastasis may proliferate, die, stay dormant, or become dormant and later reactivate.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c034--> Dormant micrometastases still divide and die, at low, offsetting rates; this is not suspended animation.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c035--> Primary tumors and metastases differ in no known genetic way, so many metastatic traits seem epigenetic.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c037-->

## Gross morphology

**What does spread look like to the naked eye?** Bowel above an obstructing colon cancer is dilated.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c038--> Ovarian cancer seeding the peritoneum leaves small nodules on the mesentery.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c041--> Cancer seeded in a body cavity grows as masses and may provoke large effusions, such as malignant ascites.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c042--> A node full of metastasis can grow far larger than normal, even bigger than the tumor it came from.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c043-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the pathologist see?** The basement membrane stops cervical carcinoma in situ from leaving the mucosa, although squamous tumor cells replace the full epithelial thickness.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c044--> Cells reaching a node lodge first in the marginal (subcapsular) sinuses, then spread through it.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c046-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**

**How it is studied:**
- Circulating tumor cells are isolated from blood, less often marrow, by epithelial markers such as EpCAM and cytokeratins.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c047-->
- They are used to monitor therapy and assess prognosis.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c048-->
- Biopsy, H&E and molecular tests are not covered in the packet pages.

## Clinical presentation

**How does spread show up in a patient?** Breast cancer spreads first to regional lymph nodes.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c050--> Lateral breast cancers generally reach axillary nodes; medial ones reach internal mammary nodes.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c051--> Sometimes the first clue to an abdominal cancer is a swollen lymph node above the collarbone.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c052--> Testicular cancer usually goes first to the periaortic nodes, because the testis arises in the abdomen; the groin nodes drain the scrotum, not the testis.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c053--> The lungs are the first stop for tumors whose veins empty into the vena cava; the liver is the first stop for those drained by the portal system.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c054-->

**Are metastatic sites random?** No. Paget (1889) held that metastases form where the tumor cell (seed) and the local environment of the organ (soil) suit each other.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c055--> Bone attracts metastases from breast, prostate and thyroid cancers, a sign of organ tropism; the spleen and muscle are seldom hit despite rich perfusion.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c056-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is the extent of spread recorded?** Staging records detectable spread: distant sites, nodes, local growth and size.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c057--> In TNM, M is distant metastasis, N regional node involvement and T the size and local extent of the primary.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c058-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What does spread mean for outcome?** Staging predicts the course better than grading, because low-grade tumors can still be aggressive.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c062--> Regional node spread strongly influences prognosis in breast cancer.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c063--> After resection, adjuvant therapy aims at hidden micrometastases yet hits fast-dividing cells best.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c064-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[carcinoma-in-situ]] | Malignant cells stay above an intact basement membrane, so the tumor has not invaded.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c066--> | Histology of the basement membrane |
| Lymphatic spread | Tumor cells reach regional nodes first, typical of breast carcinoma.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c067--> | Histology of regional nodes |
| Hematogenous spread | Cells enter thin-walled vessels, and the target organ often follows venous drainage.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c068--> | Circulating tumor cells (EpCAM, cytokeratins) |
| Seeding of body cavities | Cells that fall into the peritoneal or pleural space from a nearby organ's tumor grow as masses and cause fluid.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c069--> | Staging assessment of cavity surfaces |

## High-yield summary

- **Invasion** crosses the **basement membrane**; **carcinoma in situ** has not.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c005-->
- **Metastasis**, not the primary, causes most **cancer deaths**.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c003-->
- **EMT**, with loss of **E-cadherin**, is central to invasion.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c018-->
- Routes: **lymphatic**, **hematogenous** and **body-cavity seeding**.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c067-->
- **Platelets** coat circulating cells and shield them from **NK cells**.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c027-->
- **Premetastatic niches** guide where cells settle.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c029-->
- **Seed and soil** (Paget): tumor cell and organ must suit each other.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c055-->
- **Dormancy** is a balance of proliferation and death.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c035-->
- **TNM** stage predicts outcome better than grade.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c062-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does the primary tumor usually kill the patient?** No. Spread does, and the primary can usually be resected.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c003-->

**Is metastasis always a late event?** No. Tiny metastases are often present at diagnosis, because EMT often starts early.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c019-->

**Is dormancy suspended animation?** No. Cells still divide and die slowly, at offsetting rates.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c035-->

**Does every cancer have an in situ stage?** No. Tumors from cells without a basement membrane have none.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c007-->

**Are metastatic sites random?** No. Tumor cells and the soil of the target organ must be compatible.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c055-->

## Mnemonics

**8 D's** (original): **D**etach, **D**igest, **D**ive in, **D**odge NK cells, **D**epart the vessel, **D**well as a micrometastasis, then **D**oze or **D**evelop.<!--c:invasion-and-metastasis-c070-->
