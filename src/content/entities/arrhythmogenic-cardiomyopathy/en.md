## Definition and epidemiology

**What is arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy?** Ventricular tachyarrhythmias often start early in this primary disease of heart muscle.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c001--> It is a disease of the desmosome, the organelle that glues heart cells to each other.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c002--> About 1 in 5,000 people are affected.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c003--> Most cases occur around the Mediterranean, where it is a frequent cause of sudden death under age 35.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes it, and does it run in families?** It is familial and usually autosomal dominant.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c005--> Incomplete penetrance and a variable picture mean the disease is probably undercounted.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c006--> Mutated desmosomal genes include those for the adhesion molecules desmoglein-2 and desmocollin-2.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c007--> Genes for the intracellular linker proteins plakoglobin, desmoplakin and plakophilin-2 can be mutated as well.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c008--> In the classical right ventricular form, the gene most often mutated is *PKP2* (plakophilin-2).<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c009--> Biventricular or left-sided forms are often associated with mutations in *DSP* (desmoplakin).<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c010-->

## Pathogenesis

**How can a faulty desmosome damage the heart?** In heart cells, linker proteins join the adhesion molecules at the membrane to the desmin filaments inside.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c011--> The heart and skin carry the heaviest mechanical load, and these are the organs richest in desmosomes.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c012--> Whether skin, heart or both are diseased generally depends on where the mutant isoform is expressed.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c013--> How the mutations cause disease is not settled; altered Wnt signaling and abnormal heart responses to exercise load are suspected.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c014-->

## Gross morphology

**Where is the muscle lost?** Classically, fat takes over the outer layer of the right ventricular wall, and muscle survives mainly in the inner trabeculae.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c015--> In most cases the left ventricle is involved too, mainly its posterolateral part.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c016-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the myocardium look like on the slide?** Fat and fibrous tissue take the place of epicardial myocytes after these cells degenerate.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c017--> The amount of change varies, and in sudden death it may be subtle.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c018-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**

**How it is studied:**
- A trichrome stain colors myocytes brick-red and fibrous tissue blue.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c019-->
- Among people who meet the clinical diagnostic criteria, more than half carry an identifiable mutation in a desmosomal gene.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c020-->
- The reference pages do not describe biopsy sampling or electron microscopy for this disease.

## Clinical presentation

**How does it show itself?** Early on, patients can have dangerous rhythm disturbances or die suddenly while the ventricle still contracts well and looks nearly unchanged.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c021--> Forms involving both ventricles or mainly the left may be wrongly labeled dilated cardiomyopathy; the disease was first described in the right ventricle (ARVC).<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c022-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it diagnosed?** The clinical criteria are fairly specific but not very sensitive, so diagnosis is hard.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c023-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How dangerous is it?** Around 1% of patients die each year.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c024--> Sudden death accounts for more than 80% of the deaths.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c025--> Even though a few DCM patients are very prone to arrhythmias, sudden death is far more typical of arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c026-->

**What else can look like it?**

## High-yield summary

- ACM is a disease of the **desmosome**.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c031-->
- The lesion is **fibrofatty replacement** of epicardial myocytes, mainly of the right ventricle.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c032-->
- The main risk is **sudden death**, which can come while structural remodeling is still minor.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c033-->
- Dilated cardiomyopathy can be the wrong label for the **biventricular** and left-dominant forms.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c034-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is ACM only a disease of the right ventricle?** No. Most cases also involve the left ventricular free wall.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c035-->

**Does an unremarkable heart at autopsy exclude ACM in a sudden death?** No. The amount of fibrofatty change varies and may be subtle.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c036-->

**Must the ventricle be dilated and failing before arrhythmias start?** No. Serious rhythm problems can come first, ahead of significant remodeling.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c037-->

**Is ACM clearly separate from dilated cardiomyopathy?** Not always. The two can look alike in patients and in tissue, and share genes.<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c038-->

## Mnemonics

**P**lakophilin goes with the **P**rimarily **R**ight-sided classical form; **D**esmoplakin goes with **D**ominant-left or both-ventricle forms (the classical right-sided disease is tied mostly to PKP2, DSP variants tend to go with both ventricles or the left).<!--c:arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy-c039-->
