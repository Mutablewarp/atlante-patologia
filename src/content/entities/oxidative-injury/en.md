## Definition and epidemiology

Oxidative injury is damage to cells and tissues by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and related reactive molecules when their production exceeds the cell's protective capacity.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c001--> Nearly every kind of biomolecule within their reach can be damaged by ROS, the partially reduced derivatives of oxygen.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c002--> The same molecules are also normal signaling intermediates and the weapons phagocytes use against microbes, so the problem is excess or misdirection rather than their mere presence.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c003--> As a shared mechanism of injury rather than a disease, it has no epidemiology of its own.

## Etiology and risk factors

Oxidant stress arises both inside the cell, from its own metabolism, and from outside exposures.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c004-->

- **Mitochondrial respiration:** electrons escape from the electron transport chain, notably at coenzyme Q, and reduce O₂ to superoxide.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c005--> Even in healthy cells, mitochondria turn roughly 1–3% of the oxygen they take up into ROS.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c006-->
- **Metabolic enzymes:** cytochrome P450 mixed-function oxygenases and peroxisomal oxidases produce ROS during routine work.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c007-->
- **Phagocytes:** NADPH oxidase drives the respiratory burst of neutrophils and macrophages, and myeloperoxidase then turns H₂O₂ into hypochlorous acid.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c008-->
- **Reperfusion:** xanthine oxidase, which rises in ischemic endothelium, uses the returning oxygen to oxidize purines left over from ATP breakdown, releasing superoxide.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c009-->
- **Ionizing radiation:** splits water (radiolysis) and so yields hydroxyl radicals directly.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c010-->
- **Transition metals:** free ferrous iron or cuprous copper converts H₂O₂ into hydroxyl radicals.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c011-->
- **Chemicals:** heavy metals, xenobiotics and many drugs act as oxidants or electrophiles.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c012-->
- **Excess oxygen:** high inspired oxygen, as in oxygen therapy, is itself a source of oxidant stress.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**The reduction ladder.** Turning O₂ into water takes four electrons; stopping after one, two or three gives superoxide (O₂•⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and the hydroxyl radical (•OH).<!--c:oxidative-injury-c014-->

- Superoxide acts close to where it forms and is the parent of the other ROS.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c015-->
- H₂O₂ is only mildly harmful on its own, but it crosses membranes and spreads oxidant stress to other compartments.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c016-->
- Of all the ROS, the hydroxyl radical reacts most readily and does most of the damage to macromolecules.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c017--> It forms from H₂O₂ plus Fe²⁺ (Fenton reaction) or from superoxide plus H₂O₂ (Haber–Weiss reaction).<!--c:oxidative-injury-c018-->
- Nitric oxide combines with superoxide to give peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), which damages lipids, proteins and DNA.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c019-->
- Hypochlorous acid, which neutrophil myeloperoxidase makes to kill microbes, harms host cells too when it escapes from the cell.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c020-->

**Molecular targets.**

- **Lipids:** in membrane phospholipids, •OH pulls a hydrogen atom off an unsaturated fatty acid; the lipid radical left behind binds O₂ and becomes a peroxyl radical, which takes a hydrogen from the next fatty acid, so the damage spreads as a chain reaction.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c021--> Unstable lipid peroxides fall apart into small aldehydes, and the membrane loses its integrity.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c022--> In mitochondria, peroxidation of cardiolipin, which is rich in unsaturated fatty acids, cripples the electron transport chain and ATP output.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c023-->
- **Proteins:** amino acids whose side chains carry sulfur, such as cysteine and methionine, or nitrogen are among the most vulnerable; oxidation can split proteins into fragments or bind them together into aggregates.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c024--> Iron turns this into a vicious circle: insoluble aggregates of oxidized proteins bind Fe²⁺ and then grow under the ROS that this iron produces.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c025-->
- **Carbohydrates:** advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) are proteins altered by reactive sugar derivatives, and •OH attack on sugars is one way such derivatives arise.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c026-->
- **DNA:** •OH can alter single bases, cut the strands or tie the two strands together; repair usually copes, but a cell whose DNA damage outstrips repair either dies or keeps a permanent mutation.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c027--> A characteristic base lesion is 8-hydroxyguanine, formed when guanine is oxidized.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c076-->
- **Extracellular matrix:** oxidants also damage matrix proteins, collagen and elastin among them, and basement membranes.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c028-->

**Antioxidant defenses.** Whether injury follows depends largely on how well the cell's defenses perform.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c029-->

- **Enzymes:** superoxide dismutases convert O₂•⁻ to H₂O₂ (Cu/Zn SOD in the cytosol, Mn SOD in mitochondria); catalase in peroxisomes and glutathione peroxidase (GPX) in cytosol and mitochondria reduce H₂O₂ to water.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c030--> Peroxiredoxins (recycled by thioredoxin), NQO1 and heme oxygenase add further protection.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c031-->
- **Glutathione:** reduced glutathione (GSH) is the most abundant endogenous antioxidant; GPX oxidizes it to GSSG, which is either reduced again by glutathione reductase or exported from the cell.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c032-->
- **Scavengers:** fat-soluble vitamin E breaks lipid-peroxidation chains inside membranes; water-soluble vitamin C reacts with ROS and regenerates vitamin E; lipid-soluble vitamin A precursors also interrupt radical chains.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c033-->
- **Outside the cell:** extracellular fluids have antioxidants of their own, such as albumin and extracellular superoxide dismutase.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c034-->

**Adaptive response: Nrf2.** Of all the pathways that switch on antioxidant defenses, signaling through the transcription factor Nrf2 is the strongest.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c035--> At rest Keap1, together with a CUL3 E3 ubiquitin ligase, holds Nrf2 in the cytosol and sends it to the proteasome.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c036--> Under stress, p53-induced sestrins and p62 release Nrf2, and p62 routes Keap1 to autophagic degradation.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c037--> Free Nrf2 enters the nucleus and activates genes carrying antioxidant response elements (AREs), such as NQO1, glutathione S-transferases, peroxiredoxins and ferritins.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c038--> Oxidant sensors amplify the signal: H₂O₂ inactivates protein tyrosine phosphatases through their active-site cysteines, which boosts kinase signaling, and MAP kinases activate AP-1 and NF-κB alongside Nrf2.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c039-->

**p53 as a switch.** p53 can push the cell either way: it supports survival by keeping antioxidant genes active while oxidant stress stays low, and it promotes death once stress becomes severe, by switching on other target genes that weaken those defenses.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c040-->

## Microscopic morphology

Oxidative damage is recognized through the lesions it produces: in acetaminophen toxicity, for example, few inflammatory cells gather around the central veins, where hepatocytes are either swollen with water (hydropic swelling) or already dead (coagulative necrosis).<!--c:oxidative-injury-c041--> A long-term trace is lipofuscin, a golden-brown pigment made of peroxidized lipids and of proteins damaged and cross-linked by oxidation; it builds up with age in long-lived cells such as neurons and heart muscle cells.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c042-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Liver biopsy in drug toxicity** (for example acetaminophen): drug-induced patterns mimic those of other liver diseases, so a biopsy alone often cannot prove that a drug is to blame; the diagnosis rests above all on the drug history and on ruling out other causes.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c077--> On H&E, look for the zonal pattern: necrosis concentrated around the central veins (centrilobular), which very high doses can extend to the whole lobule (panlobular).<!--c:oxidative-injury-c078-->
- **Pigments, H&E and Perls:** lipofuscin, the lasting residue of oxidative damage (see Microscopic morphology), and hemosiderin, a storage form of iron, both appear on H&E as brownish granules in the cytoplasm; the Perls (Prussian blue) stain tells them apart because it turns iron blue (see [[g:perls-prussian-blue]] and [[pigment-accumulation]]).<!--c:oxidative-injury-c079--> In [[hereditary-hemochromatosis|hereditary hemochromatosis]] it shows the iron that has built up inside the hepatocytes (see Clinical presentation).<!--c:oxidative-injury-c080-->
- **Immunohistochemistry for oxidation products:** 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), an oxidized DNA nucleoside, and proteins carrying adducts of the aldehyde 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (HNE) are chemical marks of ROS attack that antibodies recognize, so oxidative damage can be mapped in ordinary paraffin-embedded sections.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c081--> HNE is one of the main reactive aldehydes formed when membrane lipids are peroxidized, and it has been found by immunostaining in Lewy bodies of Parkinson disease and in neurofibrillary tangles of Alzheimer disease, for example.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c082-->
- **Electron microscopy and molecular tests:** the reference text used for this card describes none that shows oxidative damage as such.

## Clinical presentation

Oxidative injury is a mechanism shared by many conditions, for example:

- **Ischemia–reperfusion:** ROS formed during ischemia surge when oxygen returns, from xanthine oxidase, a burst of mitochondrial electron flow and recruited neutrophils.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c043--> Of the myocardium finally lost to an infarct, as much as half may be due to reperfusion injury.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c044-->
- **Acetaminophen overdose:** the centrilobular necrosis reflects lost antioxidant defense, since hepatocytes can no longer neutralize peroxides once a toxic metabolite of the drug has used up their glutathione.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c045--> In the United States it leads all other causes of acute liver failure.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c046--> Hepatocytes around the central veins are hit hardest, probably because drug-metabolizing enzymes are most active there.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c086-->
- **Iron overload:** iron-driven oxidative stress is the most likely cause of the organ damage in [[hereditary-hemochromatosis|hereditary hemochromatosis]].<!--c:oxidative-injury-c083--> By contrast, hemosiderosis in the strict sense is excess stored iron that does not injure cells.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c084-->
- **Asbestos:** inhaled fibers set off a respiratory burst in the inflammatory cells that struggle to engulf them, and the iron each fiber carries boosts ROS formation; much of the harm asbestos does is thought to come from these reactive species.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c085-->
- **Chronic granulomatous disease:** recurrent infections are the price of an inherited NADPH oxidase defect that leaves phagocytes without superoxide or H₂O₂; the disease shows that controlled ROS production is needed.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c047-->
- **Oxygen toxicity:** in premature infants, high inspired oxygen injures developing alveoli and contributes to bronchopulmonary dysplasia.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c048-->
- **Radiation, chemical carcinogenesis, aging and degenerative diseases** are all linked to excess ROS.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c049-->
- **Protein aggregation:** above all in cells that no longer divide, failing antioxidant defenses let proteins oxidize, misfold and clump into toxic aggregates.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c050-->

## Complications and prognosis

With mild to moderate stress, defenses restore balance and mildly oxidized proteins are cleared by the 20S proteasome.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c051--> When stress is severe, mitochondria become the route to death: a permeability transition pore held open by ROS and calcium overload collapses their membrane potential and sets off the death programs.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c052--> Beyond acute cell death, oxidative damage can cause lasting cell dysfunction and malignant transformation.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c053-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[hypoxic-ischemic-injury]] | The primary lesion is ATP depletion with failure of ion pumps; ROS add to the damage but do not start it.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c054--> | Clinical context of reduced perfusion or oxygen |
| [[ischemia-reperfusion-injury]] | A specific setting of oxidative injury: damage is amplified when oxygen returns to ischemic tissue.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c055--> | Timing relative to restored blood flow |
| Phagocyte killing ([[acute-inflammation]]) | The same ROS are produced on purpose inside phagocytes to destroy ingested microbes.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c056--> | Inflammatory setting; injury to host cells only when ROS are released |
| Protein misfolding ([[protein-quality-control]]) | Toxic aggregates can come from mutant proteins or from oxidative damage to normal ones.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c057--> | Genetic versus acquired context |

## High-yield summary

- O₂ gains electrons stepwise: **superoxide**, then **hydrogen peroxide**, then the **hydroxyl radical**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c058-->
- Of all ROS, the **hydroxyl radical** does the most damage; the **Fenton reaction** makes it from H₂O₂ and Fe²⁺.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c059-->
- **Lipid peroxidation** is a self-propagating chain in membranes, interrupted by **vitamin E**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c060-->
- **SOD** turns superoxide into H₂O₂; **catalase** (peroxisomes) and **glutathione peroxidase** (cytosol, mitochondria) turn H₂O₂ into water.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c061-->
- **Reduced glutathione (GSH)** is the most abundant endogenous antioxidant.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c062-->
- **Nrf2**, released from **Keap1**, switches on genes with **antioxidant response elements**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c063-->
- In reperfusion, **xanthine oxidase** and neutrophils produce a burst of ROS.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c064-->
- **Acetaminophen** depletes **glutathione** and causes **centrilobular necrosis**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c065-->
- **NADPH oxidase** deficiency causes **chronic granulomatous disease**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c066-->
- **Lipofuscin** is the "wear and tear" residue of oxidized lipids and proteins.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c067-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- H₂O₂ is not very toxic by itself; the danger is its conversion to •OH in the presence of free iron.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c068-->
- Nitric oxide is double-edged: it dilates vessels and limits iron uptake, yet with superoxide it forms peroxynitrite.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c069-->
- Catalase works mainly in peroxisomes; GPX works in the cytosol and mitochondria.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c070-->
- ROS are not only harmful: without them phagocytes cannot kill microbes, as chronic granulomatous disease shows.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c071-->
- p53 is not purely protective: under severe oxidant stress it weakens antioxidant defenses and promotes cell death.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c072-->

## Mnemonics

**"1-2-3: Super, Peroxide, Hydroxyl"** (original): one electron gives superoxide, two give peroxide, three give hydroxyl, and the fourth finishes as water.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c073-->

**"SOD starts, CAT and GPX finish"** (original): SOD makes H₂O₂ from superoxide; catalase and GPX turn it into water.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c074-->

**"LiPiDS"** (original) for the targets: **Li**pids, **P**rote**i**ns, **D**NA, **S**ugars.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c075-->
