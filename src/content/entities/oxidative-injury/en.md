## Definition and epidemiology

Oxidative injury is damage to cells and tissues by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and related reactive molecules when their production exceeds the cell's protective capacity.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c001--> ROS are incompletely reduced oxygen species able to injure almost every class of biomolecule nearby.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c002--> The same molecules are also normal signaling intermediates and the weapons phagocytes use against microbes, so the problem is excess or misdirection rather than their mere presence.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c003--> As a shared mechanism of injury rather than a disease, it has no epidemiology of its own.

## Etiology and risk factors

Oxidant stress comes both from the cell's own metabolism and from external exposures.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c004-->

- **Mitochondrial respiration:** electrons escape from the electron transport chain, notably at coenzyme Q, and reduce O₂ to superoxide.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c005--> Even in healthy cells about 1% to 3% of the oxygen entering mitochondria ends up as ROS.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c006-->
- **Metabolic enzymes:** cytochrome P450 mixed-function oxygenases and peroxisomal oxidases produce ROS during routine work.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c007-->
- **Phagocytes:** NADPH oxidase drives the respiratory burst of neutrophils and macrophages, and myeloperoxidase then turns H₂O₂ into hypochlorous acid.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c008-->
- **Reperfusion:** xanthine oxidase, which rises in ischemic endothelium, uses the returning oxygen to oxidize purines left over from ATP breakdown, releasing superoxide.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c009-->
- **Ionizing radiation:** splits water (radiolysis) and so yields hydroxyl radicals directly.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c010-->
- **Transition metals:** free ferrous iron or cuprous copper converts H₂O₂ into hydroxyl radicals.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c011-->
- **Chemicals:** heavy metals, xenobiotics and many drugs act as oxidants or electrophiles.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c012-->
- **Excess oxygen:** high inspired oxygen, as in oxygen therapy, is itself a source of oxidant stress.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**The reduction ladder.** Turning O₂ into water takes four electrons; stopping after one, two or three gives superoxide (O₂•⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and the hydroxyl radical (•OH).<!--c:oxidative-injury-c014-->

- Superoxide acts close to where it forms and is the parent of the other ROS.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c015-->
- H₂O₂ is only mildly harmful on its own, but it crosses membranes and spreads oxidant stress to other compartments.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c016-->
- The hydroxyl radical is the most reactive species and the main attacker of macromolecules.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c017--> It forms from H₂O₂ plus Fe²⁺ (Fenton reaction) or from superoxide plus H₂O₂ (Haber–Weiss reaction).<!--c:oxidative-injury-c018-->
- Nitric oxide combines with superoxide to give peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), which damages lipids, proteins and DNA.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c019-->
- Hypochlorous acid from neutrophil myeloperoxidase kills microbes and, once released outside the cell, host cells too.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c020-->

**Molecular targets.**

- **Lipids:** •OH removes a hydrogen atom from an unsaturated fatty acid of membrane phospholipids; the lipid radical takes up O₂, and the peroxyl radical formed attacks the next fatty acid, so damage spreads as a chain reaction.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c021--> Unstable lipid peroxides fall apart into small aldehydes, and the membrane loses its integrity.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c022--> In mitochondria, peroxidation of cardiolipin, which is rich in unsaturated fatty acids, cripples the electron transport chain and ATP output.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c023-->
- **Proteins:** residues containing sulfur or nitrogen (cysteine, methionine, histidine, arginine, proline) are the most exposed; oxidized proteins fragment, cross-link and aggregate.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c024--> Large oxidized aggregates trap Fe²⁺, which generates more ROS and enlarges the aggregate in a vicious circle.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c025-->
- **Carbohydrates:** oxidized sugars form reactive intermediates that modify proteins into advanced glycation end-products (AGEs).<!--c:oxidative-injury-c026-->
- **DNA:** •OH breaks strands, alters bases and cross-links strands; damage beyond repair capacity ends in permanent mutation or cell death.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c027-->
- **Extracellular matrix:** collagen, elastin, fibronectin, laminin and basement membranes can also be oxidized.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c028-->

**Antioxidant defenses.** Whether injury follows depends largely on how well the cell's defenses perform.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c029-->

- **Enzymes:** superoxide dismutases convert O₂•⁻ to H₂O₂ (Cu/Zn SOD in the cytosol, Mn SOD in mitochondria); catalase in peroxisomes and glutathione peroxidase (GPX) in cytosol and mitochondria reduce H₂O₂ to water.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c030--> Peroxiredoxins (recycled by thioredoxin), NQO1 and heme oxygenase add further protection.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c031-->
- **Glutathione:** reduced glutathione (GSH) is the most abundant endogenous antioxidant; GPX oxidizes it to GSSG, which is either reduced again by glutathione reductase or exported from the cell.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c032-->
- **Scavengers:** fat-soluble vitamin E breaks lipid-peroxidation chains inside membranes; water-soluble vitamin C reacts with ROS and regenerates vitamin E; lipid-soluble vitamin A precursors also interrupt radical chains.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c033-->
- **Outside the cell:** albumin, GSH, ascorbate, α-tocopherol and an extracellular SOD.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c034-->

**Adaptive response: Nrf2.** The transcription factor Nrf2 is the most powerful single inducer of antioxidant defenses.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c035--> At rest Keap1, together with a CUL3 E3 ubiquitin ligase, holds Nrf2 in the cytosol and sends it to the proteasome.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c036--> Under stress, p53-induced sestrins and p62 release Nrf2, and p62 routes Keap1 to autophagic degradation.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c037--> Free Nrf2 enters the nucleus and activates genes carrying antioxidant response elements (AREs), such as NQO1, glutathione S-transferases, peroxiredoxins and ferritins.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c038--> Oxidant sensors amplify the signal: H₂O₂ inactivates protein tyrosine phosphatases through their active-site cysteines, which boosts kinase signaling, and MAP kinases activate AP-1 and NF-κB alongside Nrf2.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c039-->

**p53 as a switch.** Under mild oxidant stress p53 keeps antioxidant genes running and favors survival; under severe stress it switches to genes that weaken defenses and drive the cell toward death.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c040-->

## Microscopic morphology

Oxidative damage is recognized through the lesions it produces: in acetaminophen toxicity, for example, a scant inflammatory infiltrate surrounds centrilobular hepatocytes that are coagulatively necrotic or hydropically swollen, some holding small lipid droplets.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c041--> A long-term trace is lipofuscin, a golden-brown pigment of peroxidized lipids and oxidized, cross-linked proteins that builds up with age in long-lived cells such as neurons, cardiac myocytes and hepatocytes.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c042-->

## Clinical presentation

Oxidative injury is a mechanism shared by many conditions, for example:

- **Ischemia–reperfusion:** ROS formed during ischemia surge when oxygen returns, from xanthine oxidase, a burst of mitochondrial electron flow and recruited neutrophils.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c043--> In the heart, as much as half of the eventual infarct can be attributed to reperfusion injury.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c044-->
- **Acetaminophen overdose:** the centrilobular necrosis reflects lost antioxidant defence, because a toxic metabolite of the drug uses up hepatocyte glutathione and leaves peroxides unneutralized.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c045--> In the United States it leads all other causes of acute liver failure.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c046-->
- **Chronic granulomatous disease:** inherited NADPH oxidase deficiency leaves phagocytes unable to make superoxide and H₂O₂, so patients suffer recurrent infections; it shows that controlled ROS production is needed.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c047-->
- **Oxygen toxicity:** in premature infants, high inspired oxygen injures developing alveoli and contributes to bronchopulmonary dysplasia.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c048-->
- **Radiation, chemical carcinogenesis, aging and degenerative diseases** are all linked to excess ROS.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c049-->
- **Protein aggregation:** when antioxidant defenses fail, especially in non-dividing cells, oxidized proteins misfold and form toxic aggregates.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c050-->

## Complications and prognosis

With mild to moderate stress, defenses restore balance and mildly oxidized proteins are cleared by the 20S proteasome.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c051--> With severe stress, ROS together with calcium overload open the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, collapse the membrane potential and trigger cell death programs.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c052--> Beyond acute cell death, oxidative damage can cause lasting cell dysfunction and malignant transformation.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c053-->

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[hypoxic-ischemic-injury]] | The primary lesion is ATP depletion with failure of ion pumps; ROS add to the damage but do not start it.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c054--> | Clinical context of reduced perfusion or oxygen |
| [[ischemia-reperfusion-injury]] | A specific setting of oxidative injury: damage is amplified when oxygen returns to ischemic tissue.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c055--> | Timing relative to restored blood flow |
| Phagocyte killing ([[acute-inflammation]]) | The same ROS are produced on purpose inside phagocytes to destroy ingested microbes.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c056--> | Inflammatory setting; injury to host cells only when ROS are released |

## High-yield summary

- O₂ gains electrons stepwise: **superoxide**, then **hydrogen peroxide**, then the **hydroxyl radical**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c058-->
- The **hydroxyl radical** is the most damaging ROS; the **Fenton reaction** makes it from H₂O₂ and Fe²⁺.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c059-->
- **Lipid peroxidation** is a self-propagating chain in membranes, interrupted by **vitamin E**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c060-->
- **SOD** turns superoxide into H₂O₂; **catalase** (peroxisomes) and **glutathione peroxidase** (cytosol, mitochondria) turn H₂O₂ into water.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c061-->
- **Reduced glutathione (GSH)** is the most abundant endogenous antioxidant.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c062-->
- **Nrf2**, released from **Keap1**, switches on genes with **antioxidant response elements**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c063-->
- In reperfusion, **xanthine oxidase** and neutrophils produce a burst of ROS.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c064-->
- **Acetaminophen** depletes **glutathione** and causes **centrilobular necrosis**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c065-->
- **NADPH oxidase** deficiency causes **chronic granulomatous disease**.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c066-->
- **Lipofuscin** is the "wear and tear" residue of oxidized lipids and proteins.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c067-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- H₂O₂ is not very toxic by itself; the danger is its conversion to •OH in the presence of free iron.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c068-->
- Nitric oxide is double-edged: it dilates vessels and limits iron uptake, yet with superoxide it forms peroxynitrite.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c069-->
- Catalase works mainly in peroxisomes; GPX works in the cytosol and mitochondria.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c070-->
- ROS are not only harmful: without them phagocytes cannot kill microbes, as chronic granulomatous disease shows.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c071-->
- p53 is not purely protective: under severe oxidant stress it weakens antioxidant defenses and promotes cell death.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c072-->

## Mnemonics

**"1-2-3: Super, Peroxide, Hydroxyl"** (original): one electron gives superoxide, two give peroxide, three give hydroxyl, and the fourth finishes as water.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c073-->

**"SOD starts, CAT and GPX finish"** (original): SOD makes H₂O₂ from superoxide; catalase and GPX turn it into water.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c074-->

**"LiPiDS"** (original) for the targets: **Li**pids, **P**rote**i**ns, **D**NA, **S**ugars.<!--c:oxidative-injury-c075-->
