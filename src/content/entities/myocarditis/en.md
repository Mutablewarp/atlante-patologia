## Definition and epidemiology
**What is myocarditis?** It is inflammation of heart muscle with myocyte death; ischemic heart disease is excluded by definition.<!--c:myocarditis-c001--> Many cases are silent, so incidence is unclear.<!--c:myocarditis-c002--> Children aged 1 to 10 are affected most, but any age can be.<!--c:myocarditis-c003--> In a young person with a healthy heart, sudden heart failure has few causes; myocarditis is one.<!--c:myocarditis-c004--> A severe case may end in sudden cardiac death, through arrhythmia or widespread necrosis.<!--c:myocarditis-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors
**What causes myocarditis?** Most cases are called viral on circumstantial grounds, unless a biopsy shows viral genomes.<!--c:myocarditis-c006--> Coxsackievirus and other enteroviruses led Western findings in the late 20th century.<!--c:myocarditis-c007--> Genome-detection methods have since added H1N1 influenza and parvovirus B19 to the culprits.<!--c:myocarditis-c008--> The heart can also be infected by protozoa, fungi, bacteria, rickettsiae and metazoan parasites.<!--c:myocarditis-c009--> Diphtheria bacilli release toxins that attack the heart and may kill through myocarditis.<!--c:myocarditis-c010--> *Trypanosoma cruzi*, a protozoan, causes Chagas disease, a frequent source of myocarditis in South America.<!--c:myocarditis-c011--> Noninfectious causes include drug reactions, radiation, uremia, sarcoidosis, rheumatic fever and other immune diseases.<!--c:myocarditis-c012--> Some cases remain idiopathic.<!--c:myocarditis-c013--> Coxsackievirus and adenovirus drive most of the unusually frequent viral myocarditis seen in AIDS; HIV in myocytes plays little part.<!--c:myocarditis-c014-->

## Pathogenesis
**How does a virus injure the heart?** Innate immunity switches on at the very start, when the virus gets into myocytes.<!--c:myocarditis-c015--> Children are often affected, possibly because their abundant coxsackie-adenovirus receptor lets coxsackievirus and adenovirus in.<!--c:myocarditis-c016--> By cutting dystrophin, protease 2A leaves myocytes leaky and weak; the virus needs this enzyme to replicate.<!--c:myocarditis-c017--> Caspase cleavage by viral proteases is another route to apoptosis.<!--c:myocarditis-c018--> Antibodies to viral proteins probably also hit cardiac proteins by molecular mimicry.<!--c:myocarditis-c019--> Inflammation is minimal at this stage, while myocytes degenerate and undergo apoptosis.<!--c:myocarditis-c020--> Within days to weeks, T cells, macrophages and NK cells accumulate at infected sites, as infected myocytes release cytokines.<!--c:myocarditis-c021--> Once T cells have removed the virus, the heart contracts normally again and scar replaces the dead areas.<!--c:myocarditis-c022--> Dilated cardiomyopathy is a possible outcome when the virus is poorly cleared or immune activation persists.<!--c:myocarditis-c023--> Parvovirus B19 instead infects endothelial cells, and the resulting microvascular injury can cause local ischemia.<!--c:myocarditis-c024--> Sarcoidosis has no known infectious trigger despite searches; an immune disorder is the main suspect.<!--c:myocarditis-c025--> Giant cell myocarditis may be autoimmune, with dysregulated T cells.<!--c:myocarditis-c026-->

## Gross morphology
**What does the heart look like?** Giant cell myocarditis leaves a limp, dilated heart, sometimes with wall thrombi, at autopsy.<!--c:myocarditis-c027--> Sarcoid granulomas do most harm at the base of the interventricular septum.<!--c:myocarditis-c028-->

## Microscopic morphology
**What do the slides show?** Viral histology is mostly nonspecific, like toxic myocarditis.<!--c:myocarditis-c029--> T cells and macrophages infiltrate the interstitium, in patches or diffusely, while acquired immunity is active.<!--c:myocarditis-c030--> Single myocytes are often ringed by inflammatory cells.<!--c:myocarditis-c031--> Focal myocyte necrosis is present.<!--c:myocarditis-c032--> Giant cells with several nuclei can occur too.<!--c:myocarditis-c033--> Fibroblasts lay down interstitial collagen as the lesion resolves.<!--c:myocarditis-c034--> Extensive necrosis can mimic an acute infarct, although neutrophils are uncommon.<!--c:myocarditis-c035-->

**Which other causes leave a recognizable pattern?**
- Bacterial: neutrophil-rich foci throughout; infective endocarditis often supplies septic emboli that lodge in coronaries and cause microabscesses.<!--c:myocarditis-c036-->
- Rickettsial: vasculitis of small coronary vessels.<!--c:myocarditis-c037-->
- Fungal: mostly in immunocompromised patients.<!--c:myocarditis-c038-->
- Toxoplasmosis: parasites inside myocytes, with focal inflammation rich in neutrophils and eosinophils.<!--c:myocarditis-c039-->
- Chagas disease: parasites in myocytes, with an infiltrate mostly of macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells.<!--c:myocarditis-c040-->
- Hypersensitivity: many eosinophils among the lymphocytes and plasma cells of an interstitial, perivascular infiltrate.<!--c:myocarditis-c041-->
- Hypersensitivity: even intense inflammation typically comes without myocyte necrosis.<!--c:myocarditis-c042-->
- Sarcoidosis: noncaseating granulomas in the muscle with extensive myocyte loss.<!--c:myocarditis-c043-->
- Giant cell myocarditis: sinuous necrotic myocardial zones are rimmed by lymphocytes and macrophage-derived giant cells.<!--c:myocarditis-c044-->
- Giant cell myocarditis: giant cells are plentiful, yet granulomas are absent.<!--c:myocarditis-c045-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings
**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** finding viral genomes in heart biopsy tissue with special studies is what lifts a viral cause above circumstantial evidence.<!--c:myocarditis-c046-->
- Biopsy-proven active viral myocarditis responded poorly in trials of immunosuppression.<!--c:myocarditis-c047-->
- Immunosuppression may help briefly when the biopsy shows no viral genome yet autoantibodies against the heart persist.<!--c:myocarditis-c048-->
- On histology, the type of infiltrate (lymphocytes, eosinophils, giant cells, granulomas) points to the subtype.<!--c:myocarditis-c049-->
- The reference pages do not cover biopsy sampling, special stains, IHC or electron microscopy.

## Clinical presentation
**How does it present?** Symptoms, when they occur, begin some weeks after infection, as acquired immunity responds; many patients have none.<!--c:myocarditis-c050--> Some patients die suddenly in the acute phase, yet most recover.<!--c:myocarditis-c051--> In the US, autopsies of sudden unexpected deaths under age 35 show myocarditis in a few percent.<!--c:myocarditis-c052--> Hypersensitivity myocarditis is usually silent, so autopsy often finds it by chance.<!--c:myocarditis-c053--> Rarely it causes fatal ventricular arrhythmias; it can also mimic acute ischemia with chest pain and ECG changes.<!--c:myocarditis-c054--> About a quarter of sarcoidosis cases have some cardiac granulomas at autopsy.<!--c:myocarditis-c055--> Fewer than 5% of patients have cardiac symptoms.<!--c:myocarditis-c056--> The septal base carries conduction tissue, so cardiac sarcoid often causes heart block and can bring ventricular tachyarrhythmias and sudden death.<!--c:myocarditis-c057--> Chagas disease is insect-borne and endemic to Latin America.<!--c:myocarditis-c058--> Pericarditis is also caused by most of the viruses behind myocarditis.<!--c:myocarditis-c059--> Adults in their third to fifth decades are usually the ones hit by giant cell myocarditis.<!--c:myocarditis-c060-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging
**Which tests matter?** The reference pages cover no blood tests or imaging; see the biopsy block above.

## Complications and prognosis
**How can it end?** Some patients progress to chronic heart failure resembling dilated cardiomyopathy.<!--c:myocarditis-c061--> Chronic cardiomyopathy with heart failure and arrhythmia affects a third to half of Chagas patients.<!--c:myocarditis-c062--> Giant cell myocarditis kills fast: the heart fails, or an arrhythmia stops it suddenly.<!--c:myocarditis-c063--> Transplantation may be needed for some patients; for others, long-term immunosuppression helps.<!--c:myocarditis-c064--> Recurrence in the transplanted heart occurs in 25% of cases.<!--c:myocarditis-c065--> In symptomatic hypersensitivity myocarditis, the culprit drug is stopped and steroids or other immunosuppressants may follow.<!--c:myocarditis-c066-->

## High-yield summary
- **Myocarditis** is myocardial inflammation with myocyte necrosis, not ischemic in origin.<!--c:myocarditis-c077-->
- **Viruses** cause most cases; coxsackievirus and adenovirus enter through **CAR**.<!--c:myocarditis-c078-->
- Viral histology: **T cells and macrophages** with focal necrosis, nonspecific.<!--c:myocarditis-c079-->
- **Hypersensitivity**: eosinophils, no myocyte necrosis; stop the drug.<!--c:myocarditis-c080-->
- **Giant cell**: giant cells without granulomas, rapidly fatal, recurs in transplants.<!--c:myocarditis-c081-->
- **Sarcoid**: noncaseating granulomas at the septal base, heart block, ventricular tachyarrhythmias.<!--c:myocarditis-c082-->
- **Chagas**: *Trypanosoma cruzi*, Latin America.<!--c:myocarditis-c083-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps
**Is myocarditis always viral?** No. Viruses are the commonest cause, but bacteria, parasites, drugs, immune disease and sarcoidosis also cause it.<!--c:myocarditis-c084-->

**Is a neutrophilic infiltrate typical?** No. Neutrophils are uncommon in viral myocarditis; they dominate in bacterial infection.<!--c:myocarditis-c085-->

**Do giant cells mean granulomas?** Not in giant cell myocarditis, which has no granulomas, unlike sarcoidosis.<!--c:myocarditis-c086-->

**Does immunosuppression cure biopsy-proven viral myocarditis?** No. Trials in it were disappointing.<!--c:myocarditis-c087-->

## Mnemonics
**EGG:** **E**osinophils mean drug hypersensitivity; **G**iant cells without granulomas mean giant cell myocarditis; **G**ranulomas that are noncaseating mean sarcoidosis.<!--c:myocarditis-c088-->
