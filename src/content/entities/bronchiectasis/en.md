## Definition and epidemiology

**What is bronchiectasis?** The bronchi stay permanently widened because the muscle and elastic tissue of their wall has been destroyed.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c001--> After a bacterial or viral airway infection, bronchi can also widen for a while and then recover, although recovery may take months.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c002--> Vaccines and antibiotics have made the disease less common.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c003--> Today the infection that precedes most cases is typically adenovirus or RSV.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c004--> In low-resource regions, childhood respiratory infections remain a cause.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Why do bronchi widen, and what is the first split?** The first split is obstructive versus nonobstructive.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c006--> A mechanical block in a central bronchus produces the obstructive form, which is localized to the lung downstream of the block.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c007--> Such a block can come from enlarged lymph nodes, a tumor, a mucus plug in asthma or an inhaled foreign body.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c008--> The nonobstructive form can be localized or generalized, and it usually appears after a respiratory infection or with defective airway defenses.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c009--> Most generalized disease stems from an acquired condition that lets microbes into the airways or from an inborn defect of host defense.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c010-->

**Which acquired and inherited conditions predispose?**
- Acquired: chronic bronchitis, nasogastric intubation, an incompetent lower esophageal sphincter, and neurologic disease that disturbs cough and swallowing.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c011-->
- Inherited: cystic fibrosis ([[cystic-fibrosis]]), defective cilia, and antibody deficiency (hypogammaglobulinemia or low IgG subclasses).<!--c:bronchiectasis-c012-->

**What is Kartagener syndrome?** It is one form of ciliary dyskinesia, with the triad of dextrocardia, bronchiectasis and sinusitis.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c013--> The cause is abnormal dynein arms, the parts of the cilium that power and control its beat.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c014--> Cilia fail everywhere in the body, so sterility affects men and women alike.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c015--> Bronchiectasis arises because defective cilia let infections of both the upper and the lower airways keep returning.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c016-->

**Can immune defects cause it?** Yes. In hypogammaglobulinemia, low IgA or IgG leaves the lungs open to repeated viral and bacterial infection.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c017--> Neutrophil defects, inherited or acquired, make respiratory infection and bronchiectasis more likely.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c018-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does the wall get destroyed?** The whole wall of bronchi and bronchioles can be lost to severe inflammation.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c019--> Damaged bronchi dilate when the lung tissue downstream of them collapses.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c020--> The damage feeds itself: stagnant mucus gets infected, and the infection destroys more wall.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c021--> The surface epithelium of inflamed central airways is altered, and the airways make too much mucus.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c022-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the lung look like?** Generalized disease usually hits both lungs, chiefly the lower lobes and the left side more than the right.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c023--> A site of earlier obstruction or infection can become the location of localized disease.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c024--> The dilated bronchi have thick walls that look white or yellow.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c025--> Inside the lumens there is often mucus that is thick and contains pus.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c026-->

**What are the patterns of dilation?** They are cylindrical, varicose and saccular, named by the level of the bronchial tree involved.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c027--> How much lung is involved, and how badly, outweighs the pattern name.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c028--> Around the widened, thick-walled bronchi of a surgically removed lobe, the lung tissue is collapsed and scarred.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c029-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What changes are seen in the bronchial wall?** Goblet cells increase in number, and the surface epithelium undergoes squamous metaplasia.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c030--> Bronchial walls often contain lymphoid follicles.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c031--> In the outer lung, many small airways are scarred shut.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c032--> The inflamed wall and its fibrous tissue are supplied by bronchial arteries, which become enlarged.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c033-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** surgical resection may be needed for localized disease, and the removed lobe can then be examined.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c034-->
- On sections of the lobe, look for the wall changes of the Microscopic section: squamous metaplasia, goblet cells, lymphoid follicles, scarred small airways.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c035-->
- The reference pages name no special stain, immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy or molecular test for the general diagnosis.

## Clinical presentation

**What does the patient complain of?** The patient coughs chronically and brings up large volumes of sputum that contains pus.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c036--> Bronchial inflammation can eat into adjacent bronchial arteries, which is why hemoptysis is common.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c037--> The degree of dyspnea and wheezing depends on how widespread the disease is.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c038--> Pneumonia is common.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c039-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it diagnosed?** Widened bronchi with thickened walls are what the chest radiograph shows.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c040--> The definitive diagnosis comes from a CT scan of the lung.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c041--> Localized disease that bleeds heavily or leads to pneumonia may need surgical resection.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c042-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens in long-standing disease?** Long-standing disease can end in chronic hypoxia and pulmonary hypertension.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c043--> In generalized disease, resection mainly relieves symptoms and seldom cures.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c044-->

## High-yield summary

- Bronchiectasis is **irreversible dilation** of the bronchi once their wall loses its muscle and elastic tissue.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c048-->
- It is **obstructive** (localized, beyond a block) or **nonobstructive** (after infection or defective defenses).<!--c:bronchiectasis-c049-->
- Inherited associations: **cystic fibrosis**, **ciliary dyskinesia**, **hypogammaglobulinemia**.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c050-->
- **Kartagener syndrome** is dextrocardia, bronchiectasis and sinusitis.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c051-->
- Patterns are **cylindrical, varicose and saccular**; severity and extent matter more.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c052-->
- **CT** is the definitive test.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c053-->
- Complications include **hemoptysis**, pneumonia, chronic hypoxia and **pulmonary hypertension**.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c054-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is bronchiectasis always irreversible?** No. An acute widening after an airway infection can be reversible, though recovery may take months.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c055-->

**Is the pattern, cylindrical or saccular, the key to the diagnosis?** No. The label is less important than severity and extent.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c056-->

**Is bronchiectasis always bilateral?** No. Generalized disease is usually bilateral, but obstructive disease is localized.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c057-->

**Is Kartagener syndrome the same as ciliary dyskinesia?** No. It is one of the ciliary dyskinesias, defined by the triad with dextrocardia and sinusitis.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c058-->

## Mnemonics

**OICK:** **O**bstruction, **I**nfection, **C**ystic fibrosis, **K**artagener (ciliary dyskinesia) are the main routes to dilated bronchi.<!--c:bronchiectasis-c059-->
