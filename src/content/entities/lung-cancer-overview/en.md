## Definition and epidemiology

**What is lung cancer, and how is it divided?** It is the leading cause of cancer death worldwide.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c001--> Pathologists separate four main types: small cell, squamous cell, large cell and glandular (adenocarcinoma).<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c002-->

The old name "bronchogenic carcinoma" is discouraged, since in roughly one case in four the tumor cannot be traced to a bronchus.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c003--> More women now smoke, so the old excess of cases in men is shrinking.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c004-->

<aside class="edition">Classification, grading cut-offs and testing rules change between editions. Check the current WHO classification and staging manual before quoting a category or a number.</aside>

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes it?** Cigarette smoking dominates: in the United States, the share of lung cancers found in smokers is 85% to 90%.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c005--> Smoking raises the risk of every main type, small cell included.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c006--> Lung cancer in a never-smoker is most often an adenocarcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c007-->

## Pathogenesis

**What happens in the bronchial epithelium and in the genes?** Smoking injures the bronchial lining, which regrows from basal cells as squamous metaplasia; this metaplasia can turn dysplastic, then in situ, then invasive carcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c008--> No one mutation is responsible for lung cancer.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c009--> Some mutations are common and matter for treatment:
- Adenocarcinomas with an activating **EGFR** mutation respond to tyrosine kinase inhibitors.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c010--> Women, Asians and nonsmokers carry these mutations more often.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c011-->
- A **KRAS** mutation in adenocarcinoma is linked to smoking and to a worse outcome.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c012-->
- Adenocarcinomas with an **EML4-ALK** fusion respond to crizotinib; the fusion occurs mostly in nonsmokers.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c013-->
- A **ROS1** fusion leaves the kinase domain intact, so crizotinib can target these tumors.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c014-->
- Small cell carcinoma has **TP53** and **RB** mutations far more often than the other types.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c015-->
- Loss of part of chromosome 3p is frequent in every type.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c016-->

## Gross morphology

**Where does squamous cell carcinoma sit, and what does it look like?** Most are central, starting in major or segmental bronchi.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c017--> It ulcerates, breaks through the bronchial wall into adjacent lung, and feels firm and grayish white.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c018--> Central cavitation is frequent.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c019-->

## Microscopic morphology

**How is a lung cancer typed under the microscope?** The grade comes from the least differentiated area, whereas the type is named from the most differentiated one.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c020--> A tumor that contains any small cell component counts as small cell carcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c021--> Finding squamous or glandular foci in a mostly large-cell tumor makes it a squamous carcinoma or adenocarcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c022-->

Squamous cell carcinoma has three subtypes: keratinizing, nonkeratinizing and basaloid.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c023--> Keratin pearls mark well-differentiated tumors, and some also show intercellular bridges.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c024-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**

**How it is studied:**
- Early cancers are mostly resected, so the pathologist can examine the whole tumor.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c025--> Unresectable advanced disease is typed on small biopsies and cytology instead of a resection.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c026-->
- Cells forming glands in a Papanicolaou-stained needle aspirate suggest adenocarcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c027--> Aspirated squamous cancer cells show dark nuclei with sharp corners and thick eosinophilic cytoplasm.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c028-->
- Nuclear **TTF-1** positivity suggests a lung primary with glandular differentiation.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c029-->
- **p40**, a squamous marker, helps when keratin is absent or the tumor is basaloid, if staining is diffuse.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c030-->
- Before treatment, EGFR mutation and ALK or ROS fusion status must be checked at least in adenocarcinoma and unsubtyped non–small cell carcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c031-->
- No proven targeted drug exists for squamous cell carcinoma, so molecular screening is not a routine step.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c032-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** Local effects are chest pain, dyspnea, hemoptysis, cough, pneumonia behind an obstruction and pleural effusion.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c033--> Shoulder pain that runs down the arm can come from a Pancoast tumor (usually squamous) in the lung apex, which may involve the C8, T1 and T2 nerves.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c034--> Sympathetic nerve paralysis in the neck from a Pancoast tumor produces Horner syndrome: ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis and enophthalmos.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c035--> The superior vena cava can be blocked by tumor growing in the mediastinum.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c036--> Spread most often reaches the hilar and mediastinal nodes; the adrenal gland, liver, bone and brain are frequent distant sites.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c037-->

**Which paraneoplastic syndromes occur?** See [[paraneoplastic-syndromes]].
- Lung cancer can bring clubbing, acanthosis nigricans, dermatomyositis or polymyositis, and Eaton–Lambert myasthenic syndrome.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c038-->
- Small cell carcinoma can cause SIADH or Cushing syndrome.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c039-->
- Squamous cell carcinoma can cause hypercalcemia by producing a parathyroid hormone-like substance.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c040-->
- Circulating anti-Hu antibodies mark the encephalomyelitis and sensory neuropathy that small cell carcinoma can cause.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c041-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How are type and stage established?** Lumping tumors under "non–small cell carcinoma" is discouraged, because newer chemotherapy and targeted drugs make the exact subtype matter.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c042--> Of all prognostic factors, stage matters most.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c043--> In TNM, M is distant metastasis or malignant pleural fluid, N is nodal spread (regional or distant), and T is tumor size with local or regional extent.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c044--> Any tumor with distant metastasis is stage IV; stages II and III are set mostly by regional nodes, and stage I is a small tumor without nodal or distant spread.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c045--> See [[tumor-grading-and-staging]].

## Complications and prognosis

**What is the outlook?** Small cell carcinoma is dismal: 5% or fewer of patients live 5 years.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c046--> Advanced adenocarcinoma may respond to the antifolate drug pemetrexed; squamous carcinoma does not.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c047--> Progression-free survival is longer when tumors with an EGFR mutation or ALK rearrangement receive targeted therapy.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c048-->

**What else can look like lung cancer, or which type is it?**

| [[lung-carcinoid-tumors]] | Not linked to smoking; presumably derived from bronchial neuroendocrine cells.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c053--> | Necrosis or a higher mitotic count marks the atypical form.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c054--> |

## High-yield summary

- Four major types: **small cell**, **large cell**, **adenocarcinoma** and **squamous cell** carcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c058-->
- **Stage** (TNM) is the best single predictor of prognosis.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c059-->
- **TTF-1** points to adenocarcinoma and lung origin; **p40** points to squamous cell carcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c060-->
- Adenocarcinoma needs **EGFR, ALK and ROS** testing; squamous cell carcinoma does not routinely.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c061-->
- **Small cell** carcinoma brings SIADH, Cushing syndrome and anti-Hu syndromes; **squamous** carcinoma brings hypercalcemia.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c062-->
- **Hamartoma** is a benign coin lesion with popcorn calcification.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c063-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is "bronchogenic carcinoma" the right name?** No. In roughly one case in four the tumor cannot be traced to a bronchus.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c064-->

**Do smokers get only squamous cell and small cell carcinoma?** No. Smokers can develop any major type, while lung cancer in never-smokers is mostly adenocarcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c065-->

**Is "non–small cell carcinoma" enough for the clinician?** No. Subtype and molecular tests guide treatment: pemetrexed can work in advanced adenocarcinoma, not in squamous carcinoma.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c066-->

**Is a hamartoma a malformation, not a tumor?** The name suggests it, but it is a true benign tumor.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c067-->

## Mnemonics

**Squamous = four C's:** Central, Cavitating, Cornified (keratin pearls) and high Calcium.<!--c:lung-cancer-overview-c068-->
