## Definition and epidemiology

**What is Wilson disease?** Copper piles up in the liver and the brain because of an inherited recessive defect in copper handling.<!--c:wilson-disease-c001--> The older name is hepatolenticular degeneration.<!--c:wilson-disease-c002--> About 1 in 150 to 180 people carries one mutant allele.<!--c:wilson-disease-c003--> Clinical disease affects about 1 in 30,000 children.<!--c:wilson-disease-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which gene is mutated?** Mutations in *ATP7B*, on chromosome 13, cause the disease.<!--c:wilson-disease-c005--> Before excretion, hepatocytes rely on the ATP-dependent transporter that this gene encodes (copper-transporting ATPase2) to move copper around inside them.<!--c:wilson-disease-c006--> Roughly 200 distinct mutations have been described.<!--c:wilson-disease-c007--> H1069Q causes most European and North American cases but few in India or Asia.<!--c:wilson-disease-c008--> Two different mutant alleles are usually present, so most patients are compound heterozygotes.<!--c:wilson-disease-c009-->

## Pathogenesis

**Why does copper accumulate?** The liver clears surplus copper into bile, because the diet usually supplies more than the body needs.<!--c:wilson-disease-c010--> In healthy hepatocytes, ceruloplasmin picks up copper and carries it into the circulation.<!--c:wilson-disease-c011--> With faulty ATP7B, less copper reaches bile or ceruloplasmin.<!--c:wilson-disease-c012--> Hepatic copper overload is thought to explain why serum ceruloplasmin is very low.<!--c:wilson-disease-c013-->

**How does copper reach other organs?** Copper-damaged hepatocytes die and spill copper into the blood, which lodges in other organs.<!--c:wilson-disease-c014--> The brain accumulates copper because bile excretion of the metal is defective.<!--c:wilson-disease-c015--> How copper injures cells is not fully clear.<!--c:wilson-disease-c016--> Copper, like iron, can drive the Fenton reaction that turns hydrogen peroxide into hydroxyl radicals.<!--c:wilson-disease-c017--> Transplanting a liver cures the disease, which marks the liver as the key organ.<!--c:wilson-disease-c018-->

## Gross morphology

**What do the affected organs look like?** Over time the cirrhotic liver changes from micronodular to macronodular.<!--c:wilson-disease-c019--> A faint golden tint colors the lenticular nuclei of the brain.<!--c:wilson-disease-c020--> In a quarter of cases the putamen or deep neocortex contains tiny cysts or slit-like spaces.<!--c:wilson-disease-c021--> The Kayser–Fleischer ring is a golden-brown band that slit-lamp examination reveals at the edge of the cornea, where copper is deposited.<!--c:wilson-disease-c022-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the pathologist see?** Chronic hepatitis of the liver worsens from mild to severe.<!--c:wilson-disease-c023--> Steatosis may occur, along with severe hepatocyte injury.<!--c:wilson-disease-c024--> [[g:mallory-denk-bodies|Mallory–Denk hyalin]] is often found inside hepatocytes.<!--c:wilson-disease-c025--> Cholestasis is common.<!--c:wilson-disease-c026--> A cirrhotic liver can show hydropic change of hepatocytes.<!--c:wilson-disease-c027--> Typical brain findings are gliosis and mild loss of neurons.<!--c:wilson-disease-c028--> Alzheimer type II astrocytes also occur.<!--c:wilson-disease-c029--> The corneal brown ring comes from copper laid down in Descemet membrane.<!--c:wilson-disease-c030-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** routine H&E shows Mallory–Denk hyalin, steatosis and hepatocyte injury against a background of chronic hepatitis.<!--c:wilson-disease-c031-->
- Liver copper can be measured chemically on unfixed tissue or on paraffin blocks not yet cut.<!--c:wilson-disease-c032-->
- In Wilson disease the result is above 250 μg of copper per gram of dried liver.<!--c:wilson-disease-c033-->
- This card does not yet cover histochemical copper stains, immunohistochemistry or electron microscopy.

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** Half of patients have symptoms by adolescence.<!--c:wilson-disease-c034--> Beyond adolescence, most of the other patients first fall ill as young adults, though onset can be later.<!--c:wilson-disease-c035--> Liver disease is the first sign in about half.<!--c:wilson-disease-c036--> Neurologic complaints bring about a third to the clinic first.<!--c:wilson-disease-c037--> In about a tenth, psychiatric illness comes first.<!--c:wilson-disease-c038-->
- **Liver:** the chronic liver disease of Wilson disease can mimic other chronic hepatitis; early symptoms are vague.<!--c:wilson-disease-c039--> Late cirrhosis causes portal hypertension, liver failure and jaundice.<!--c:wilson-disease-c040--> Acute liver failure can be the first presentation.<!--c:wilson-disease-c041--> Wilson disease raises the risk of liver cancer, yet hemochromatosis raises it more.<!--c:wilson-disease-c042-->
- **Brain:** mild incoordination and tremor are the first neurologic signs.<!--c:wilson-disease-c043--> Dystonia and spasticity become disabling in untreated patients, after dysarthria and dysphagia have appeared.<!--c:wilson-disease-c044--> The movement disorder usually begins in the second decade and tends toward choreoathetosis.<!--c:wilson-disease-c045-->
- **Eye:** Kayser–Fleischer rings, brown-gold corneal bands in both eyes, veil the iris.<!--c:wilson-disease-c046--> Whenever neurologic disease is present, the eyes are invariably affected.<!--c:wilson-disease-c047--> In some patients copper forms green discs on the front of the lens capsule: sunflower cataracts.<!--c:wilson-disease-c048-->
- **Bone:** spontaneous fractures are frequent, along with bone softening, bone thinning and joint disease.<!--c:wilson-disease-c049-->
- **Kidney:** glomerular and tubular damage lowers filtration and lets phosphate, amino acids and protein escape into the urine.<!--c:wilson-disease-c050--> The cause is copper laid down in the renal tubules.<!--c:wilson-disease-c051-->
- **Blood:** in 15% of patients, a surge of free copper from the liver probably causes brief hemolysis.<!--c:wilson-disease-c052-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which findings point to the diagnosis?** Serum ceruloplasmin is very low.<!--c:wilson-disease-c053--> A Kayser–Fleischer ring is visible on slit-lamp examination.<!--c:wilson-disease-c054--> Anyone with a hyperkinetic movement disorder and liver or psychiatric signs should be tested for Wilson disease, most of all if it began in young adulthood.<!--c:wilson-disease-c055-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens with and without treatment?** Even children can develop cirrhosis, and it may arrive quickly.<!--c:wilson-disease-c056--> Therapy has two goals: stopping new copper from accumulating in tissues and clearing what is already there.<!--c:wilson-disease-c057--> Urinary copper loss rises with the chelators trientine and d-penicillamine.<!--c:wilson-disease-c058--> Both the liver disease and the brain dysfunction often reverse with treatment.<!--c:wilson-disease-c059--> Zinc in the diet blocks copper absorption in the intestine, and presymptomatic patients are given it.<!--c:wilson-disease-c060--> Liver transplantation is curative.<!--c:wilson-disease-c061--> Damage to liver and brain can become permanent when the disease is left untreated.<!--c:wilson-disease-c062--> Some patients stay presymptomatic because copper never reaches high enough levels.<!--c:wilson-disease-c063-->

## High-yield summary

- **ATP7B** mutation impairs biliary copper excretion and ceruloplasmin loading, so copper accumulates in the **liver** and **brain**.<!--c:wilson-disease-c071-->
- Liver: **Mallory–Denk hyalin**, **steatosis** and chronic hepatitis, with cirrhosis that turns from micronodular to macronodular.<!--c:wilson-disease-c072-->
- Brain: neuron loss and gliosis, cysts or clefts in the putamen, and golden **lenticular nuclei**.<!--c:wilson-disease-c073-->
- Eye: the **Kayser–Fleischer ring** is copper in Descemet membrane.<!--c:wilson-disease-c074-->
- Lab: **low ceruloplasmin** and liver copper above 250 μg/g dry weight.<!--c:wilson-disease-c075-->
- Treatment: **chelators** (trientine, d-penicillamine) or **zinc**; liver transplantation is curative.<!--c:wilson-disease-c076-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is Wilson disease only a liver disease?** No. It also affects brain, eye, kidney, bone and blood.<!--c:wilson-disease-c077-->

**Is serum copper the key lab clue?** No. Serum ceruloplasmin is very low, even though tissue copper is high.<!--c:wilson-disease-c078-->

**Does ATP7B encode ceruloplasmin?** No. It encodes a copper transporter, and low ceruloplasmin is a consequence.<!--c:wilson-disease-c079-->

**Is it an untreatable genetic disease?** No. Chelators treat it, zinc protects presymptomatic patients, and liver transplantation is curative.<!--c:wilson-disease-c080-->

**Are Alzheimer type II astrocytes specific to Alzheimer disease?** No. They are seen in Wilson disease and hepatic encephalopathy, where they signal a metabolic disturbance.<!--c:wilson-disease-c081-->

## Mnemonics

**Four Cs:** Copper, low Ceruloplasmin, Corneal ring, Cirrhosis.<!--c:wilson-disease-c082-->
