## Definition and epidemiology

Dysplasia is an abnormal state of an epithelium in which cells lose their uniform size and shape and no longer mature in an orderly sequence.<!--c:dysplasia-c001--> What the microscope shows is the visible footprint of epithelial growth and maturation gone wrong.<!--c:dysplasia-c002--> Pathologists regard it as a precancerous stage; in the cervix and the prostate, for instance, it belongs to the spectrum known as intraepithelial neoplasia.<!--c:dysplasia-c003--> In developmental pathology the same word has a different meaning, namely abnormal tissue formation during embryogenesis.<!--c:dysplasia-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

Dysplasia develops where injury persists, the same setting that produces metaplasia.<!--c:dysplasia-c005--> Any epithelium can be affected:<!--c:dysplasia-c006-->

- **Skin:** keratinocytes damaged by sunlight, as in actinic keratosis.<!--c:dysplasia-c007-->
- **Colon:** columnar mucosa chronically inflamed by ulcerative colitis.<!--c:dysplasia-c008-->
- **Uterine cervix:** squamous epithelium infected by human papillomavirus (HPV).<!--c:dysplasia-c009-->
- **Oral mucosa:** tobacco and alcohol, which predispose to oral squamous carcinoma, also cause white patches (leukoplakia).<!--c:dysplasia-c065-->
- **Metaplastic epithelium:** the squamous metaplasia of a smoker's bronchus and the glandular metaplasia of Barrett esophagus.<!--c:dysplasia-c010-->
- **Colorectal adenomas:** dysplasia is part of their definition, and most colon carcinomas arise from an adenoma.<!--c:dysplasia-c011-->

## Pathogenesis

**The normal reference.** Healthy squamous mucosa is built like a ladder of maturity: dividing cells sit at the base, with a large nucleus for their cytoplasm; flat, fully mature cells with a small nucleus lie at the top; and each layer in between is one step further up that ladder.<!--c:dysplasia-c012--> Dysplasia is the breakdown of this ordered sequence.<!--c:dysplasia-c013-->

**From injury to clone.**

- Persistent injury keeps the epithelium dividing, and it is in these dividing cells that dysplasia takes shape, one mutation after another, along the same route that leads to cancer.<!--c:dysplasia-c014-->
- Each round of DNA replication can introduce copying errors, so more divisions mean more chances of a harmful mutation.<!--c:dysplasia-c015-->
- Selection then does the rest: the clone that comes to fill the field descends from a single cell whose mutation helped it grow or survive, so that its offspring outnumbered those of its neighbors.<!--c:dysplasia-c016-->
- The expanding clone divides more often, collects further hits and slips progressively out of normal growth control.<!--c:dysplasia-c017-->

**Still reversible.** At this stage the loss of control is only partial, so removing the cause can let the tissue revert to normal.<!--c:dysplasia-c018-->

**The HPV model in the cervix.** Low-grade lesions reflect early infection, in which the viral DNA stays separate from the host chromosomes in many independent cells; progression to a high-grade lesion follows when a high-risk type inserts its DNA into the host genome and the E6 and E7 oncoproteins favor the expansion of a single clone.<!--c:dysplasia-c019-->

**The oral model.** In the mouth the sequence can be followed clinically: the precursor stages appear as white or red patches, and invasive squamous carcinoma usually develops from a carcinoma in situ.<!--c:dysplasia-c087-->

## Gross morphology

Dysplasia is defined under the microscope; in the colon of patients with ulcerative colitis it may lie in flat mucosa or form a raised lesion, called a dysplasia-associated lesion or mass (DALM).<!--c:dysplasia-c020-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Cytologic atypia**

- Cells vary in size and shape.<!--c:dysplasia-c021-->
- Nuclei are enlarged, irregular in outline and hyperchromatic.<!--c:dysplasia-c022-->
- The nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio is higher than normal.<!--c:dysplasia-c023-->

**Architectural atypia**

- Mitotic figures appear well above the basal layer.<!--c:dysplasia-c024-->
- Polarity is lost and the normal maturation from base to surface is replaced by a disorderly arrangement.<!--c:dysplasia-c025-->
- Low-grade dysplasia in a colonic adenoma has slender, dark, cigar-shaped nuclei that are only slightly stratified.<!--c:dysplasia-c026--> In high-grade dysplasia the gland architecture becomes more complex, at times cribriform, and the nuclei are more pleomorphic with conspicuous nucleoli.<!--c:dysplasia-c027-->

**Grading**

- Dysplasia is graded by the degree of its cytologic and architectural abnormalities; in the colon it is reported as low grade or high grade.<!--c:dysplasia-c028-->
- In the squamous epithelium of the cervix and anal canal the older grading was mild, moderate or severe; since 2012 the LAST terminology (LSIL/HSIL) has applied to all anogenital sites, the anal canal included.<!--c:dysplasia-c029-->
- Cervical terminology: low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) = CIN 1 = mild dysplasia; high-grade lesion (HSIL) = CIN 2 (moderate dysplasia) and CIN 3 (severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ).<!--c:dysplasia-c030-->
- Under the microscope, severe cervical dysplasia and cervical carcinoma in situ are hard to separate.<!--c:dysplasia-c031-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Specimen:** cervical smears, biopsies and loop excisions; endoscopic biopsies in Barrett esophagus and long-standing colitis; polypectomy specimens of colorectal adenomas; biopsies of white or red patches in the mouth.<!--c:dysplasia-c066-->
- **Cytology screens, histology grades:** the Pap smear is a screening test, yet from the cells shed at the cervical surface the cytologist can usually tell LSIL from HSIL, judging how abnormal the nuclei are and how far the cytoplasm has matured.<!--c:dysplasia-c067--> An abnormal smear leads to a biopsy: only a tissue section shows the whole thickness of the epithelium, and the final grade is assigned there.<!--c:dysplasia-c068-->
- **Targeting the sample:** a loop excision (LEEP) hands the pathologist the whole transformation zone and can diagnose and treat the lesion in one procedure; alternatively, colposcopy guides targeted biopsies to the abnormal areas, where HSIL typically shows a mosaic pattern or punctate vessels.<!--c:dysplasia-c069--> In colitis, surveillance biopsies may find more dysplasia when the mucosa is sprayed with a dye during endoscopy (chromoendoscopy).<!--c:dysplasia-c070-->
- **H&E, what to look for:** how far the cells mature from base to surface, how large and dark the nuclei are relative to the cytoplasm, how high the mitoses reach, and whether the basement membrane is intact.<!--c:dysplasia-c071--> In the cervix, read the epithelium layer by layer: in LSIL the basal layer stays orderly and the main change lies higher up, where koilocytes gather (squamous cells whose large, wrinkled nucleus is ringed by a clear halo); in HSIL immature atypical cells, rising from a disordered basal layer, fill much more of the thickness, and abnormal mitoses may betray aneuploidy.<!--c:dysplasia-c072--> In a polypectomy specimen, carcinoma counts as invasive only once neoplastic glands lie beneath the muscularis mucosae; the resection margin at the base then also matters, because a low-risk carcinoma removed with an adequate margin may need nothing more than the polypectomy.<!--c:dysplasia-c073-->
- **Immunohistochemistry:** lesions driven by high-risk HPV typically overexpress p16, and immunostaining shows it in the section.<!--c:dysplasia-c074--> In cervical biopsies, one study found that reading p16 slides alongside H&E made CIN grading much more consistent between pathologists; the pattern that points to a true high-grade lesion (CIN 2–3) is diffuse, strong staining.<!--c:dysplasia-c075-->
- **Molecular tests:** HPV DNA can be detected directly, and in situ hybridization shows where the viral nucleic acid lies inside the cells of a section.<!--c:dysplasia-c076--> Typing matters: HSIL usually harbors high-risk types such as HPV 16 and 18, whereas cervical condylomas often carry the low-risk types 6 and 11.<!--c:dysplasia-c077-->

## Clinical presentation

- Dysplasia is usually discovered by sampling epithelia at risk rather than by symptoms: in the cervix, abnormal cells reach the surface even in low-grade lesions and are shed into a cytologic smear.<!--c:dysplasia-c032-->
- Widespread cervical cytologic screening has sharply reduced the incidence of cervical carcinoma in Western countries.<!--c:dysplasia-c033-->
- Cervical squamous intraepithelial lesions generally occur before age 40, whereas invasive cervical cancer usually presents between 40 and 60.<!--c:dysplasia-c034-->
- Barrett esophagus is kept under endoscopic surveillance so that dysplasia is caught early.<!--c:dysplasia-c035-->
- In inflammatory bowel disease dysplasia is searched for actively: once the disease is established, most patients should have repeated surveillance colonoscopies with systematic biopsies.<!--c:dysplasia-c036-->
- In the mouth and the rest of the upper aerodigestive tract, precursor lesions are described clinically by their color: leukoplakia is a white patch, erythroplakia a red one.<!--c:dysplasia-c078-->
- Erythroplakia is less common than leukoplakia but more worrying, because a red patch often already harbors high-grade dysplasia or carcinoma, although many red patches may be merely inflammatory.<!--c:dysplasia-c079-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

- The diagnosis is morphologic, made on biopsy and, in the cervix, also on cytology.<!--c:dysplasia-c037-->
- The key boundary with invasive carcinoma is the basement membrane: a lesion that has not crossed it is in situ, whereas one that has crossed it can invade and reach vessels.<!--c:dysplasia-c038-->
- When heavy inflammation stops the pathologist from either confirming or ruling out dysplasia, the biopsy is signed out as "indefinite for dysplasia".<!--c:dysplasia-c039-->
- Leukoplakia names a clinical appearance, not a histologic diagnosis: under the microscope a white patch may turn out to be simple hyperkeratosis, dysplasia or even invasive squamous carcinoma, so only a biopsy can tell.<!--c:dysplasia-c080-->

## Complications and prognosis

- Dysplasia often regresses when the stimulus stops, for example after smoking cessation or when the immune system clears HPV-infected cervical cells.<!--c:dysplasia-c040-->
- In the cervix, LSIL seldom progresses and often disappears, whereas HSIL tends to progress and needs treatment.<!--c:dysplasia-c041-->
- Fewer than 1% of LSIL turn into invasive carcinoma and about 6% advance to HSIL, whereas about 70% regress; untreated HSIL, by contrast, progresses to invasive carcinoma in 10–20% of cases.<!--c:dysplasia-c081-->
- LSIL is often just watched with repeat smears, whereas HSIL is treated, for example by loop excision, which may suffice if its margins are clear, or by conization.<!--c:dysplasia-c082-->
- In the mouth, the more red, the higher the risk: leukoplakia carries a 10–12% risk of malignancy, erythroplakia the highest, and speckled leukoplakia, a white patch with red areas that counts as a form of erythroplakia, lies in between.<!--c:dysplasia-c083-->
- The carcinogenic agent acts on a whole mucosal field: in the mouth this is called field cancerization, and a patient may develop more than one squamous carcinoma, together or at different times; in the lower genital tract HPV acts in the same way, so a squamous carcinoma of the vagina or vulva can still arise later, and follow-up after a cervical lesion is lifelong.<!--c:dysplasia-c084-->
- Severe dysplasia is a reason to act, not to wait: either the lesion is excised or the cause that keeps it going is removed.<!--c:dysplasia-c042-->
- Colectomy is often advised once biopsy shows high-grade dysplasia in ulcerative colitis, because the later cancer risk is high.<!--c:dysplasia-c043-->
- In Barrett esophagus the grade of dysplasia, together with the length of the Barrett segment, sets the risk of adenocarcinoma, so dysplasia found at surveillance is usually treated endoscopically, by radiofrequency ablation plus resection of any nodular area.<!--c:dysplasia-c088-->
- Complete polypectomy cures an adenoma whose dysplasia has not gone beyond the mucosa; if left in place, a high-grade lesion may turn into invasive adenocarcinoma.<!--c:dysplasia-c044-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[metaplasia]] | One mature cell type is replaced by another adult cell type as an adaptation, without the nuclear atypia and disordered maturation of dysplasia; dysplasia may, however, arise inside metaplastic epithelium.<!--c:dysplasia-c045--> | Cytologic atypia and maturation on histology |
| [[hyperplasia]] | An increase in cell number driven by a stimulus, without the atypical nuclei and loss of orderly maturation that define dysplasia.<!--c:dysplasia-c046--> | Nuclear features and maturation pattern |
| [[carcinoma-in-situ]] | Atypia runs from the base to the surface with no maturation at all, but the basement membrane is still intact; in the cervix, severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ are grouped together as CIN 3.<!--c:dysplasia-c047--> | Full-thickness atypia without surface maturation, basement membrane intact |
| Invasive carcinoma | Unlike the lesions above, it can metastasize: its cells have crossed the basement membrane and lie in the stroma, within reach of blood and lymphatic vessels.<!--c:dysplasia-c048--> | Stromal invasion on biopsy |
| Inflammatory (reactive) atypia | Intense inflammation can produce changes that cannot be confidently separated from dysplasia.<!--c:dysplasia-c049--> | Rebiopsy once inflammation is controlled ("indefinite for dysplasia") |

## High-yield summary

- Dysplasia = **disordered growth and maturation** of an epithelium; it is **precancerous**.<!--c:dysplasia-c050-->
- Cytologic features: **pleomorphism**, **enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei**, **high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio**.<!--c:dysplasia-c051-->
- Architectural features: **loss of polarity and maturation**, **mitoses above the basal layer**.<!--c:dysplasia-c052-->
- Mechanism: **sequential mutations** in proliferating cells with **clonal selection**.<!--c:dysplasia-c053-->
- Grading: **low vs high grade**; cervix **LSIL = CIN 1**, **HSIL = CIN 2–3**.<!--c:dysplasia-c054-->
- Dysplasia is **not autonomous** and can **regress** when the stimulus stops.<!--c:dysplasia-c055-->
- **Carcinoma in situ** lies above an intact **basement membrane**; invasion begins when it is breached.<!--c:dysplasia-c056-->
- Oral **leukoplakia** (white patch) is a clinical term that needs a biopsy; **erythroplakia** (red patch) is rarer but more often hides high-grade dysplasia or carcinoma.<!--c:dysplasia-c085-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- **Dysplasia is not cancer.** Its cells are not fully autonomous, and the tissue can revert to normal.<!--c:dysplasia-c057-->
- Dysplasia does not progress inevitably: cervical LSIL often disappears on its own.<!--c:dysplasia-c058-->
- Dysplasia is not limited to squamous epithelium; it also arises in glandular mucosa such as the colon and in metaplastic epithelium such as Barrett esophagus.<!--c:dysplasia-c059-->
- "Renal dysplasia" or other developmental dysplasias are malformations of tissue formation, not precancerous epithelial lesions.<!--c:dysplasia-c060-->
- CIN 3 includes both severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ; the two are hard to tell apart.<!--c:dysplasia-c061-->
- Sarcomas and lymphomas have no in situ stage, because the cells they arise from do not rest on a basement membrane.<!--c:dysplasia-c062-->
- Leukoplakia, Barrett esophagus and long-standing colitis signal a higher risk but are not dysplasia in themselves: only a biopsy shows whether dysplasia, the actual precursor lesion, is present.<!--c:dysplasia-c086-->

## Mnemonics

**"Lost LAMP"** (original), the core features of dysplasia, pictured as an epithelium whose lamp has gone out so the cells can no longer find their order: **L**ayering and maturation lost, **A**typical nuclei (large, dark, irregular), **M**itoses up high, **P**leomorphism.<!--c:dysplasia-c063-->

**"Low lets go, high holds on"** (original): low-grade lesions such as cervical LSIL tend to regress, while high-grade lesions tend to persist and progress.<!--c:dysplasia-c064-->
