## Definition and epidemiology

Dysplasia is an abnormal state of an epithelium in which cells lose their uniform size and shape and no longer mature in an orderly sequence.<!--c:dysplasia-c001--> What the microscope shows is the visible footprint of epithelial growth and maturation gone wrong.<!--c:dysplasia-c002--> Pathologists regard it as a precancerous stage; in the cervix, prostate and bladder, for instance, it belongs to the spectrum known as intraepithelial neoplasia.<!--c:dysplasia-c003--> In developmental pathology the same word has a different meaning, namely abnormal tissue formation during embryogenesis.<!--c:dysplasia-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

Dysplasia develops where injury persists, the same setting that produces metaplasia.<!--c:dysplasia-c005--> Any epithelium can be affected:<!--c:dysplasia-c006-->

- **Skin:** keratinocytes damaged by sunlight, as in actinic keratosis.<!--c:dysplasia-c007-->
- **Colon:** columnar mucosa chronically inflamed by ulcerative colitis.<!--c:dysplasia-c008-->
- **Uterine cervix:** squamous epithelium infected by human papillomavirus (HPV).<!--c:dysplasia-c009-->
- **Metaplastic epithelium:** the squamous metaplasia of a smoker's bronchus and the glandular metaplasia of Barrett esophagus.<!--c:dysplasia-c010-->
- **Colorectal adenomas:** their epithelium is dysplastic by definition, and they are the usual precursors of colon carcinoma.<!--c:dysplasia-c011-->

## Pathogenesis

**The normal reference.** Healthy squamous mucosa renews itself from its base, where the plump basal cells proliferate; on the way up, cells stop dividing and enlarge their cytoplasm while their nuclei become small in proportion, ending as flattened surface cells.<!--c:dysplasia-c012--> Dysplasia is the breakdown of this ordered sequence.<!--c:dysplasia-c013-->

**From injury to clone.**

- Persistent injury keeps the epithelium dividing, and dysplasia is built from mutations that pile up step by step in those dividing cells, the same route that leads to cancer.<!--c:dysplasia-c014-->
- Each round of DNA replication can introduce copying errors, so more divisions mean more chances of a harmful mutation.<!--c:dysplasia-c015-->
- When one error favors growth or survival, the cell carrying it leaves more offspring than its neighbors, and its clone gradually takes over the field.<!--c:dysplasia-c016-->
- The expanding clone divides more often, collects further hits and slips progressively out of normal growth control.<!--c:dysplasia-c017-->

**Still reversible.** At this stage the loss of control is only partial, so removing the cause can let the tissue revert to normal.<!--c:dysplasia-c018-->

**The HPV model in the cervix.** Low-grade lesions reflect early infection, in which the viral DNA stays separate from the host chromosomes in many independent cells; progression to a high-grade lesion follows when a high-risk type inserts its DNA into the host genome and the E6 and E7 oncoproteins favor the expansion of a single clone.<!--c:dysplasia-c019-->

## Gross morphology

Dysplasia is defined under the microscope; in the colon of patients with ulcerative colitis it may lie in flat mucosa or form a raised lesion, called a dysplasia-associated lesion or mass (DALM).<!--c:dysplasia-c020-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Cytologic atypia**

- Cells vary in size and shape.<!--c:dysplasia-c021-->
- Nuclei are enlarged, irregular in outline and hyperchromatic.<!--c:dysplasia-c022-->
- The nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio is higher than normal.<!--c:dysplasia-c023-->

**Architectural atypia**

- Mitotic figures appear well above the basal layer.<!--c:dysplasia-c024-->
- Polarity is lost and the normal maturation from base to surface is replaced by a disorderly arrangement.<!--c:dysplasia-c025-->
- Low-grade dysplasia in a colonic adenoma has slender, dark, cigar-shaped nuclei that are only slightly stratified.<!--c:dysplasia-c026--> In high-grade dysplasia the gland architecture becomes more complex, at times cribriform, and the nuclei are more pleomorphic with conspicuous nucleoli.<!--c:dysplasia-c027-->

**Grading**

- Dysplasia is graded by the degree of its cytologic and architectural abnormalities; in the colon it is reported as low grade or high grade.<!--c:dysplasia-c028-->
- In the squamous epithelium of the cervix and anal canal the older grading was mild, moderate or severe; since 2012 the LAST terminology (LSIL/HSIL) has applied to all anogenital sites, the anal canal included.<!--c:dysplasia-c029-->
- Cervical terminology: low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) = CIN 1 = mild dysplasia; high-grade lesion (HSIL) = CIN 2 (moderate dysplasia) and CIN 3 (severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ).<!--c:dysplasia-c030-->
- Under the microscope, severe cervical dysplasia and cervical carcinoma in situ are hard to separate.<!--c:dysplasia-c031-->

Not applicable.

## Clinical presentation

- Dysplasia is usually discovered by sampling epithelia at risk rather than by symptoms: in the cervix, abnormal cells reach the surface even in low-grade lesions and are shed into a cytologic smear.<!--c:dysplasia-c032-->
- Widespread cervical cytologic screening has sharply reduced the incidence of cervical carcinoma in Western countries.<!--c:dysplasia-c033-->
- Cervical squamous intraepithelial lesions generally occur before age 40, whereas invasive cervical cancer usually presents between 40 and 60.<!--c:dysplasia-c034-->
- Patients with Barrett esophagus are followed with surveillance endoscopy to detect dysplasia early.<!--c:dysplasia-c035-->
- Surveillance colonoscopy with systematic biopsies is advised for most people with established inflammatory bowel disease, to catch dysplasia.<!--c:dysplasia-c036-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

- The diagnosis is morphologic, made on biopsy and, in the cervix, also on cytology.<!--c:dysplasia-c037-->
- The key boundary with invasive carcinoma is the basement membrane: a lesion that has not crossed it is in situ, whereas one that has crossed it can invade and reach vessels.<!--c:dysplasia-c038-->
- When heavy inflammation stops the pathologist from either confirming or ruling out dysplasia, the biopsy is signed out as "indefinite for dysplasia".<!--c:dysplasia-c039-->

## Complications and prognosis

- Dysplasia often regresses when the stimulus stops, for example after smoking cessation or when the immune system clears HPV-infected cervical cells.<!--c:dysplasia-c040-->
- In the cervix, LSIL seldom progresses and often disappears, whereas HSIL tends to progress and needs treatment.<!--c:dysplasia-c041-->
- Severe dysplasia calls for firm prevention, whether by eliminating the cause, ending the harmful exposure or excising the lesion.<!--c:dysplasia-c042-->
- Colectomy is usually advised once biopsy shows high-grade dysplasia in ulcerative colitis, because the later cancer risk is high.<!--c:dysplasia-c043-->
- Complete polypectomy cures an adenoma whose dysplasia has not gone beyond the mucosa; if left in place, a high-grade lesion may turn into invasive adenocarcinoma.<!--c:dysplasia-c044-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[metaplasia]] | One mature cell type is replaced by another adult cell type as an adaptation, without the nuclear atypia and disordered maturation of dysplasia; dysplasia may, however, arise inside metaplastic epithelium.<!--c:dysplasia-c045--> | Cytologic atypia and maturation on histology |
| [[hyperplasia]] | An increase in cell number driven by a stimulus, without the atypical nuclei and loss of orderly maturation that define dysplasia.<!--c:dysplasia-c046--> | Nuclear features and maturation pattern |
| [[carcinoma-in-situ]] | Neoplastic cells occupy the whole thickness of the epithelium, with no surface maturation, yet stay above the basement membrane; in the cervix, severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ are grouped together as CIN 3.<!--c:dysplasia-c047--> | Full-thickness atypia without surface maturation, basement membrane intact |
| Invasive carcinoma | Cells breach the basement membrane, invade surrounding tissue and can metastasize through blood and lymphatic vessels.<!--c:dysplasia-c048--> | Stromal invasion on biopsy |
| Inflammatory (reactive) atypia | Intense inflammation can produce changes that cannot be confidently separated from dysplasia.<!--c:dysplasia-c049--> | Rebiopsy once inflammation is controlled ("indefinite for dysplasia") |

## High-yield summary

- Dysplasia = **disordered growth and maturation** of an epithelium; it is **precancerous**.<!--c:dysplasia-c050-->
- Cytologic features: **pleomorphism**, **enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei**, **high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio**.<!--c:dysplasia-c051-->
- Architectural features: **loss of polarity and maturation**, **mitoses above the basal layer**.<!--c:dysplasia-c052-->
- Mechanism: **sequential mutations** in proliferating cells with **clonal selection**.<!--c:dysplasia-c053-->
- Grading: **low vs high grade**; cervix **LSIL = CIN 1**, **HSIL = CIN 2–3**.<!--c:dysplasia-c054-->
- Dysplasia is **not autonomous** and can **regress** when the stimulus stops.<!--c:dysplasia-c055-->
- **Carcinoma in situ** lies above an intact **basement membrane**; invasion begins when it is breached.<!--c:dysplasia-c056-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- **Dysplasia is not cancer.** Its cells are not fully autonomous, and the tissue can revert to normal.<!--c:dysplasia-c057-->
- Dysplasia does not progress inevitably: cervical LSIL often disappears on its own.<!--c:dysplasia-c058-->
- Dysplasia is not limited to squamous epithelium; it also arises in glandular mucosa such as the colon and in metaplastic epithelium such as Barrett esophagus.<!--c:dysplasia-c059-->
- "Renal dysplasia" or other developmental dysplasias are malformations of tissue formation, not precancerous epithelial lesions.<!--c:dysplasia-c060-->
- CIN 3 includes both severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ; the two are hard to tell apart.<!--c:dysplasia-c061-->
- Tumors arising from cells that do not sit on a basement membrane, such as connective tissue cells, lymphoid cells and hepatocytes, have no in situ stage.<!--c:dysplasia-c062-->

## Mnemonics

**"Lost LAMP"** (original), the core features of dysplasia, pictured as an epithelium whose lamp has gone out so the cells can no longer find their order: **L**ayering and maturation lost, **A**typical nuclei (large, dark, irregular), **M**itoses up high, **P**leomorphism.<!--c:dysplasia-c063-->

**"Low lets go, high holds on"** (original): low-grade lesions such as cervical LSIL tend to regress, while high-grade lesions tend to persist and progress.<!--c:dysplasia-c064-->
