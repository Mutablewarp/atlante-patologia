## Definition and epidemiology

**What is ischemic heart disease?** The myocardium receives less oxygen than it needs, usually because atherosclerosis has narrowed the coronary arteries.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c001--> Patients present with sudden death, myocardial infarction, angina pectoris or chronic heart failure.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c002--> An acute coronary syndrome is a sudden fall in flow through an epicardial coronary artery, and it is what causes infarction and sudden death.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Why does the myocardium run short of oxygen?** Ischemia follows whenever supply and demand part ways, because, unlike skeletal muscle, the heart cannot rely on anaerobic glycolysis.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c005-->

**What reduces coronary flow?**
- Coronary atherosclerosis, often with a thrombus on top, is the commonest cause.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c006-->
- Coronary spasm can also cut flow.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c007-->
- An embolus rarely causes infarction; when one does, it usually comes from valve vegetations or a clot inside the heart.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c008-->
- A coronary artery with an abnormal origin can cause sudden death in a young person; so can a left anterior descending artery that runs inside the myocardium.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c010-->
- Anemia and carbon monoxide lower the oxygen carried in the blood.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c011-->
- Fever, hyperthyroidism, hypertension and valve disease make the heart work harder and need more oxygen.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c012-->

**Which risk factors matter most?** High blood cholesterol, diabetes, smoking and hypertension are the four major ones.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c013-->

## Pathogenesis

**Why is coronary atherosclerosis often silent?** Resting flow stays normal despite a stenosis in a large epicardial artery, because the small intramural vessels dilate.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c017--> Exertion raises oxygen need, and once the small vessels have dilated as far as they can, supply falls short and ischemia with angina follows.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c018-->

**What turns silent disease into an infarct?** Infarction usually begins when a vulnerable plaque tears or wears away and a thrombus forms on it.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c019-->

**Can collateral vessels change the outcome?** Collaterals grown in severe atherosclerosis can spare myocardium after a sudden main-artery occlusion, preventing the infarct or making it smaller.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c021-->

**Why can restoring flow also hurt?** Contraction bands form when calcium rushes into myocytes whose membrane was damaged by reactive oxygen species generated on reperfusion.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c023-->

## Gross morphology

**What does an infarct look like to the naked eye?** Nothing shows for the first hours; pallor appears at about one day.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c024--> At a few days, hyperemic tissue rings a yellow necrotic core and the whole infarct looks mottled.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c025--> Healing leaves a light gray scar that shrinks and hardens.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c027-->

**Which artery supplies which wall?** An inferior infarct, involving the posterior septum, points to right coronary occlusion.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c028--> Apical, anterior and anteroseptal infarcts are the territory of the left anterior descending artery.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c029-->

**Transmural or subendocardial?** Type 1 infarcts, usually thrombotic, are transmural: one artery's territory, one compact block of necrosis.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c031--> Global hypoperfusion (type 2) produces subendocardial infarcts; they are patchy and can wrap around the ventricle.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c032--> Reperfusion lets blood leak from injured microvessels, making the infarct hemorrhagic.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c033-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the myocardium show over time?** The first cellular signs of ischemic injury can be seen only by electron microscopy.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c034--> Within a day the necrotic myocytes show the hallmarks of coagulative necrosis and stain intensely with eosin.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c035--> Two to three days after infarction, neutrophil numbers peak at the infarct border.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c036--> By day five to seven, macrophages clear the dead muscle and collagen starts forming.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c037--> Reperfused infarcts typically show contraction band necrosis: stripes of hypercontracted sarcomeres cutting across the dead myocytes.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c039-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** on H&E, dead pink fibers lose nuclei and striations while inflammatory cells crowd around.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c040-->
- Of the blood markers of myocardial necrosis, cardiac troponin (I or T) is the one tested most often.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c043-->

## Clinical presentation

**How do the angina types differ?**
- Rest or nitroglycerin under the tongue relieves stable angina, a recurrent chest pain that exertion or stress triggers.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c044-->
- Prinzmetal (variant) angina is the form that strikes at rest; coronary spasm underlies it.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c045-->
- Unstable angina pain tracks exertion less reliably, and a plaque often carries a thrombus that doesn't fully block the artery.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c046-->
- Stable angina is not an acute coronary syndrome.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c047-->

**What does an infarct feel like?** Infarction typically starts suddenly with severe retrosternal pain; patients are often sweaty, nauseated and short of breath.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c048-->

**What is chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy?** It is ventricular failure with global loss of contractility but no discrete infarct, seen in some patients with severe coronary disease.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c050-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is an acute coronary syndrome sorted?** The ECG shows ST elevation in STEMI and ST depression in non-STEMI.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c052--> Calling an episode an infarction requires troponin that rises or falls over the same period as the symptoms.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c053--> In unstable angina troponin stays normal.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c054--> At pathology, transmural and subendocardial infarcts correspond fairly well to STEMI and non-STEMI on the ECG.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c055-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What can go wrong after an infarct?**
- After infarction, rhythm disturbances are near-universal and cause many deaths.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c056-->
- If the left ventricle fails early after infarction, much of its muscle has probably died; the outlook is poor and shock may follow.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c057-->
- Only transmural infarcts can tear through the free wall, mostly in the first week while the dead muscle is soft, and tamponade is the usual result.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c058-->
- Mitral regurgitation results when a papillary muscle ruptures, and a ruptured septum lets blood shunt from left to right.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c059-->
- Current can circle back at an aneurysm's edge, which puts the patient at risk of ventricular tachycardia.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c060-->

**Why does speed matter?** Permanent salvage of ischemic myocytes depends on restoring arterial flow early; no other intervention achieves it.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c064-->

**How is sudden death linked?** Ventricular tachycardia that deteriorates into fibrillation can kill a patient whose acute coronary syndrome has given no earlier sign.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c065--> The fatal rhythm is probably triggered by ischemia alone: many people saved by defibrillation had no infarct on markers or ECG.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c066-->

**What happens in chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy?** Myocytes that have lost myofibrils after repeated bouts of ischemic injury form hibernating myocardium.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c068--> Revascularization brings its function back.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c069-->

## High-yield summary

- **Ischemic heart disease** is a supply-demand mismatch, mostly from **coronary atherosclerosis**.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c075-->
- **Unstable angina** has normal troponin; **infarction** does not.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c076-->
- **Thrombosis** on a ruptured plaque usually precipitates infarction.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c077-->
- **Pallor** is the first gross sign of infarct, at about one day.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c078-->
- **Neutrophils** peak at two to three days.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c079-->
- **Reperfusion** brings hemorrhage and **contraction bands**.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c080-->
- **Sudden death** can occur without infarct, through **ventricular fibrillation**.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c081-->
- **Hibernating myocardium** recovers after revascularization.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c082-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is stable angina an acute coronary syndrome?** No. Myocardial damage appears only if the episodes are prolonged or severe enough to produce necrosis.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c083-->

**Does a lethal arrhythmia need an infarct?** No. Often ischemia alone is enough to set it off.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c085-->

**Is Prinzmetal angina caused by a thrombus?** No. Spasm causes it, often near a plaque.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c086-->

## Mnemonics

**SUV drives angina:** Stable is Stress-related, Unstable is Unpredictable, Variant is Vasospasm at rest.<!--c:ischemic-heart-disease-c087-->
