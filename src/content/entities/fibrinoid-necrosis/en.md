## Definition and epidemiology

Fibrinoid necrosis is a lesion of damaged blood vessels in which plasma proteins leak into the wall and pile up there, so the wall takes on an intense pink color with eosin.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c001--> Fibrin is a major component of the deposit, as the name suggests.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c002--> The word "necrosis" is not quite accurate: the bright protein deposit masks the wall, so it is often impossible to tell whether its cells have really died.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c003--> It is the classic tissue sign of vasculitis caused by immune complexes.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c004--> Malignant hypertension produces the same change in arterioles and small arteries.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c072-->

## Etiology and risk factors

- **Immune complex disease (type III hypersensitivity):** serum sickness, the experimental Arthus reaction, systemic lupus erythematosus, cryoglobulinemic vasculitis with hepatitis C and Henoch–Schönlein purpura with IgA deposits.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c005-->
- **Polyarteritis nodosa:** patchy necrotizing arteritis of small and medium-sized muscular arteries; some cases are linked to hepatitis B antigen–antibody complexes.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c006-->
- **ANCA-associated vasculitis:** segmental fibrinoid necrosis of glomerular tufts and of interlobular arteries in the kidney.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c007-->
- **Hypersensitivity (leukocytoclastic) vasculitis:** a reaction to drugs such as aspirin, penicillin or thiazides, or to infections, that affects superficial skin venules.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c008-->
- **Malignant hypertension**, and other thrombotic microangiopathies such as hemolytic–uremic syndrome, which share the same arteriolar lesion.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c009-->
- **Antibody-mediated transplant rejection:** both the hyperacute form and acute humoral rejection.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c010-->
- **Rheumatic fever and systemic sclerosis:** the Aschoff body of rheumatic myocarditis has a fibrinoid center, and even before it forms, in the earliest phase, the myocardial collagen undergoes fibrinoid degeneration.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c083--> In systemic sclerosis fibrinoid necrosis is common in the afferent arterioles of the kidney.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c084-->
- **Other settings:** maternal decidual spiral arteries in preeclampsia, as part of acute atherosis.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c011--> The core of the rheumatoid nodule is also fibrinoid.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c012-->

## Pathogenesis

Two quite different roads lead to the same picture: an endothelium that no longer holds back plasma, and plasma proteins, fibrin included, soaking into the injured wall.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c013-->

**Immune route.** Antigen–antibody complexes form in the circulation or in place and lodge in small vessel walls, where they fix complement.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c014--> C3a and C5a make vessels leakier, and C5a also draws in neutrophils.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c015--> Wall damage comes from neutrophil products (proteases, oxygen radicals, eicosanoids), released once the cells meet or swallow the complexes.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c016--> The experimental Arthus reaction reproduces this route: an antigen injected into the skin of a previously immunized subject meets circulating antibody in the walls of small dermal vessels, and within 2 to 10 hours fibrin collects there, giving the fibrinoid look, while neutrophils crowd the injured walls.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c017--> In ANCA vasculitis, primed neutrophils display myeloperoxidase or proteinase 3 on their surface; ANCA binding activates them, and their granule products injure the endothelium.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c018-->

**Hemodynamic route.** In malignant hypertension, very high pressure in narrowed small vessels is thought to injure the endothelium.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c019--> The end result is a wall soaked in plasma and fibrin: medial myocytes die, the weakened segment balloons, and the broken endothelial lining lets proteins through.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c020-->

## Gross morphology

- The lesion itself is microscopic: in polyarteritis nodosa the necrotic segments are under 1 mm long and scattered.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c021-->
- On section the kidney in malignant hypertension shows a blotchy red-yellow pattern; a few tiny cortical infarcts may be present.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c022-->

## Microscopic morphology

- **The deposit:** an intensely pink band without any visible structure, which hides the normal layers of the wall; in a necrotic arterial segment you can no longer tell where the media ends and the tissue around it begins.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c023-->
- **Inflammation around it (vasculitis):** neutrophils, lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages, sometimes many eosinophils, often involving the whole adventitia.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c024-->
- **Leukocytoclasis:** in cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis the venule wall shows fibrinoid necrosis with acute inflammation and nuclear dust from broken-down neutrophils.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c025-->
- **Malignant hypertension:** arteriolar fibrinoid necrosis is soon followed by concentric layers of proliferating smooth muscle (**onion-skin** lesion).<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c026--> Arteries of the kidney show edematous, myxoid widening of the intima.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c027-->
- **Glomeruli:** segmental fibrinoid necrosis of the tuft in ANCA glomerulonephritis, later followed by crescents.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c028-->
- **Rheumatoid nodule:** a central core of fibrinoid material (fibrin plus degraded collagen) rimmed by palisading macrophages.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c029-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**How it is studied:**

- **Specimen:** the lesion is found in biopsies of the organ involved, such as skin with palpable purpura, the native or transplanted kidney or, for a suspected neuropathy, the sural nerve together with the gastrocnemius muscle; the placenta of a pregnancy with preeclampsia can show it as well.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c074--> Axons degenerate when the nerve loses its blood supply: in polyarteritis nodosa with mononeuropathy multiplex, the cause to look for in the biopsy is fibrinoid necrosis of the epineurial arteries.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c075-->
- **H&E:** in a vessel, check that the pink deposit lies in the wall and not in the lumen, then look at what surrounds it: neutrophils with nuclear dust point to a vasculitis, concentric layers of smooth muscle to malignant hypertension; outside vessels, palisading macrophages around a fibrinoid core point to a rheumatoid nodule.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c076-->
- **Special stains:** the fused, eosinophilic mass of a polyarteritis lesion stains positively for fibrin.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c030--> A fibrin stain such as PTAH (phosphotungstic acid–hematoxylin) turns fibrin deep purple.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c077--> With Masson trichrome, fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles appears red.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c031--> An elastic stain outlines the internal and external elastic laminae and shows the stretches that vasculitis has destroyed, as in granulomatosis with polyangiitis.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c078-->
- **Immunofluorescence and IHC:** direct immunofluorescence on a biopsy lights up fibrin and other plasma proteins trapped in the damaged wall.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c032--> In the kidney the pattern points to the mechanism: granular deposits of immune complexes (mostly IgA in Henoch–Schönlein purpura), linear IgG along the glomerular basement membrane in anti-GBM disease, and hardly any immunoglobulin or complement in ANCA disease, hence the name pauci-immune.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c079--> In antibody-mediated graft rejection, C4d deposited along peritubular capillaries marks complement activation.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c033--> It can be shown by immunofluorescence or by immunohistochemistry.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c080-->
- **Electron microscopy:** the same leak can be seen inside the glomerulus, where plasma proteins that have seeped under the injured endothelium form a pale (electron-lucent) band that narrows the capillary lumen, in malignant hypertension as in the other thrombotic microangiopathies.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c081-->
- **Serology (ANCA):** P-ANCA is mostly directed against myeloperoxidase, C-ANCA mainly against proteinase 3.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c034-->
- **Pattern, not cause:** malignant hypertension and hemolytic–uremic syndrome produce the same renal lesions, so the biopsy names the pattern (thrombotic microangiopathy) and the cause comes from the clinical picture, for example bloody diarrhea beforehand or a drug such as cyclosporine.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c082-->

## Clinical presentation

Symptoms depend on the underlying disease and on which vessels are hit.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c035-->

- **Cutaneous vasculitis:** palpable purpura, mostly on the legs.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c036-->
- **Polyarteritis nodosa:** fever and weight loss, with lesions most often in kidneys, heart, skeletal muscle, skin and mesentery.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c037-->
- **Malignant hypertension:** headache, dizziness, visual disturbance, sometimes encephalopathy, with hematuria and proteinuria.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c038--> In the brain, fibrinoid necrosis of small arteries and arterioles comes with petechial hemorrhages and edema (hypertensive encephalopathy).<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c039-->
- **Serum sickness:** fever, joint pain, vasculitis and acute glomerulonephritis about 6 to 8 days after a foreign protein is given.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c040-->
- **Hyperacute rejection:** urine output stops suddenly and the graft darkens, sometimes while the patient is still in the operating room.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c041-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

- **Biopsy** is often needed in a kidney graft to separate rejection from acute kidney injury or immunosuppressive drug toxicity.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c042-->
- **Malignant hypertension:** severe elevation (commonly ≥180/≥120 mm Hg) with retinal hemorrhages or papilledema and organ damage.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c043-->
- **Thrombotic microangiopathy:** Coombs-negative hemolytic anemia with schistocytes, plus thrombocytopenia.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c044-->
- **ANCA serology:** titers follow disease activity in some patients; microscopic polyangiitis is strongly linked to P-ANCA.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c045-->

<aside class="sources-differ">Sources differ: instead of a ≥180/≥120 mm Hg threshold, the kidney chapter of the same reference uses a diastolic pressure above 130 mm Hg, together with eye and kidney damage, as a usual criterion, and adds that no pressure value alone defines the condition.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c073--></aside>

## Complications and prognosis

- **Thrombosis** of injured segments causes **infarcts** in the organs supplied.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c046-->
- **Aneurysms:** fatal bleeding can follow rupture of the small (under 0.5 cm) aneurysms that polyarteritis nodosa leaves in organ arteries, for example in the kidney or the heart.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c047-->
- **Healing:** scar replaces the media once the debris is cleared, and the elastic layers stay broken.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c048-->
- **Malignant hypertension:** the vascular lesions are called malignant arteriosclerosis or arteriolosclerosis, and malignant nephrosclerosis in the kidney.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c049--> Lowering the pressure firmly usually brings it under control; left in place, it steadily erodes kidney function.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c050-->
- **Polyarteritis nodosa:** steroids plus cyclophosphamide put most patients into remission or cure them, whereas untreated disease is mostly lethal.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c051-->
- **Necrotizing transplant arteritis:** fewer than 30% of affected grafts survive one year.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c052-->
- **Cutaneous vasculitis** usually resolves on its own.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c053-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Hyaline arteriolosclerosis | Seen in benign hypertension: the arteriole's smooth muscle cells dwindle or are pushed to the outer edge, while glassy pink hyaline material builds up in the wall into a thick concentric ring.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c055--> | Tempo of the hypertension; necrotic wall versus thick glassy ring |

## High-yield summary

- **Plasma proteins and fibrin** leak into a damaged vessel wall, which turns **intensely eosinophilic**.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c060-->
- Two main causes: **immune complex vasculitis** (type III) and **malignant hypertension**.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c061-->
- Immune route: complexes fix **complement**; **C5a** recruits **neutrophils**, which damage the wall.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c062-->
- Malignant hypertension: fibrinoid necrosis of **arterioles**, then **onion-skin** smooth muscle proliferation.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c063-->
- **Polyarteritis nodosa**: patchy segments of fibrinoid necrosis with **thrombosis**, **infarcts** and small **aneurysms**.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c064-->
- **Antibody-mediated rejection** can cause necrotizing arteritis with fibrinoid necrosis, a poor sign for the graft.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c065-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- The name promises necrosis, but the deposit usually hides the wall, so true cell death is often not demonstrable.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c066-->
- It is not specific to immune disease: hypertension alone can produce it.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c067-->
- Fibrinoid necrosis lies in the wall; a fibrin thrombus fills the lumen, and the two are listed as separate lesions.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c068-->
- Benign hypertension gives hyaline arteriolosclerosis; fibrinoid necrosis belongs to the malignant phase.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c069-->
- Immune complexes are found only in some cases of human vasculitis, so their absence does not exclude vasculitis.<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c070-->

## Mnemonics

**FIBRIN** (original): **F**ibrin and other plasma proteins soak into the wall, **I**mmune complexes (type III, serum sickness, lupus), **B**lood pressure crisis (malignant hypertension, onion skin), **R**ejection mediated by antibodies, **I**ntense pink color that hides the wall, **N**ecrotizing vasculitis (polyarteritis nodosa, ANCA).<!--c:fibrinoid-necrosis-c071-->
