## Definition and epidemiology

**What is prostatitis?** It is inflammation of the prostate, and it occurs in acute and chronic forms.<!--c:prostatitis-c001--> In many patients no cause is found; when one is, it is usually a coliform bacterium from the urinary tract.<!--c:prostatitis-c002--> Four patterns are worth knowing: acute bacterial, chronic bacterial, chronic nonbacterial and granulomatous prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c003--> Of all forms of prostatitis, chronic nonbacterial prostatitis is the one met most often in pathology material.<!--c:prostatitis-c004--> Men over 50 are typically affected, yet no age group is spared.<!--c:prostatitis-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What triggers prostatitis?** Acute prostatitis usually arises on top of an infection elsewhere in the urinary tract.<!--c:prostatitis-c006--> Reflux of urine into the prostate can contribute to chronic bacterial prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c007--> A blocked duct inside the gland, or stones in it, can also help the chronic bacterial form develop.<!--c:prostatitis-c008--> *Ureaplasma urealyticum*, *Mycoplasma* and *Chlamydia trachomatis* may explain some nonbacterial cases.<!--c:prostatitis-c009--> Most granulomatous cases have no demonstrable cause.<!--c:prostatitis-c010--> Only occasionally is a specific agent identified, such as the fungus *Histoplasma capsulatum*, BCG or *M. tuberculosis*.<!--c:prostatitis-c011--> Granulomas resembling rheumatoid nodules have followed transurethral resection of the prostate.<!--c:prostatitis-c012-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does the gland become inflamed?** In acute prostatitis, urine carrying bacteria backs up into the gland.<!--c:prostatitis-c013--> A chronic bacterial case can begin with an acute episode, but it does not have to.<!--c:prostatitis-c014--> In granulomatous prostatitis, the granulomas sit where prostatic ducts and acini have been destroyed, and scarring appears later.<!--c:prostatitis-c015-->

## Gross morphology

Not applicable.

## Microscopic morphology

**What does each form look like under the microscope?**
- **Acute:** acute inflammatory cells infiltrate both the acini and the stroma.<!--c:prostatitis-c016-->
- **Chronic bacterial:** macrophages, plasma cells and lymphocytes typically infiltrate the gland.<!--c:prostatitis-c017-->
- **Chronic nonbacterial:** the gland lumens are widened and packed with neutrophils and foamy macrophages, while chronic inflammatory cells sit around them.<!--c:prostatitis-c018-->
- **Granulomatous:** the granulomas may be caseating or noncaseating.<!--c:prostatitis-c019-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** biopsies taken to look for cancer often end up showing chronic prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c020-->
- **How it is studied:** in granulomatous prostatitis the diagnosis rests on histology.<!--c:prostatitis-c021-->

This packet supports no special stain, immunohistochemistry or molecular test for prostatitis.

## Clinical presentation

**What does the patient complain of?** Patients with acute prostatitis feel intense discomfort when they urinate.<!--c:prostatitis-c022--> Perineal pain, fever and chills often come with it.<!--c:prostatitis-c023--> A burning sensation at the urethral opening and dysuria are the usual complaints in chronic bacterial prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c024--> Waking at night to urinate may also occur, as may pain in the low back, the perineum or above the pubis.<!--c:prostatitis-c025--> Some patients with nonbacterial prostatitis feel nothing, while others have complaints resembling chronic bacterial prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c026--> Granulomatous prostatitis gives vague symptoms.<!--c:prostatitis-c027--> Symptoms of chronic prostatitis vary widely.<!--c:prostatitis-c028--> Chronic prostatitis can raise serum PSA.<!--c:prostatitis-c029-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is prostatitis recognized?** Bacteria are usually found in the urine of patients with chronic bacterial prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c030--> Doctors diagnose nonbacterial prostatitis in practice only by excluding other conditions.<!--c:prostatitis-c031--> A high PSA raises concern for prostatic malignancy.<!--c:prostatitis-c032-->

## Complications and prognosis

**Does it respond to treatment?** Antibiotics work well in most patients with acute prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c033--> A long course of antibiotics cures chronic bacterial prostatitis in many patients, but not in all.<!--c:prostatitis-c034--> Nonbacterial prostatitis usually has no specific treatment.<!--c:prostatitis-c035-->

## High-yield summary

- Acute prostatitis starts when urine carrying bacteria **backs up** into the gland.<!--c:prostatitis-c039-->
- Chronic **nonbacterial** prostatitis is a diagnosis of exclusion.<!--c:prostatitis-c040-->
- Chronic bacterial prostatitis shows **macrophages, plasma cells and lymphocytes**.<!--c:prostatitis-c041-->
- **Fungi**, **BCG** and *M. tuberculosis* are rare identifiable causes of granulomatous prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c042-->
- Chronic prostatitis can raise serum **PSA**.<!--c:prostatitis-c043-->
- **Antibiotics** work well in most patients with acute prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c044-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a raised PSA prove prostate cancer?** No. Chronic prostatitis can also raise PSA, so biopsies for suspected cancer often turn out to show prostatitis.<!--c:prostatitis-c045-->

**Is prostatitis always a bacterial infection?** No. Some chronic cases yield no organism, and nonbacterial prostatitis is a diagnosis of exclusion.<!--c:prostatitis-c046-->

**Is granulomatous prostatitis always tuberculosis?** No. For most patients no cause is ever demonstrated.<!--c:prostatitis-c047-->

**Does every prostatitis cause symptoms?** No. Nonbacterial prostatitis may be asymptomatic.<!--c:prostatitis-c048-->
