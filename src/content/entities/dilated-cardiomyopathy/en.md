## Definition and epidemiology

**What is dilated cardiomyopathy?** Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a primary disease of the heart muscle in which both ventricles dilate and contract poorly, ending in heart failure.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c001--> No other cardiomyopathy is seen more often, and DCM ranks among the main reasons for heart transplantation.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c002--> Primary DCM mostly stems from inherited myocyte defects; secondary DCM follows a known injury to myocytes.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes it?** The causes are partly inherited and partly acquired, so DCM counts as a "mixed" cardiomyopathy.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c004--> Single-gene inheritance accounts for at least a third of cases.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c005--> Familial DCM is usually dominant; mitochondrial, X-linked and recessive inheritance are also reported.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c006--> DCM mutations occur in sarcomeric and cytoskeletal protein genes, for example lamin A/C and desmin.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c007--> Truncating variants of titin (TTN) are a major single genetic cause.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c008--> The same gene, different diseases: DES variants can produce RCM, ACM or DCM.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c009-->

Secondary forms:

- Many toxic or metabolic insults to myocytes end in secondary DCM.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c010-->
- Among identifiable causes, alcohol ranks first in the United States and Europe.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c011-->
- DCM can follow anthracyclines such as doxorubicin, whose cardiac toxicity grows with the cumulative dose.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c012-->
- DCM occurs more often with diabetes mellitus or smoking.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c013-->
- Some patients with viral myocarditis go on, over time, to develop DCM.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c014-->
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy is a form of DCM that appears in late pregnancy or after delivery.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c015-->
- Women with preeclampsia or hypertension are strongly predisposed to it.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c016-->
- In the minority of PPCM women with a gene defect, DMD or TTN is usually involved.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c017-->

## Pathogenesis

**How do these causes injure the myocardium?** In most patients DCM probably results from genes and environment acting together, including changes in gene regulation.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c018--> A relative can carry the family's mutant allele and stay healthy, because penetrance is often incomplete.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c019--> When cytoskeletal or anchoring proteins lose function through mutation, force may not be passed on properly.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c020--> Variants in one gene can cause opposite diseases: loss of function weakens contraction (DCM), gain of function tends to give HCM.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c021-->

- Immune activity can continue after viral myocarditis if viral nucleic acid lingers in the heart.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c022-->
- T cells that turn against the heart's own proteins can sustain inflammation after the virus is gone, and DCM may follow.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c023-->
- In cardiac myocytes, alcohol harms mitochondria and increases reactive oxygen species.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c024-->
- Years of heavy drinking increase myocyte apoptosis.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c025-->
- Anthracyclines disturb topoisomerase II as an unintended effect, breaking DNA strands and hurting mitochondria.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c026-->
- In PPCM, excess sFlt1 binds blood VEGF; a heart that cannot offset this with its own VEGF may develop DCM.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c027-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the heart look like?** Both ventricles are conspicuously hypertrophied, and the heart is always enlarged.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c028--> Dilation usually involves all chambers, but is worse in the ventricles than in the atria.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c029--> In end-stage DCM a thin-looking left ventricular wall can hide hypertrophy, because the chamber is stretched out.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c030--> Small scars just under the endocardium may be seen in this pale, limp muscle.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c031--> Mural thrombi often attach at the left ventricular apex, where the thickened endocardium is thickest.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c032-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does it show under the microscope?** Histology does not point to a specific cause and looks alike across etiologies.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c033--> Atrophic and hypertrophic fibers are mixed.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c034--> Loss of myofibrils (myocytolysis) hollows out myocytes, mostly under the endocardium, and leaves them vacuolated.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c035--> Fibrosis is heaviest just beneath the endocardium, spreading between fibers and around vessels.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c036--> Chronic inflammatory cells are not conspicuous; a few may be scattered about.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c037-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**

**How it is studied:**

- Light microscopy cannot reveal the cause; it shows subendocardial fibrosis, vacuolated myocytes and mixed atrophic and hypertrophic fibers.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c038-->
- On electron microscopy, mitochondria look more abundant and sarcomeres scarcer.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c039-->
- Special features can hint at the cause: abnormally folded intermediate filaments may clump inside myocytes when the desmin gene is mutated.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c040-->
- Variants in many genes, including TTN, LMNA, DES, SCN5A and PLN, are linked to DCM.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c041-->

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** The clinical course is much the same whether DCM is primary or secondary.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c042--> At first there are no symptoms: the left ventricle dilates silently while the ventricular wall thickens to compensate.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c043--> Patients usually tolerate exercise less and less until overt heart failure develops.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c044--> In heart failure, altered Ca2+ handling and K+ currents favor triggered arrhythmias and a longer QT interval.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c045--> Patients with strongly arrhythmogenic DCM often carry variants in desmosome or calcium-handling genes.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c046-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it diagnosed?** Clinically, a patient has heart failure with weak contraction and dilated ventricles on both sides.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c047--> Alcoholic cardiomyopathy resembles ordinary DCM, so the diagnosis rests on documented heavy drinking and exclusion of other causes.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c048--> Cardiac biomarker assays and sensitive imaging help catch heart damage from cancer drugs early.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c049--> Relatives with incomplete penetrance can look well while disease is starting, so it is easy to overlook.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c050-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What is the outlook?** Death or transplantation affects nearly half of patients within 5 years of the first symptoms.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c051--> Sudden death, blamed on ventricular arrhythmias, accounts for about a quarter of deaths.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c052--> Even with modern treatment, many patients eventually need a transplant or ventricular assist device.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c053--> In PPCM the left ventricle stays dysfunctional in about half of women, while the other half recover spontaneously.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c054--> Persistently low ventricular function after PPCM makes a later pregnancy especially risky: relapse or maternal death.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c055-->

## High-yield summary

- **DCM** is the most frequent cardiomyopathy: heart failure from dilated ventricles that contract poorly.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c061-->
- **Titin (TTN) truncations** are a major genetic cause.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c062-->
- **Alcohol** is the leading identifiable cause.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c063-->
- **Doxorubicin** toxicity is dose dependent.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c064-->
- **Peripartum cardiomyopathy** is a special DCM variant arising late in pregnancy or postpartum.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c065-->
- Gross: **all chambers usually dilated**, with hypertrophy hidden by a thin-looking wall.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c066-->
- Micro: nonspecific, with **myocytolysis** and **subendocardial fibrosis**.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c067-->
- Course: **heart failure** and sudden death from **ventricular arrhythmia**.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c068-->
- In one gene, **loss-of-function** variants give DCM and **gain-of-function** variants give HCM.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c069-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a thin left ventricular wall mean there is no hypertrophy?** No. The heart is heavy, and the dilation masks the hypertrophy.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c070-->

**Is DCM always genetic?** No. Viruses, alcohol, drugs, autoimmunity and inherited mutations can all end in DCM.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c071-->

**Does cocaine commonly cause true DCM?** No. Cocaine abuse rarely produces genuine DCM.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c072-->

**Can the biopsy name the cause?** Usually not. The findings are nonspecific, apart from rare clues such as desmin aggregates.<!--c:dilated-cardiomyopathy-c073-->
