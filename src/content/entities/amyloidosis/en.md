## Definition and epidemiology

**What is amyloidosis?** Misfolded proteins form insoluble fibrils that pile up between cells, in tissues and organs.<!--c:amyloidosis-c001-->

To diagnose amyloidosis, the pathologist must name the misfolded protein, because H&E and Congo red cannot separate the types.<!--c:amyloidosis-c003-->
The fibril protein now defines each type; in AL it is immunoglobulin light chains.<!--c:amyloidosis-c004-->
A deposit that stays where its precursor is made is localized; one in organs distant from the producing cells is systemic.<!--c:amyloidosis-c005-->
AA is the only type seen in children.<!--c:amyloidosis-c006-->
Long-term dialysis is the setting of Aβ2M amyloidosis.<!--c:amyloidosis-c007-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which protein builds each type?**

- **AL:** usually a plasma cell dyscrasia, with or without myeloma; the fibrils are monoclonal immunoglobulin light chains.<!--c:amyloidosis-c008-->
- **AA:** serum amyloid A, an acute-phase protein made in the liver.<!--c:amyloidosis-c009-->
- AA is commonest in rheumatic disease or lasting infection such as tuberculosis, usually after years of inflammation.<!--c:amyloidosis-c010-->
- **ATTR:** transthyretin; inherited variants are autosomal dominant.<!--c:amyloidosis-c011-->
- The heart bears most of the damage when normal, unmutated transthyretin aggregates in older patients.<!--c:amyloidosis-c012-->
- **Aβ2M:** β2-microglobulin, in long-term hemodialysis.<!--c:amyloidosis-c013-->
- **Localized forms:** calcitonin in medullary thyroid carcinoma, islet amyloid polypeptide in the diabetic pancreas, amyloid β protein in Alzheimer disease.<!--c:amyloidosis-c014-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does a soluble protein turn into amyloid?** The exact route of fibril formation is unknown and may differ between types; one proposal is that a precursor that has begun to unfold gives small oligomers, which then assemble into bigger polymers.<!--c:amyloidosis-c016-->

## Gross morphology

**What does amyloid look like to the naked eye?** Thickened walls make the amyloid heart larger without widening its chambers, and the muscle feels rubbery and stiff.<!--c:amyloidosis-c023-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does amyloid look like on a slide?** Under the light microscope amyloid is an amorphous eosinophilic material.<!--c:amyloidosis-c025-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**

**How it is studied:**

- **Specimen:** amyloid must be seen in a biopsy to make the diagnosis; the least invasive sample is a fat aspirate from the abdomen.<!--c:amyloidosis-c028-->
- **Congo red:** under polarized light the stained amyloid is apple-green and birefringent.<!--c:amyloidosis-c030-->
- **Immunohistochemistry:** it is required to tell the types apart.<!--c:amyloidosis-c031-->
- **Mass spectrometry:** in heart biopsies AL and ATTR look alike on slides, and this method tells them apart.<!--c:amyloidosis-c033-->
- **Molecular tests:** TTR gene sequencing finds the exact mutation after an abnormal isoelectric focusing, which usually detects variant transthyretin.<!--c:amyloidosis-c034-->

## Clinical presentation

**Which organs are hit, and how?**

- Almost any tissue except the brain can be involved in **AL**, but the kidneys and heart are hit most often.<!--c:amyloidosis-c035-->
- Kidney: renal function worsens over time, and the usual picture is nephrotic syndrome.<!--c:amyloidosis-c036-->
- Heart: restrictive cardiomyopathy stiffens the ventricles, and heart failure progresses fast.<!--c:amyloidosis-c037-->
- A hallmark of AL, macroglossia is nonetheless present in only a minority of patients.<!--c:amyloidosis-c039-->
- **AA** rarely causes cardiomyopathy; its main presentation is proteinuria or renal insufficiency.<!--c:amyloidosis-c040-->
- **Familial ATTR** can hardly be separated from AL by clinical features alone.<!--c:amyloidosis-c041-->
- The first symptom of **Aβ2M** amyloidosis is usually carpal tunnel syndrome, within a picture of bone and joint disease.<!--c:amyloidosis-c044-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which tests point to the type?** Serum or urine tests detect a clonal immunoglobulin or free light chains in most AL patients.<!--c:amyloidosis-c045-->

MGUS is common in older people, so finding a monoclonal protein in serum cannot by itself prove amyloidosis.<!--c:amyloidosis-c046-->
Familial amyloidosis has to be excluded in a patient who has neither a plasma cell dyscrasia nor AA disease.<!--c:amyloidosis-c048-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What is the outlook?** Heart involvement with impaired function cuts untreated AL median survival to about 6 months, from about 1 to 2 years overall.<!--c:amyloidosis-c050-->

In AL, treatment has mostly aimed at the plasma cells producing the light chain.<!--c:amyloidosis-c051-->
Liver transplantation is the main treatment for ATTR, and drugs that mimic thyroxine can stabilize the transthyretin tetramer.<!--c:amyloidosis-c052-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| Diabetic glomerulosclerosis | Its nodules mimic amyloid, which is PAS negative and Congo red positive.<!--c:amyloidosis-c054--> | PAS and Congo red |
| Monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition disease | Similar nodules that do not stain with Congo red.<!--c:amyloidosis-c055--> | Congo red; immunofluorescence |
| [[hypertensive-heart-disease\|Hypertensive heart disease]] | Hard to separate from cardiac amyloid without an endomyocardial biopsy.<!--c:amyloidosis-c056--> | Endomyocardial biopsy |

## High-yield summary

- **Congo red** with **apple-green birefringence** identifies amyloid but not its type.<!--c:amyloidosis-c058-->
- The type guides treatment, so it must be found by **immunohistochemistry** or **mass spectrometry**.<!--c:amyloidosis-c059-->
- **AL** is light chains, **AA** is serum amyloid A, **ATTR** is transthyretin, **Aβ2M** is dialysis.<!--c:amyloidosis-c060-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does a Congo red-positive deposit tell you the type?** No. Congo red and H&E cannot separate the types; only identifying the protein does.<!--c:amyloidosis-c063-->

**Does a monoclonal protein in the blood prove AL?** No. With biopsy-proven amyloid it points strongly to AL, but alone it proves nothing, since MGUS is common in older people.<!--c:amyloidosis-c064-->

## Mnemonics

**LATD:** "Lazy Amyloid Takes Dialysis" stands for Light chains (AL), Acute-phase SAA (AA), Transthyretin (ATTR), Dialysis β2-microglobulin (Aβ2M).<!--c:amyloidosis-c066-->
