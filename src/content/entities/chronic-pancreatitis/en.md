## Definition and epidemiology

**What is chronic pancreatitis?** Pancreatic tissue is steadily destroyed, and fibrosis fills the space it leaves.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c001-->
Some patients have pain that comes and goes or never stops; in others the only sign is that the gland no longer makes enough enzymes or insulin.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c002-->
Its treatment, course and mechanism all remain poorly understood.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes it?** Years of alcohol abuse lie behind nearly 80% of adult cases, the single biggest cause.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c004-->
The chronic form shares most of its causes with acute pancreatitis.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c005-->
Pancreas divisum or CFTR variants may predispose some drinkers, explaining why only a minority fall ill.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c006-->
Sometimes the trigger is a pancreatic duct that is blocked or deficient.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c007-->
Gallstones that suddenly block the duct cause acute attacks, yet chronic disease seems not to follow.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c008-->
Fibrosis and atrophy go with long-term acinar cell injury, hemochromatosis being one example.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c009-->
Patients with chronic renal failure develop acute and chronic pancreatitis more often.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c010-->
Most patients with groove (paraduodenal) pancreatitis are alcoholics; it lies between pancreatic head, bile duct and duodenum.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c011-->
Jaundice and duodenal obstruction are frequent results of its position.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c012-->
Severe abdominal pain recurring from childhood is common in hereditary pancreatitis, a rare dominant disease.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c013-->
Trypsinogen often activates itself, and point mutations leave the gland with more trypsin.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c014-->
PRSS1, the gene for cationic trypsinogen, carries one of three point mutations in most patients; SPINK1 changes are linked too.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c015-->
Cystic fibrosis can end in chronic pancreatitis: sticky secretions plug and dilate the ducts.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c016-->
Many "idiopathic" cases may really be cystic fibrosis or hereditary pancreatitis, since 10% to 30% carry CFTR variants.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c017-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does alcohol damage the pancreas?** The favored theory says small ducts get clogged with protein plugs, because alcohol drives enzyme secretion without more fluid.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c018-->
Early plugs may trigger no more than a mild acute pancreatitis.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c019-->
A vicious cycle follows: each new, more severe attack becomes likelier as the gland scars and the plugs enlarge into calcium carbonate stones.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c020-->
**Does every case start with acute attacks?** Roughly half of patients have no history of acute pancreatitis, although repeated acute bouts with scarring may be one route.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c021-->

**What is autoimmune pancreatitis?** Patients with autoimmune pancreatitis often also have inflammatory bowel disease, retroperitoneal fibrosis or another sclerosing autoimmune condition.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c022-->
More than 50 IgG4-positive plasma cells per high-power field, with raised serum IgG4, mark type 1.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c023-->
ANA, rheumatoid factor and other autoantibodies, with hypergammaglobulinemia, point to an autoimmune mechanism.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c024-->
Inflammatory bowel disease is more closely linked to type 2, which lacks IgG4-positive plasma cells.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c025-->
Steroid treatment may work in either type.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c026-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the gland look like?** Alcoholism lies behind more than 90% of the commonest type, chronic calcifying pancreatitis.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c027-->
The whole gland, only part of it, or scattered patches may be involved.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c028-->
On the cut surface the normal lobules are gone, and the tissue is firm.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c029-->
Strictures or stones block outflow, so the main duct and its branches often widen upstream.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c030-->
Pseudocysts and abscesses are common.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c031-->
By late cystic fibrosis, fat has taken the place of all the parenchyma.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c032-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What do you see under the microscope?** Acinar cells vanish from irregular fibrotic areas over much of the gland; endocrine tissue goes later.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c033-->
Eventually islets vanish; first they are buried in scar and can look merged and enlarged.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c034-->
Scars contain myofibroblasts and a variable infiltrate of macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c035-->
Alcohol-related disease more often shows calcified protein deposits inside ducts, large or small.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c036-->
Squamous metaplasia can occur in the duct epithelium, which may also be atrophic or hyperplastic.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c037-->
In autoimmune pancreatitis the ducts are ringed by many lymphocytes and plasma cells, and the tissue around them is scarred.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c038-->
Type 2 adds acute inflammation inside the epithelium.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c039-->
Obliterative venulitis occurs in both types.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c040-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** on routine H&E, expect dilated ducts holding calcified protein plugs, set in fibrosis with acinar loss.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c041-->
- In type 1 autoimmune disease the tissue holds more than 50 IgG4-positive plasma cells per high-power field.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c042-->
- Serum IgG4 is raised in type 1.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c043-->
- Molecular findings explain causes: PRSS1 and SPINK1 variants in hereditary disease, CFTR variants in some idiopathic cases.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c044-->
- The reference pages do not cover sampling, special stains or electron microscopy.

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** Repeated acute episodes came first in about half of patients.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c045-->
A third of patients never have an acute attack; their pain creeps in gradually and is either steady or comes and goes.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c046-->
Diabetes or malabsorption may be the first sign, because a few patients have no pain at the outset.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c047-->
Unremitting pain in the upper abdomen that spreads to the back can incapacitate patients, and many lose a lot of weight.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c048-->
Autoimmune disease may cause only abdominal discomfort in one patient and painless jaundice in another.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c049-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which findings point to the diagnosis?** Recognition usually comes late, when the disease is already advanced.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c050-->
Most patients already have diabetes, malabsorption or both by the time calcification shows on radiographs.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c051-->
Beaded, uneven pancreatic and bile ducts, or a tumor-like mass, may be seen on imaging in autoimmune pancreatitis.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c052-->
Raised IgG4 or autoantibodies favor an autoimmune type.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c053-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What are the complications and the outcome?** Local complications include pseudocysts, duct stones and ascites.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c054-->
Loss of gland function gives diabetes and fat malabsorption, with steatorrhea and vitamin K deficiency.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c055-->
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma later arises in roughly 40% of people with the hereditary form.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c056-->
About half of patients have died after 20 to 25 years; the annual death rate is 3% to 4%.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c057-->
Alcohol-related illness causes most deaths other than the roughly one fifth from complications of acute attacks.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c058-->

## High-yield summary

- **Alcohol** is the leading cause, behind nearly 80% of adult cases.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c063-->
- The hallmark is **fibrosis with acinar loss** and dilated ducts holding **calcified protein plugs**.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c064-->
- **Hereditary pancreatitis** is autosomal dominant, from **PRSS1** trypsinogen mutations.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c065-->
- **Type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis** has high **IgG4** and responds to steroids.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c066-->
- **Type 2** lacks IgG4 and is tied to inflammatory bowel disease.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c067-->
- Complications include **pseudocyst**, **diabetes** and **steatorrhea**.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c068-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does every case follow repeated acute pancreatitis?** No. About half of patients have no history of acute attacks.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c069-->

**Does a gallstone attack lead to chronic pancreatitis?** Not usually. Chronic disease does not seem to follow such an attack.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c070-->

**Is "idiopathic" chronic pancreatitis truly of unknown cause?** Not always. Some cases carry CFTR variants, and many may really be cystic fibrosis or hereditary disease.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c071-->

**Is a pancreatic mass always cancer?** No. Autoimmune and groove pancreatitis can mimic a tumor, and some patients are operated on for one.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c072-->

**Does every heavy drinker get chronic pancreatitis?** No. Only a minority do.<!--c:chronic-pancreatitis-c073-->
