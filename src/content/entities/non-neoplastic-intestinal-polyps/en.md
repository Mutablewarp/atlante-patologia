## Definition and epidemiology

**What is a non-neoplastic intestinal polyp?** Any lesion that juts into the bowel lumen is a polyp, whether it sits flat on the wall or hangs from a stalk.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c001--> Some polyp types can turn malignant, which is why they matter; on their own they seldom cause symptoms.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c002-->

In an inflammatory polyp, a small raised area forms where the epithelium is inflamed and healing.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c003--> A hamartomatous polyp is a tangle of the organ's usual epithelium and stroma, grown out of proportion.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes each type?**
- Colitis of any cause can lead to inflammatory polyps; ulcerative colitis and Crohn disease are the usual settings.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c005--> Some arise in an otherwise normal colon, perhaps after a healed infectious colitis.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c006-->
- A lymphoid polyp is a single focus of lymphoid tissue lying in the submucosa.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c007--> Children and people with common variable immunodeficiency account for most cases of nodular lymphoid hyperplasia.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c008-->
- Some hamartomatous polyps appear on their own, others as part of an inherited syndrome.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c009-->
- Peutz–Jeghers syndrome is autosomal dominant.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c010--> The tumor suppressor gene LKB1 is mutated, with loss of function, in most patients.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c011-->
- Juvenile polyps are sporadic or part of a polyposis syndrome.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c012--> Some families carry SMAD4 or BMPR1A mutations.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c013--> In 30% to 40% of patients no mutation is found.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c014-->
- Cowden and Bannayan–Ruvalcaba–Riley syndromes are inherited as autosomal dominant traits with germline PTEN mutations.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c015-->
- Cronkhite–Canada syndrome is not inherited.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c016-->

## Pathogenesis

**How do these polyps form?** Granulation tissue is often mixed into a raised patch where inflamed epithelium is regenerating.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c017--> AMPK and related kinases regulate growth and energy metabolism; LKB1 normally switches them on.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c018--> Cell proliferation is regulated by the TGFβ pathway, in which SMAD4 and BMPR1A take part.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c019-->

## Gross morphology

**What do they look like to the naked eye?**
- A lymphoid polyp can be as small as a pinpoint or as large as 5 cm.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c020--> When many are present, the mucosal surface may look cobblestoned.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c021--> The overlying mucosa is intact.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c022-->
- The small intestine is the commonest site of Peutz–Jeghers polyps, although they can occur anywhere along the gut.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c023--> They have a bosselated surface.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c024-->
- Juvenile polyps are mostly rectal and usually solitary, but may occur anywhere in the gut.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c025--> A stalk and a smooth, round surface are typical; most measure 2 cm or less.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c026-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What do you see on H&E?**
- The follicles of a lymphoid polyp are conspicuous and have germinal centers.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c027-->
- Granulation tissue often sits between the glands, whose distortion and inflammation vary from polyp to polyp.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c028--> By definition there is no dysplasia.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c029-->
- Peutz–Jeghers epithelium resembles normal small bowel, yet its arrangement is often unusual.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c030--> Under the epithelium, smooth muscle branches like a tree in thick bundles.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c031-->
- Juvenile polyps: a fibrovascular lamina propria holds cystically dilated glands.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c032--> The lumina contain mucus and inflammatory cells.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c033--> Granulation tissue often lies under a surface that has eroded.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c034--> Dysplasia is rare.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c035-->
- In appearance, the polyps of PTEN syndromes frequently cannot be told from juvenile polyps.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c036--> Some contain many neurons and ganglion cells.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c037-->
- Cronkhite–Canada polyps also look like juvenile polyps.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c038-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** on routine H&E, think of Peutz–Jeghers when thick, branching smooth muscle lies under unremarkable epithelium.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c039--> A juvenile polyp is suggested by a fibrovascular stroma containing cystic glands full of mucus.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c040-->
- **Molecular:** the Peutz–Jeghers gene LKB1 lies on chromosome 19p13.3.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c041-->

The reference pages give no specimen, special-stain, immunohistochemistry or electron microscopy details.

## Clinical presentation

**How do they present?**
- Lymphoid polyps are benign; they seldom cause symptoms.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c042-->
- A sporadic juvenile polyp typically bleeds from the rectum or prolapses.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c043--> Children under 10 years are affected most often.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c044--> Up to 30% arise in adults.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c045-->
- Pigmented spots on the lips, inside the mouth, and on the extremities and genitals mark Peutz–Jeghers syndrome.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c046-->
- PTEN syndromes bring hamartomas of the bowel, the thyroid and breast, and the skin.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c047-->
- Cronkhite–Canada syndrome can bring anemia and electrolyte problems, as well as protein-losing enteropathy.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c048--> The skin darkens, nails deform and hair falls out.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c049-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is the syndrome recognized?** Finding at least five juvenile polyps in the colon and rectum allows the diagnosis of juvenile polyposis syndrome.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c050--> Juvenile polyps outside the colon also qualify.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c051--> So does a single juvenile polyp, or more, in someone whose family has the syndrome.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c052--> Cowden syndrome is recognized from the clinical picture and from manifestations found outside the intestine.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c053--> Imaging of nodular lymphoid hyperplasia can lead to a mistaken diagnosis of FAP.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c054-->

## Complications and prognosis

**Which of these polyps carry a cancer risk?**
- Inflammatory polyps have no malignant potential.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c055--> A link between nodular lymphoid hyperplasia and malignant lymphoma is uncommon.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c056-->
- Peutz–Jeghers syndrome raises cancer risk mainly outside the gut, in the pancreas and reproductive organs (ovary, uterus, testis).<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c057-->
- Malignancy is no more likely if the juvenile polyps are sporadic.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c058--> Carcinoma of the gastrointestinal tract and of the pancreas is more likely in juvenile polyposis syndrome.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c059-->
- Cowden syndrome raises the risk of breast, thyroid and gynecologic cancers (ovary, cervix, uterus), bladder cancer and meningioma.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c060--> The risk of gastrointestinal cancer does not seem to be raised.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c061-->
- Cronkhite–Canada patients may face more gastric and colorectal cancer, but this is not entirely clear.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c062-->

## High-yield summary

- **Inflammatory polyps** show no dysplasia and have no malignant potential.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c068-->
- **Lymphoid polyps** are benign submucosal lymphoid follicles with germinal centers.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c069-->
- **Peutz–Jeghers** polyps: bland epithelium over **arborizing smooth muscle**.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c070-->
- **Peutz–Jeghers** syndrome: autosomal dominant, **LKB1**, pigmented lips and hands.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c071-->
- **Juvenile polyps** are mostly rectal, with cystic mucus-filled glands and bleeding.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c072-->
- **Juvenile polyposis** (SMAD4, BMPR1A) raises gastrointestinal and pancreatic cancer risk.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c073-->
- Juvenile polyposis needs **five or more** colorectal polyps, or polyps outside the colon, or a family history.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c074-->
- **Cowden** syndrome (PTEN) does not raise gastrointestinal cancer risk.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c075-->
- **Cronkhite–Canada** syndrome is not inherited and shows alopecia and nail dystrophy.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c076-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does every polyp carry malignant potential?** No. Inflammatory polyps have none, by definition.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c077-->

**Is a single juvenile polyp a cancer warning?** No. A sporadic juvenile polyp carries no added risk.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c078--> The risk belongs to the syndrome.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c079-->

**Do Peutz–Jeghers patients mainly get gut cancer?** No. Their extra cancers are mostly outside the gut.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c080-->

**Is Cronkhite–Canada syndrome hereditary, like juvenile polyposis?** No, it is not inherited.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c081-->

**Does Cowden syndrome raise gastrointestinal cancer risk?** It does not appear to.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c082-->

**Are inflammatory polyps found only in inflammatory bowel disease?** No. They can follow any colitis, and some arise in a colon with no disease.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c083-->

**Is every serrated right-colon polyp a harmless hyperplastic polyp?** No. Hyperplastic polyps are benign, but a sessile serrated adenoma can progress to colorectal carcinoma.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c084-->

## Mnemonics

**PLS** for Peutz–Jeghers: Pigmented lips, LKB1, Small-bowel polyps.<!--c:non-neoplastic-intestinal-polyps-c085-->
