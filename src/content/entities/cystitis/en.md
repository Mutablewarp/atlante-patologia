## Definition and epidemiology

**What is cystitis, and how common is it?** Cystitis is inflammation of the bladder, either acute or chronic.<!--c:cystitis-c001--> Among urinary tract infections, it is the one seen most often.<!--c:cystitis-c002--> Inpatients, above all those with an indwelling catheter, are often affected.<!--c:cystitis-c003-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What predisposes to cystitis, and which agents cause it?** In most cases an infection of the lower urinary tract comes first.<!--c:cystitis-c004--> Diabetes, weak immunity, stones and blocked outflow favor bladder infection, as do prior instrumentation, radiotherapy and chemotherapy.<!--c:cystitis-c005--> A short urethra puts women at higher risk, which rises further in pregnancy.<!--c:cystitis-c006--> In men, an enlarged prostate that blocks bladder outflow favors cystitis.<!--c:cystitis-c007-->
- The leading cause is a coliform, mostly E. coli; Proteus, Pseudomonas and Enterobacter are also named.<!--c:cystitis-c008-->
- Almost all bladder tuberculosis follows kidney tuberculosis; in the West it is now rare.<!--c:cystitis-c009-->
- Fungal cystitis can occur in immunosuppressed patients.<!--c:cystitis-c010-->
- S. haematobium, endemic in North Africa and the Middle East, commonly causes cystitis there.<!--c:cystitis-c011-->
- Radiation cystitis is most often seen after pelvic irradiation for bladder, rectal or uterine cancer.<!--c:cystitis-c012-->
- Cyclophosphamide, the drug most often responsible, typically causes the hemorrhagic form.<!--c:cystitis-c013-->
- E. coli infection often accompanies malakoplakia, but a direct causal role is doubtful.<!--c:cystitis-c014-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does the bladder become inflamed, and what happens if it does not heal?** Long-term catheters make cystitis frequent, and cystoscopy can introduce pathogens.<!--c:cystitis-c015--> Chronic cystitis, with lymphocytes, plasma cells and lamina propria fibrosis, is linked to acute cystitis that fails to resolve.<!--c:cystitis-c016--> Interstitial cystitis has no known cause.<!--c:cystitis-c017--> The cause of malakoplakia is unknown; it is an uncommon inflammatory condition.<!--c:cystitis-c018--> Bacteria left undigested in macrophage lysosomes hint that malakoplakia may be an acquired digestion defect.<!--c:cystitis-c019--> The Michaelis–Gutmann bodies arise when calcium salts build up inside the swollen lysosomes.<!--c:cystitis-c020-->

## Gross morphology

**What does the inflamed bladder look like?**
- In acute cystitis caused by a catheter, the mucosa is congested and dotted with foci of bleeding.<!--c:cystitis-c021-->
- Acute bacterial cystitis frequently shows mucosal petechiae, hence "hemorrhagic cystitis".<!--c:cystitis-c022-->
- Extensive hemorrhagic cystitis is often due to a bleeding disorder or disseminated intravascular coagulation.<!--c:cystitis-c023-->
- Ulcers that bleed locally may follow long-term catheter irritation or injury at cystoscopy.<!--c:cystitis-c024-->
- In suppurative cystitis the bladder holds pus: within the wall, free in the lumen or spread over the lining.<!--c:cystitis-c025-->
- In pseudomembranous cystitis the lining is ulcerated and bleeding, under a loose film of fibrin and necrotic material.<!--c:cystitis-c026-->
- This form typically appears when a patient treated with a cytotoxic drug, cyclophosphamide for example, gets an infection.<!--c:cystitis-c027-->
- Schistosomiasis typically causes calcific cystitis: calcified eggs form gritty crusts on the wall.<!--c:cystitis-c028-->
- A late result is a stiff, fully calcified bladder, formed as the crusts fuse.<!--c:cystitis-c029-->
- Petechiae and bleeding cracks appear in the mucosa of interstitial cystitis when cystoscopy distends the bladder.<!--c:cystitis-c030-->
- The Hunner ulcer lies on the posterior bladder wall: a raw, bleeding patch in an otherwise swollen lining.<!--c:cystitis-c031-->
- Malakoplakia shows yellow, soft plaques on the lining.<!--c:cystitis-c032-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the tissue show under the microscope?**
- Neutrophils, in varying numbers, infiltrate the edematous and hemorrhagic stroma of acute cystitis.<!--c:cystitis-c033-->
- In chronic cystitis the lamina propria is fibrotic and infiltrated mainly by lymphocytes and plasma cells.<!--c:cystitis-c034-->
- Lymphoid follicles define follicular cystitis.<!--c:cystitis-c035-->
- Dense eosinophil infiltrates define eosinophilic cystitis.<!--c:cystitis-c036-->
- Tuberculosis characteristically causes granulomatous cystitis, and so can intravesical BCG for bladder cancer.<!--c:cystitis-c037-->
- In schistosomiasis, one bladder can show both granulomas and eosinophils around S. haematobium ova.<!--c:cystitis-c038-->
- Small gas bubbles in the lamina propria mark emphysematous cystitis, caused by gas-producing bacilli, mostly in diabetes.<!--c:cystitis-c039-->
- Interstitial cystitis shows mast cells and fibrosis with chronic inflammation in mucosa and muscularis.<!--c:cystitis-c040-->
- Marked acute inflammation fills a Hunner ulcer.<!--c:cystitis-c041-->
- The inflammatory cells of malakoplakia are chiefly macrophages: large, with a broad pink cytoplasm.<!--c:cystitis-c042-->
- A subset of these macrophages contain Michaelis–Gutmann bodies, which are basophilic and laminated.<!--c:cystitis-c043-->
- In an inflamed bladder the epithelial cells often look atypical, but this passes and does not mean cancer.<!--c:cystitis-c044-->
- Urothelial hyperplasia and metaplasia are mostly found together with long-standing inflammation.<!--c:cystitis-c045-->
- Cystitis cystica cysts are dilated Brunn nests that normal urothelium still lines.<!--c:cystitis-c046-->
- When the urothelium turns into mucus-secreting epithelium, the change is named cystitis glandularis.<!--c:cystitis-c047-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**
- **How it is studied:** the source pages describe no specimen, sampling, IHC or molecular tests.
- The macrophage granules of malakoplakia stain with PAS.<!--c:cystitis-c048-->
- Lysosomes stuffed with bacterial pieces make up the granules on electron microscopy.<!--c:cystitis-c049-->

## Clinical presentation

**What does the patient notice?** Acute or chronic, cystitis nearly always means frequent, painful voiding and low abdominal or pelvic ache.<!--c:cystitis-c050--> Interstitial cystitis brings dysuria and hematuria, with suprapubic pain and urgency.<!--c:cystitis-c051--> Interstitial cystitis affects middle-aged women, painfully and persistently.<!--c:cystitis-c052--> Women are affected far more often than men in malakoplakia, which can arise at any age.<!--c:cystitis-c053--> Many patients have cancer, long-standing infection or immunosuppression in their history.<!--c:cystitis-c054--> Its symptoms match those of other chronic cystitis.<!--c:cystitis-c055-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which tests point to the cause?** Urine typically holds inflammatory cells.<!--c:cystitis-c056--> Urine culture identifies the causative agent.<!--c:cystitis-c057--> Interstitial cystitis almost always has a sterile urine culture.<!--c:cystitis-c058--> Cytologic atypia from cytotoxic drugs is usually short-lived.<!--c:cystitis-c059--> Imaging is not covered.

## Complications and prognosis

**How does cystitis end?** Antimicrobial therapy works well in most acute cases.<!--c:cystitis-c060--> Recurrent and chronic cystitis can be hard to treat.<!--c:cystitis-c061--> Interstitial cystitis typically lingers and does not respond to treatment.<!--c:cystitis-c062--> Treatment of malakoplakia is ineffective.<!--c:cystitis-c063--> Late radiation cystitis can cause widespread, disabling fibrosis.<!--c:cystitis-c064--> Bladder cancer risk rises with S. haematobium infection where the parasite is endemic.<!--c:cystitis-c065--> Where schistosomiasis is endemic, as in parts of Africa like Egypt and Sudan, bladder cancer is frequent.<!--c:cystitis-c066--> Most of these cancers are squamous cell carcinomas.<!--c:cystitis-c067--> Urothelial carcinoma is more likely in patients with benign proliferative or metaplastic urothelial changes.<!--c:cystitis-c068--> Nothing indicates that the lesions are themselves precursors of cancer.<!--c:cystitis-c069-->

## High-yield summary

- **Coliform bacteria**, above all **E. coli**, are the commonest cause of cystitis.<!--c:cystitis-c075-->
- **Cyclophosphamide** is the classic drug cause of **hemorrhagic cystitis**.<!--c:cystitis-c076-->
- **Interstitial cystitis** is almost always **culture-negative**.<!--c:cystitis-c077-->
- A **Hunner ulcer** can appear in chronic **interstitial cystitis**.<!--c:cystitis-c078-->
- **Michaelis–Gutmann bodies** are found in the macrophages of **malakoplakia**.<!--c:cystitis-c079-->
- **Schistosomiasis** predisposes to **squamous cell carcinoma** of the bladder.<!--c:cystitis-c080-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is cystitis always a bacterial infection?** No: interstitial cystitis has no known cause and its urine culture is nearly always sterile.<!--c:cystitis-c081-->

**Does hemorrhagic cystitis always mean a drug effect?** No. Petechial hemorrhages are also common in acute bacterial cystitis.<!--c:cystitis-c082-->

**Does atypia in an inflamed bladder mean cancer?** No. Such atypia is usually short-lived and is not a sign of cancer.<!--c:cystitis-c083-->

**Is malakoplakia a tumor?** No. It is a rare inflammatory disease whose cause is unknown.<!--c:cystitis-c084-->
