## Definition and epidemiology

**What happens to the mitral valve in prolapse?** Oversized leaflets with excess tissue bulge back into the left atrium as the ventricle contracts.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c001--> The condition is also called floppy mitral valve syndrome.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c002--> No other lesion leads more often to valve repair or replacement.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c003--> On echocardiography, as many as 3% of adults may have the lesion.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c004--> Most of them never need an operation for the leak.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Is prolapse always inherited as part of a syndrome?** No: it can stand alone (primary, nonsyndromic) or accompany another disease (secondary, syndromic).<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c006--> [[ehlers-danlos-syndromes|Ehlers–Danlos syndrome]] and [[osteogenesis-imperfecta]] are inherited matrix-protein defects linked to syndromic prolapse.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c007--> In [[marfan-syndrome]], prolapse is found in more than 90% of patients.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c008--> Penetrance depends on age and sex, but the nonsyndromic form is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, so it clusters in families.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c009--> Loci on chromosomes 16, 11 and 13 are linked to it, but the causal allele is unknown.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c010--> DCHS1 encodes a cadherin-type protein; a missense mutation in it has been found in one large affected family.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c011--> No role of FBN1 has been observed in nonsyndromic prolapse, although prolapse is so common in Marfan syndrome.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c012-->

## Pathogenesis

**Which signal is disturbed in the valve tissue?** Primary prolapse shows disturbed TGFβ signaling in the valves that surgeons have excised.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c013--> Valve interstitial cells are activated by TGFβ, which also promotes matrix remodeling.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c014--> Metallothioneins, which help the cell respond to oxidative stress, are expressed less under TGFβ.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c015--> Genes of the ADAMTS family encode enzymes that degrade proteoglycans, and TGFβ downregulates them.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c016--> The same pathway has been linked to the syndromic forms.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c017--> TGFBR2, the gene for TGFβ receptor-2, is one place where a mutation can produce Marfan syndrome.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c018--> Heterozygous mutations of TGFBR1 or TGFBR2 cause Loeys–Dietz syndrome, a connective tissue disorder with frequent prolapse.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c019-->

## Gross morphology

**What does a floppy valve look like?** The leaflets bulge into the left atrium and have lost their normal shape; there is simply too much leaflet tissue.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c020--> Slippery, jelly-like cut surfaces are the visible result of proteoglycan deposition.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c021--> The chordae tendineae are long and thin.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c022--> Weakened chordae can snap.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c023--> A valve whose chordae have ruptured in numbers is called flail and cannot seal at all.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c024--> Usually no valve other than the mitral is involved.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c025--> In Marfan syndrome the tricuspid valve is often abnormal, and myxomatous change can in general reach other valves too.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c026-->

## Microscopic morphology

**Where is the abnormal tissue in the leaflet?** Much of the leaflet's central layer, the spongiosa, is taken up by myxomatous connective tissue.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c027--> Proteoglycans (acid mucopolysaccharides) accumulate there.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c028--> The collagen fibrils are fragmented.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c029--> Prolapse and regurgitation get worse when the annulus and chordae are also degenerated in the same myxomatous way.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c030-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**

**How it is studied:**
- Specimen: the tissue examined for TGFβ changes in primary prolapse comes from surgically excised valves.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c031-->
- On a leaflet section, look in the center for myxomatous tissue.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c032-->
- The reference text used for this card names no special stain, immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy or diagnostic molecular test for this lesion.

## Clinical presentation

**What does the patient notice, and what does the doctor hear?** Most patients are asymptomatic.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c033--> The leaflets are too large, and when they flip back into the left atrium they snap, producing the classic mid- to late-systolic click.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c034--> A significant leak adds a murmur at the end of systole.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c035-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is prolapse suspected and shown?** Prolapse is first suspected from characteristic sounds on auscultation.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c036--> Echocardiography can show the prolapse.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c037-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What can go wrong over time?** Prolapse can be complicated by endocarditis, which may be serious; see [[infective-endocarditis]].<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c038--> After 10 to 15 years of prolapse, 15% of patients have significant mitral regurgitation.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c039--> At that point the valve should be repaired or replaced.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c040--> Compared with the general population, patients die suddenly twice as often, presumably because of ventricular tachyarrhythmias.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c041--> The degree of regurgitation seems to be the main driver of this risk; volume overload and the ventricular remodeling it brings may be the link.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c042--> Fixing a leaking floppy valve lowers it.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c043-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What else makes the mitral valve leak?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[ischemic-heart-disease\|Papillary muscle dysfunction]] | It is usually due to ischemia.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c044--> Regurgitation is permanent when infarction leaves the papillary muscles scarred, but temporary after brief ischemia such as angina, which stuns the muscle.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c045--> | Look for coronary ischemia: angina or infarct. |
| [[dystrophic-calcification\|Mitral annulus calcification]] | The leaflet shape is normal; the calcium is deposited chiefly in the annulus.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c046--> A heavily calcified annulus can stop the posterior leaflet from moving freely in systole, and the valve then leaks.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c047--> | Calcification of the annulus may show up on a radiograph as a curved structure.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c048--> |

- Papillary muscles are especially vulnerable to ischemia because their blood comes from terminal branches of coronary arteries running within the myocardium.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c049-->
- After an infarct has healed, the muscle may still work poorly because the myocardium at its base contracts weakly.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c050-->
- A papillary muscle can rupture in a fresh infarct, which is rare and causes abrupt, dangerous regurgitation.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c051-->

## High-yield summary

- In systole the leaflets of a **floppy valve** bulge into the left atrium; the underlying change is **myxomatous degeneration**, with thin, long chordae.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c052-->
- Most patients are **asymptomatic**; the classic sign is a **mid- to late-systolic click**.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c053-->
- In **Marfan syndrome**, prolapse is found in more than 90% of patients.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c054-->
- **TGFβ** signaling is dysregulated in primary prolapse.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c055-->
- Prolapse can be complicated by **infective endocarditis**, which may be serious.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c056-->
- Rupture of several chordae gives a **flail mitral valve**.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c057-->
- The degree of regurgitation seems to be the main determinant of the raised risk of **sudden death**.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c058-->
- **Papillary muscle dysfunction** is another cause of mitral regurgitation and is usually ischemic.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c059-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does every patient with prolapse need surgery?** No. Most patients have no symptoms, and most never need an operation for the leak.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c060-->

**Is the myxomatous change limited to the leaflets?** No. It also affects the annulus and the chordae, and sometimes other valves.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c061-->

**Is a fibrillin-1 defect the cause of ordinary, nonsyndromic prolapse?** No. FBN1 variants have not been found in the nonsyndromic form, even though prolapse is very common in Marfan syndrome.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c062-->

**Is papillary muscle dysfunction always permanent?** No. A short episode of angina can stun the muscle, and the regurgitation then lasts only a while.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c063-->

## Mnemonics

**FLOP:** **F**loppy, redundant leaflets, **L**ong thin chordae, **O**ne click in mid- to late systole, **P**rolapse into the left atrium.<!--c:mitral-valve-prolapse-c064-->
