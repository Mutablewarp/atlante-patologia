## Definition and epidemiology

**What is a colorectal adenoma?** It is a polyp lined by dysplastic epithelium, and colon carcinoma usually arises from it.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c001--> Low-grade dysplasia is the minimum finding that qualifies a polyp as an adenoma.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c002--> Small-bowel adenomas look like colonic ones but are much rarer.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c003--> Industrialized countries account for most of them.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c004--> Half of US adults have an adenoma; risk climbs steeply from 50.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Who gets adenomas, and why?** Among environmental factors, only a Western diet is known to track consistently with adenoma-prone populations.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c006--> A relative with colonic adenomas or cancer, as well as smoking and obesity, makes adenomas more likely.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c007--> In [[familial-adenomatous-polyposis|familial adenomatous polyposis]] (FAP), the underlying defect is a germline loss-of-function mutation of the *APC* gene, and the trait is autosomal dominant.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c008--> Most FAP patients inherit the mutation, but in some it appears de novo.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c009--> Biallelic *MYH* mutations (a DNA repair gene) cause a recessive polyposis, rare and clinically FAP-like, but not FAP itself.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c010-->

## Pathogenesis

**What goes wrong in the crypt?** The epithelial cells keep replicating when they should stop.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c011--> They die less often by apoptosis.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c012--> Cells ascending the crypt never fully mature or differentiate.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c013--> Normally, shedding comes last, after DNA synthesis ends in the upper crypt and the cell matures.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c014--> In adenomas, proliferation can happen at any crypt depth, because the normal sequence is interrupted in patches.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c015--> As the lesion grows, cells are made faster than shed, so they build up toward the crypt top and the mucosal surface.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c016--> APC acts as a tumor suppressor within Wnt/β-catenin signaling, a pathway that controls how fast colon lining cells divide.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c017--> High-grade lesions may become invasive adenocarcinoma.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c018--> Adenomatous polyps are where most colorectal adenocarcinomas begin.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c019-->

## Gross morphology

**Where do adenomas sit, and what do they look like?** In the US, colonic adenomas are evenly spread apart from a rectosigmoid cluster holding almost half.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c020--> A stalked adenoma can be small, a sessile (flat) one large, and some are barely visible nodules.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c021--> About two thirds of large-bowel adenomas are tubular, which makes this the most common type.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c022--> Small tubular adenomas may be sessile; typical ones are smooth, often stalked.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c023--> The rectosigmoid is the main site of villous adenomas, which are the rarest of the three types.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c024--> Small stalked villous adenomas exist, but most are large, flat-based masses with a ragged cauliflower surface.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c025-->

## Microscopic morphology

**How is an adenoma classified, and how is its dysplasia graded?** The proportion of villous architecture decides the type: tubular, villous or tubulovillous.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c026--> Adenoma villi are long narrow projections, like small-bowel villi.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c027--> Tubular adenomas pack many heavily branching epithelial tubes together, regularly or irregularly.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c028--> Tubulovillous adenomas are 25% to 75% villous.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c029--> In a villous adenoma, neoplastic epithelium covers each villus and lamina propria forms its core.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c030--> Low-grade dysplasia shows little layering of nuclei, which are large, dark and cigar-shaped.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c031--> Prominent nucleoli and greater nuclear pleomorphism mark high-grade dysplasia.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c032--> Its glands also form more intricate patterns, for example cribriform ones.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c033--> Ordinary colonic lining can cover the stalk of a tubular adenoma, which then contains no tumor.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c034-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** polypectomy removes the polyp; if invasive tumor is present, the base margin of the specimen shows whether that was enough.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c035-->
- **Routine histology:** look at the proportion of villi and at the grade of dysplasia.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c036-->
- **Special stains, immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy:** this card does not cover them.
- **Molecular:** three quarters of familial FAP cases carry a detectable *APC* mutation.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c037-->

## Clinical presentation

**What symptoms do adenomas cause?** This card does not cover symptoms of sporadic adenomas. In FAP, symptoms usually start by age 36, whereas a few polyps are already present by age 10.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c038-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is invasion recognized, and how is FAP recognized?** A polyp is invasive adenocarcinoma only if tumor glands reach beneath the muscularis mucosae.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c039--> An FAP colon is blanketed with adenomas, hundreds to thousands of them.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c040--> Tubulovillous and villous types can be present as well, but most FAP adenomas are tubular.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c041--> This card does not cover laboratory or imaging tests.

## Complications and prognosis

**Which adenomas are most likely to hide a cancer?** Size matters: the bigger the adenoma, the likelier it contains invasive carcinoma.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c042--> High-grade dysplasia also makes hidden carcinoma more likely.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c043--> So does a villous growth pattern.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c044--> At resection, invasive cancer is found in 1% of tubular adenomas smaller than 1 cm.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c045--> Among tubular adenomas of 1 to 2 cm, about 10% are malignant.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c046--> In tubular adenomas larger than 2 cm the proportion rises to about 35%.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c047--> Size for size, villous adenomas under 1 cm carry ten times the cancer risk of tubular ones.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c048--> Invasive carcinoma is present in as many as half of villous adenomas larger than 2 cm.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c049--> Complete polypectomy cures an adenoma whose dysplasia has not passed the mucosa.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c050--> When the tumor is low-risk and the base margin adequate, polypectomy by itself can cure a cancer inside a polyp.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c051--> In FAP, colorectal carcinoma is inevitable without treatment.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c052--> The mean age at onset is about 40 years.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c053--> Total colectomy before cancer develops is curative.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c054--> Gastric and small-bowel adenomas in FAP carry the same malignant potential as colonic ones.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c055-->

| [[serrated-colorectal-lesions\|Sessile serrated adenoma]] | Crypts can look like boots, an L or an upside-down T, and some have widened bases.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c057--> | Histology of crypt architecture |
| [[serrated-colorectal-lesions\|Traditional serrated adenoma]] | The epithelium is serrated and its cytoplasm is abundant and eosinophilic; the growth pattern is usually tubulovillous or villous.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c058--> | Histology |

## High-yield summary

- Adenomas are **dysplastic** precursors of most **colorectal adenocarcinomas**.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c060-->
- **Tubular** adenomas are the most common type.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c061-->
- **Villous** adenomas are riskier than tubular ones of the same size.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c062-->
- **Size** is the first predictor of hidden cancer: the larger the adenoma, the likelier it is.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c063-->
- **High-grade dysplasia** is not invasion: neoplastic glands must breach the **muscularis mucosae**.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c064-->
- **Complete polypectomy** is curative while dysplasia has not passed the mucosa.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c065-->
- **FAP** means a germline **APC** mutation, hundreds to thousands of adenomas, and cancer without colectomy.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c066-->
- Risk factors for adenomas: **Western diet**, smoking, obesity, family history.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c067-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is the villous adenoma the common type?** No. Tubular adenomas are the most common and villous ones the least common.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c068-->

**Does high-grade dysplasia mean the polyp is already invasive cancer?** No. Neoplastic glands must lie deeper than the muscularis mucosae.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c069-->

**Does cancer found in a polyp always need surgery?** No. With low-risk features and an adequate base margin, polypectomy alone can be enough.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c070-->

**Do all adenomas sit in the rectosigmoid?** No. About half do; the rest are spread over the colon.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c071-->

**Are the polyps of FAP mostly villous?** No. Most are tubular.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c072-->

## Mnemonics

**Think of a VHS tape:** **V**illous, **H**igh-grade dysplasia, **S**ize are the three features that raise an adenoma's cancer risk.<!--c:colorectal-adenoma-c073-->
