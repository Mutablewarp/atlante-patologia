## Definition and epidemiology

**What is minimal change disease?** It is a glomerular disease that causes nephrotic syndrome. Its hallmark lesion is the flattening (effacement) of podocyte foot processes across the whole glomerulus.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c001-->

**Who gets it?** Children above all. Of all children under 5 years with primary nephrotic syndrome, about 90% have this disease; the share is about 50% in older children and about 15% in adults.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c002-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What causes it?** The cause depends on the form.

- The primary (idiopathic) form has no identified cause and occurs especially in children.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c003-->
- The secondary form occurs especially in adults.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c004-->
- Infectious mononucleosis and HIV infection are among the infections that can trigger it.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c005-->
- Allergic triggers include bee stings, vaccination and drugs, mainly NSAIDs.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c006-->
- It can accompany a lymphoma, for example Hodgkin lymphoma or mantle-cell lymphoma.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c007-->

## Pathogenesis

**What goes wrong in the glomerulus?** Nobody knows why the primary form starts.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c008-->

**Why do we suspect the immune system?** An immune cause is suspected: allergy or lymphoid tumors may accompany it, and steroids can induce remission.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c009-->

**What is the leading hypothesis?** It rarely coexists with Hodgkin disease, thymoma or T-cell lymphoma, so a T-cell disorder is suspected; a cytokine acting on podocytes could make the filter leakier.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c010-->

**Why does albumin leak?** Albumin and other anionic proteins leak because the basement membrane and podocytes lose their negative (polyanionic) charge, a change seen with heavy proteinuria.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c011-->

**Can it turn into FSGS?** Some patients might go on to develop a form of FSGS, hinting at a shared mechanism, though that link is unproven.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c012-->

## Gross morphology

Not applicable.

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the kidney biopsy show?** On light microscopy the glomeruli look essentially normal.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c013-->

On electron microscopy the podocyte foot processes are fused over most of the glomerulus.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c014-->

Nearly every patient with nephrotic-range proteinuria shows this fusion, so it cannot identify the disease alone.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c015-->

The mesangium may stain faintly for IgM and C3, but immunofluorescence for immunoglobulins and complement is usually negative.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c016-->

**What do the tubules show?** Hyaline droplets in the cytoplasm of proximal tubular cells are lipid taken up from lipoproteins lost across the glomerulus.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c017-->

Any glomerular cause of nephrotic syndrome can produce the same droplets, so they do not single out this disease.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c018-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which stain?**

- **How it is studied:** diagnosis rests on the clinical picture plus a kidney tissue sample studied by three methods: light microscopy, immunofluorescence and electron microscopy.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c019-->
- On routine light microscopy the glomeruli are normal, so look at the proximal tubules for hyaline droplets.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c020-->
- On immunofluorescence expect little or no immunoglobulin or complement deposition.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c021-->
- On electron microscopy look for extensive foot-process effacement.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c022-->

The packet pages describe no special stains, immunohistochemistry or molecular tests for this disease.

## Clinical presentation

**What happens when so much protein is lost?** The urinary protein loss leads to a low blood albumin (hypoalbuminemia).<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c023-->

Hyperlipidemia follows because the liver, to compensate, secretes more lipoproteins.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c024-->

**Is the proteinuria selective?** Usually more so than in nephrotic syndrome from other causes: albumin is lost in larger amounts than globulins.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c025-->

Selectivity cannot serve as a diagnostic criterion, because other diseases overlap with it too much.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c026-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which findings point to the diagnosis?** Proteinuria with low blood albumin and raised blood lipids: the nephrotic picture.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c027-->

The biopsy then confirms it: normal glomeruli on light microscopy, foot-process effacement on electron microscopy and little or no immune deposit.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c028-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How does it respond to steroids?** Over 90% of children lose all their proteinuria within 8 weeks of starting steroids; adults do so less often.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c029-->

Remission in adults often takes a longer steroid course.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c030-->

Most patients relapse on and off for as long as 10 years once steroids are stopped.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c031-->

**What if the response is poor?** Complete steroid resistance is rarer than partial response, in which urinary protein loss persists.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c032-->

Total steroid resistance casts doubt on the diagnosis: FSGS in tissue the first biopsy did not sample may be the real disease.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c033-->

**What is the long-term outlook?** Barring complications, patients do as well in the long term as the general population.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c034-->

Interstitial nephritis from a drug, evolution into FSGS, or FSGS missed at the first biopsy can each explain azotemia appearing after the diagnosis (see [[drug-induced-interstitial-nephritis]]).<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c035-->

## Differential diagnosis

**What else can cause nephrotic syndrome?**

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[focal-segmental-glomerulosclerosis\|FSGS]] | Only some glomeruli are affected, and each only in part, with matrix-rich scarred (sclerotic) segments.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c036--> | Renal biopsy, light microscopy |
| [[membranous-nephropathy]] | Subepithelial dense deposits with granular capillary-wall staining.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c037--> | Electron microscopy and immunofluorescence |
| [[diabetic-nephropathy]] | Thickened glomerular basement membrane.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c038--> | Electron microscopy |

## High-yield summary

- **Children** are the typical patients, with nephrotic syndrome from a primary (idiopathic) form.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c039-->
- **Normal light microscopy** with **foot-process effacement** on electron microscopy.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c040-->
- **Immunofluorescence** is usually negative.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c041-->
- **Selective proteinuria** (albumin more than globulins) is common but not diagnostic.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c042-->
- **Steroid response** is the rule: complete remission in most children, with frequent relapses afterward.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c043-->
- **Steroid resistance** should make you suspect missed FSGS.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c044-->
- **Adults** more often have a secondary form (infection, drugs, lymphoma).<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c045-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does foot-process effacement prove minimal change disease?** No. It is found in almost all nephrotic-range proteinuria.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c046-->

**Does selective proteinuria confirm the diagnosis?** No. Too many other diseases overlap with it.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c047-->

**Are hyaline droplets in tubular cells specific?** No. Any nephrotic glomerular disease can produce them.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c048-->

**A patient does not respond to steroids: is it still minimal change disease?** Doubt it. FSGS missed on the first biopsy may be the real diagnosis.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c049-->

**Does a normal glomerulus on light microscopy mean no glomerular disease?** No. The lesion lies in the podocytes and shows only on electron microscopy.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c050-->

**Is it only a childhood disease?** No. It also occurs in adults, who more often have a secondary form.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c051-->

## Mnemonics

**FEET:** Foot processes fused on electron microscopy, Everything else normal on light microscopy, Empty immunofluorescence, Treated successfully with steroids.<!--c:minimal-change-disease-c052-->
