## Definition and epidemiology
**What is pulmonary hypertension, and what is cor pulmonale?** In this textbook, a mean resting pressure above 25 mm Hg in the pulmonary arteries counts as pulmonary hypertension.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c001--> The pressure climbs when the lungs receive more flow or their vessels offer more resistance.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c002--> When high pulmonary arterial pressure makes the right ventricle thicken and widen, the result is cor pulmonale.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c003-->

<aside class="edition">Cut-offs and group names differ between classification editions, so check a current guideline.</aside>

Pulmonary arterial hypertension mostly strikes women in their 20s and 30s, though any age can.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors
**What can push pulmonary pressure up?** Ask whether the problem lies before or after the capillary bed.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c005-->
- Precapillary: left-to-right shunts add flow; emboli, hypoxia, lung fibrosis and primary vascular disease add resistance.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c006-->
- Postcapillary: left heart disorders, mitral stenosis among them, and veno-occlusive disease of the lung.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c007-->

Drugs or toxins (such as "fen-phen") and collagen vascular disease can underlie pulmonary arterial hypertension; other cases are idiopathic or hereditary.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c008--> Hereditary cases show mutations in BMPR2, ALK1 or ENG.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c009--> Poor ventilation (kyphoscoliosis, Pickwickian syndrome), lung disease or altitude can cause hypoxia and raise pulmonary pressure.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c010--> Small emboli arriving again and again from peripheral veins, sometimes the deep leg veins, slowly raise pulmonary pressure.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c011--> Most chronic cor pulmonale comes from pulmonary fibrosis, or from obstructed breathing in COPD or sleep apnea.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c012--> Massive pulmonary embolism of abrupt onset is the usual cause of acute cor pulmonale.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c013-->

## Pathogenesis
**Why does the pressure rise?** After birth the pulmonary arteries dilate and thin, whereas before birth their walls are thick and the pressure high.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c014--> Left-to-right shunts add flow; most are congenital.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c016--> In mitral valve disease venous pressure rises first and arterial pressure follows, so the lungs show hypertensive vessel changes together with chronic congestion.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c017-->

Ventilation and perfusion stay matched because vessels in poorly oxygenated lung regions narrow.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c018--> Low oxygen, excess carbon dioxide and acidosis can each narrow pulmonary vessels directly.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c019--> Thicker blood from hypoxia-induced polycythemia is an indirect way hypoxia raises resistance.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c020--> In chronic lung disease the usable vascular bed shrinks, as vessels are destroyed and arterioles constrict.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c021-->

BMPR2 mutations occur in many people with familial pulmonary arterial hypertension, a dominant trait with incomplete penetrance.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c023--> Faulty BMPR2 signaling damages the endothelium: thromboxane rises while nitric oxide and prostacyclin fall short.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c024--> Small arteries thicken as smooth muscle multiplies once vasoconstrictors outweigh vasodilators.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c025--> The gene findings point to altered TGF-β signaling.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c026-->

## Gross morphology
**What do the heart and lungs look like?** A normal right ventricular wall measures 0.3 to 0.5 cm; in chronic cor pulmonale hypertrophy can push it beyond 1.0 cm.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c027--> Chronic cor pulmonale frequently enlarges the right ventricular and atrial chambers.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c028--> Severe right hypertrophy flattens the septum or even bows it to the right; normally it curves to the left.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c029--> In acute cor pulmonale the heart is otherwise normal at autopsy; the one finding is a very dilated right ventricle, occasionally with the atrium.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c030--> In veno-occlusive disease the lung shows brown induration.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c032-->

## Microscopic morphology
**How are the small pulmonary arteries changed?** Heath and Edwards grade the wall changes in lung arteries; the grade relates to whether repairing the heart defect can undo the raised pressure.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c033--> Reversal is usually possible at grades 1 to 3 and usually not from grade 4 upward.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c034-->
- Grade 1: the media of muscular arteries thickens, and arterioles gain smooth muscle.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c035-->
- Grade 2: the media keeps thickening while the intima begins to proliferate.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c036-->
- Grade 3: the intima scars over, and the lumen may close off.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c037-->
- Grade 4: plexiform lesions, tangles of tiny channels that worsen the blockage; arteries are also dilated and thin.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c038-->
- Grade 5: thin dilated vessels can rupture, with bleeding and hemosiderin in the lung; angiomatoid lesions join the plexiform ones.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c039-->
- Grade 6: fibrinoid necrosis, affecting both arteries and arterioles.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c040-->

A pulmonary artery in pulmonary hypertension keeps its fetal elastic lamellae, normally lost after birth.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c041--> In veno-occlusive disease, intimal fibrosis of loose texture and few cells partly or completely blocks small veins and venules.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c042--> The pulmonary arteries may hold fresh thrombi and marked hypertensive damage, while alveolar walls are moderately fibrotic with patchy hemosiderosis.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c043--> Small pulmonary arteries may be crossed by fibrous strands ("webs") left when an embolus organizes.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c044-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings
**How is it studied?**
- **How it is studied:** the vessel changes are graded on the Heath and Edwards scale, from grade 1 to grade 6.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c045-->
- Genomic studies found rare recessive variants in CAV1, KCNK3 and EIF2AK4, and variants in other TGF-β family members.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c046-->

## Clinical presentation
**How does it present?** In pulmonary arterial hypertension, breathlessness creeps in while examination and imaging show little at first, more later.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c047--> Death comes from cor pulmonale, after severe disease with plexiform lesions has set in.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c048--> Veno-occlusive disease runs a more fulminant course than primary pulmonary hypertension, although both give progressive dyspnea.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c050--> Some patients with recurrent emboli have a venous clot, usually in the deep leg veins, or a history predisposing to venous thrombosis.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c051-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging
**What do the tests show?** Chest films in veno-occlusive disease show patchy infiltrates of hemorrhage and hemosiderosis that multiply over time.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c052-->

## Complications and prognosis
**What are the outcomes?** In chronic lung disease, pulmonary pressure is often the best match for survival among the variables followed.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c053--> Five-year survival is under 10% when pulmonary artery pressure is above 45 mm Hg.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c054--> PDE-5, endothelin and prostacyclin-targeted drugs now reach about 30% five-year survival, where older treatment mostly failed.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c055--> Transplantation is often indicated: lung alone, or heart plus lung when the heart has an uncorrectable defect.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c056--> Nothing treats veno-occlusive disease effectively, so a lung transplant is the option to weigh.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c057--> A filter in the inferior vena cava lowers the chance that new clots reach the lungs when recurrent emboli are recognized in life.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c058-->

## High-yield summary
- **Precapillary** forms (such as shunts and emboli) differ from **postcapillary** forms (such as veno-occlusive disease and mitral stenosis) by where flow or resistance rises.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c062-->
- **Cor pulmonale** is the right ventricular thickening and widening that high pulmonary pressure causes.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c063-->
- **Intimal fibrosis** marks grade 3 and **plexiform lesions** grade 4; grades 4 and above are generally not reversible.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c064-->
- **BMPR2** mutations underlie many hereditary cases, pointing to altered **TGF-β** signaling.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c065-->
- Alveolar hypoxia causes **vasoconstriction** of small pulmonary arteries.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c066-->
- Pulmonary arterial hypertension is commonest in **young women**, and patients die of cor pulmonale.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c067-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps
**Is primary pulmonary hypertension the usual form?** No. The textbook describes it as uncommon and precapillary.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c068-->

**Does cor pulmonale mean left heart failure?** No. It is right ventricular thickening and widening from high pulmonary pressure.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c069-->

**Are the vessel changes always reversible once the cause is fixed?** No. Grades 1 to 3 can usually be reversed; grade 4 onward usually cannot.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c070-->

**Is mitral stenosis a precapillary cause?** No. It is postcapillary.<!--c:pulmonary-hypertension-c071-->
