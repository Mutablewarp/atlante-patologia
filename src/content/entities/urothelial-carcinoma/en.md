## Definition and epidemiology

**What is urothelial carcinoma, and who gets it?** Urothelial carcinoma is a malignant tumor of the urothelium, the epithelium that lines the urinary tract.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c001--> It was formerly called transitional cell carcinoma.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c002--> Most urinary tract tumors arise in the bladder.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c003--> About 90% of bladder cancers are urothelial.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c004--> The median age at diagnosis is 65 years.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c005--> Men are affected about three times as often as women.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c006--> Any urothelial site, renal pelvis to posterior urethra, may hold several tumors.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c007-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**Which exposures cause it?**
- Today, smoking is the main risk factor, through polycyclic hydrocarbons.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c009-->
- Smoking raises the risk about fourfold.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c010-->
- Industrial exposure to azo dyes raises the risk.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c011-->
- Bladder cancer as an occupational disease was described in 1895 in German aniline dye workers.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c012-->
- *Schistosoma haematobium* infection adds risk in endemic regions,<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c014--> where most related cancers are squamous.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c015-->
- Cyclophosphamide and analgesics increase the risk.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c016-->
- So does radiation therapy for cervical, prostate or rectal cancer.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c017-->
- Nearly all cases have no identified inherited cause.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c018-->

## Pathogenesis

**How do chemicals in the urine damage urothelial DNA?** In the liver, aromatic amines are glucuronidated; urine then carries the conjugates to the bladder.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c019--> Arylnitrenium ions bind guanines in DNA and mutate it; they are freed in the bladder when β-glucuronidase in acidic urine cleaves the conjugate.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c020-->

**Is there one molecular route or two?** Two. Tumors differ by architecture (flat or papillary) and by behavior (low-grade superficial or high-grade muscle-invading).<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c021--> Flat CIS and low-grade papillary tumors share a recurrent 9p deletion, the arm that carries p16.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c022--> Low-grade papillary tumors also carry MAPK and PI3K pathway mutations.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c023--> Flat CIS and invasive cancers add other cell-cycle hits, notably loss of 17p, where TP53 lies.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c024-->

**What is the field effect?** Because the same exposures cause upper tract and bladder tumors, the lining is one shared field at risk.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c026--> Nearly half of patients treated for an upper tract tumor later form bladder tumors.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c027--> Upper tract tumors are far rarer than bladder ones, mostly urothelial.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c028-->

## Gross morphology

**What does it look like to the naked eye?** Papillary tumors favor the lateral and posterior bladder walls.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c029--> A low-grade tumor may be a tiny, fragile papillary tuft that stays on the mucosa.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c030--> High-grade tumors infiltrate the wall and often ulcerate, forming large solid masses.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c031--> Flat CIS patches resemble red velvet, often several, close to a papillary tumor.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c032-->

## Microscopic morphology

**How do the lesions run from flat to invasive?**
- **PUNLMP** (papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential) is a papilloma with more cells but not the atypia of low-grade carcinoma.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c034-->
- **Low-grade papillary carcinoma:** long, delicate papillae, slight atypia, nuclei that vary little.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c035-->
- **High-grade papillary carcinoma** shows mitoses in every layer of a disordered epithelium, with dark, pleomorphic nuclei.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c036-->
- **Carcinoma in situ (CIS)** is flat, with an intact basement membrane and atypia with lost polarity through the full epithelial thickness.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c037-->
- **Invasive carcinoma** arises from a papillary tumor or from flat CIS,<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c038--> though CIS is the more frequent precursor, per the reference.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c039--> One in three bladder CIS is followed by invasive carcinoma.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c040-->
- Variants include nested, micropapillary, plasmacytoid and sarcomatoid types.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c041-->

<aside class="edition">The fourth-edition WHO noninvasive list adds urothelial proliferation of uncertain malignant potential and dysplasia/atypia to the third.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c042--></aside>

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** cystoscopy with biopsy detects recurrence or progression.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c043-->
- Less invasive: urine cytology and urinalysis for tumor markers.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c044-->
- On urinary cells, FISH hunts for 9p21 loss and for aneuploidy of chromosomes 3, 7 and 17.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c045-->
- Papillary tumors are graded as PUNLMP, low-grade or high-grade carcinoma.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c046-->
- The pages used here describe no IHC or electron microscopy.

## Clinical presentation

**How does it present?** Hematuria of abrupt onset is the typical first sign of bladder tumors; dysuria comes less often.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c047--> Cystoscopy shows one or several tumors.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c048--> Most tumors (85%) are still limited to the bladder at diagnosis.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c049--> Upper tract tumors cause hematuria in 80% of patients<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c050--> and flank pain in 25%.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c051--> Bone metastases are least frequent; lung, liver and nodes (regional, periaortic) are progressively more common.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c052-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How is it staged?** In TNM staging, depth of invasion sets the T stage.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c053-->
- **Ta** is noninvasive papillary carcinoma, **Tis** is CIS and **T1** invades the lamina propria.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c054-->
- **T2** invades the muscularis propria (T2a inner half, T2b outer half).<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c055-->
- **T3** reaches perivesical tissue (T3a microscopic, T3b macroscopic).<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c056-->
- **T4** leaves the bladder: nearby organs (T4a), pelvic or abdominal wall (T4b).<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c057-->
- **N1** is one node, **N2** several regional nodes and **N3** common iliac nodes.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c058-->
- **M1a** is distant nodes beyond the common iliac; **M1b** is nonnodal metastasis.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c059-->

Ta, Tis and T1 are nonmuscle invasive; muscle invasion starts at T2.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c060-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What decides the outcome?** Spread and recurrence are likelier with bulky, multiple, advanced or high-grade tumors, with vessel or lymphatic invasion, or with dysplasia or CIS elsewhere in the bladder.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c062--> Half of low-grade papillary carcinomas recur, and 5% to 10% progress.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c063--> Low-grade tumors that stay superficial or barely invade give above 95% 10-year survival.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c064--> Five-year survival drops sharply with muscularis propria invasion,<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c065--> and overall mortality is 25% to 30% with muscle invasion.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c066-->

**How is it treated?** Nonmuscle-invasive tumors are monitored closely and usually treated by transurethral resection with fulguration plus intravesical BCG or chemotherapy.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c067--> Muscle invasion (T2) is the main reason for radical cystectomy; advanced stage is a rarer one.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c068--> Death most often follows distant metastases, local spread or uremia from urinary obstruction.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c069--> Because urothelial carcinomas often coexist or follow, upper tract tumors need nephroureterectomy of the whole ureter.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c070-->

## High-yield summary

- Urothelial tumors favor the **bladder** (about 90% of its tumors) but can start anywhere from **pelvis to posterior urethra**.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c078-->
- Risks: pelvic **radiation**, **cyclophosphamide**, ***S. haematobium***, **aromatic amine dyes** and **smoking**.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c079-->
- Low-grade papillary: **9p loss**, MAPK, PI3K; flat **CIS** and invasive: **TP53** on 17p.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c080-->
- **Field effect**: the whole urothelium is one target, as upper tract and bladder share causes.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c081-->
- **CIS** is flat with an intact basement membrane; most invasive carcinomas arise from it.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c082-->
- **T1** is lamina propria; **T2** is **muscularis propria** and the threshold for **cystectomy**.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c083-->
- Follow-up: **cystoscopy**, **urine cytology**, **FISH**.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c085-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is transitional cell carcinoma a different tumor?** No. It is the older name.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c086-->

**Is carcinoma in situ the same as mucosal invasion?** No. CIS has an intact basement membrane, while T1 invades the lamina propria.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c087-->

**Are Brunn nests and cystitis cystica cancer precursors?** Not proven. Nothing shows the lesions themselves turn malignant, yet the patients carry a higher cancer risk.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c088-->

**Is every bladder cancer urothelial?** No. Sarcoma, neuroendocrine, adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinomas occur but are rare.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c089-->

**CARDS** (original): **C**igarette smoke, **A**romatic amine dyes, **R**adiation, **D**rugs (cyclophosphamide, analgesics), **S**chistosoma haematobium.<!--c:urothelial-carcinoma-c091-->
