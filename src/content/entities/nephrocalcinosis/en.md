## Definition and epidemiology

**What are nephrocalcinosis and urate nephropathy?** Nephrocalcinosis is calcium deposited in the kidney parenchyma.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c001--> Urate nephropathy is kidney damage from urate crystals in the interstitium and tubules.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c002--> Primary gout is the classic chronic cause, but any hyperuricemia can cause it.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c003--> Calcium stones, nephrocalcinosis or both can follow hypercalciuria.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c004--> Sarcoidosis, vitamin D excess, bone-destroying tumors and primary hyperparathyroidism are hypercalcemic states behind it.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c005--> Metastatic calcification is the type driven by hypercalcemia.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c006--> Dystrophic calcification is calcium laid down in tissue that was already injured, for instance after an infarct.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c007-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the pathologist see?**

- Proximal convoluted tubules show the most conspicuous calcium in their basement membranes.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c009-->
- Calcium precipitates also lie in the interstitium.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c010-->
- Wedge-shaped scars, separated by better-preserved kidney, can appear when hypercalcemia is severe.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c011-->
- In acute phosphate nephropathy the interstitium is scarred and chronically inflamed, and calcium phosphate lies in the damaged collecting ducts and distal tubules.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c013-->
- Yellow streaks in the papillae point to acute urate nephropathy, where uric acid has crystallized inside the collecting ducts.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c014-->
- Frozen sections show birefringent crystals; after processing the deposits look amorphous.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c015-->
- In chronic urate nephropathy the interstitium slowly fills with urate, and the cortex scars and shrinks.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c017-->
- Giant cells and a granuloma-like pattern may appear in the inflammation ringing a tophus's urate crystals.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c018-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**What points to the diagnosis?** The gouty tophus is diagnostic of urate nephropathy.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c019--> Kidney injury weeks after a colonoscopy phosphate prep suggests acute phosphate nephropathy.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c021--> Acute urate nephropathy presents as acute kidney injury.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c022-->

## High-yield summary

- **Sarcoidosis**, vitamin D excess, hyperparathyroidism and osteolytic tumors raise calcium and give **metastatic** calcification of the kidney.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c028-->
- In routine processed sections urate deposits look **amorphous**; **frozen** sections show **birefringent** crystals.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c031-->
- The **gouty tophus** is diagnostic of urate nephropathy.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c032-->
- **Tumor lysis syndrome** injures the kidney when urate crystals clog the acid collecting ducts.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c033-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Does every kidney calcification mean hypercalcemia?** No. Hypercalcemia has no known tie to the incidental calcium specks seen in some autopsy kidneys.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c034-->

**Are nephrocalcinosis and kidney stones the same?** No. Calcium-containing stones are nephrolithiasis, not calcium deposited within the parenchyma.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c035-->

**Does chronic gout always cause kidney failure?** No. Fewer than half of patients have significant kidney dysfunction, though most show microscopic lesions.<!--c:nephrocalcinosis-c036-->
