## Definition and epidemiology

Caseous necrosis is a pattern of necrosis in which, unlike in coagulative necrosis, the dead cells lose their outlines and, unlike in liquefactive necrosis, they are not dissolved, so they linger for a long time as shapeless, granular, pink debris.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c001--> The pattern is the hallmark of tuberculosis, although it is seen less often in other conditions.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c002--> It typically forms the center of a granuloma, the tubercle.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c003--> Whenever you find caseation, consider tuberculosis first, while remembering that some tuberculous lesions never caseate and that other agents can produce the same picture.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

- **Mycobacterium tuberculosis** is the classic cause, in the lung, lymph nodes and many other organs it seeds.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c005-->
- **Histoplasma capsulatum** produces necrotizing granulomas with caseous centers in the lung, hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes, spleen and liver.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c006-->
- **Coccidioides immitis** also forms necrotizing granulomas that can heal as a fibrocaseous nodule.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c007-->
- **Bacillary load:** in tuberculosis, a large number of organisms pushes the host response toward extensive caseation, whereas a small load in a competent host is more often killed and walled off.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c008-->

## Pathogenesis

**Bacilli survive inside macrophages.** Alveolar macrophages engulf inhaled mycobacteria but cannot kill them, because lipids of the bacterial wall prevent the phagosome from fusing with lysosomes.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c009-->

**T cells arm the macrophages.** Infected macrophages display bacillary antigens to T lymphocytes; as these lymphocytes proliferate they secrete IFN-γ, which switches macrophages to an activated state richer in lytic enzymes.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c010--> This combined hypersensitivity and cell-mediated response needs about 3 to 6 weeks to develop.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c011-->

**The same response destroys tissue.** Lytic enzymes released from activated macrophages also injure host tissue, and when bacilli are numerous the hypersensitivity reaction causes extensive caseous necrosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c012--> Waxy, toxic glycolipids in the mycobacterial wall are usually blamed for the unusual quality of this necrosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c013--> The granuloma may partly serve the bacillus, helping it survive despite the host immune response.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c014-->

**Why the center dies.** Agents that kill the macrophages ingesting them, such as M. tuberculosis, produce necrotizing granulomas whose centers are a mix of debris, dead cells and dead organisms; stimuli that spare macrophages, as in sarcoidosis, produce nonnecrotizing granulomas.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c015-->

## Gross morphology

- The name means cheese-like: on the cut surface the necrotic center looks like soft, crumbly, gray-white curd.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c016-->
- **Ghon focus:** primary tuberculosis leaves a subpleural nodule with sharp margins and a necrotic core; once healed it measures 1 to 2 cm, and over time it scars and calcifies.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c017-->
- **Cavities:** in secondary tuberculosis they favor the upper-lobe apices and hold caseous material rich in acid-fast bacilli; size ranges from under 1 cm to almost a whole lung, though most are 3 to 10 cm.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c018-->
- **Miliary pattern:** many small yellow-white nodules, each the size of a millet seed, scattered through several organs.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c019-->
- **Other sites:** enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes with caseation in intestinal tuberculosis, and brain masses (tuberculomas) with a caseous center.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c020-->

## Microscopic morphology

- **Center:** amorphous, granular, eosinophilic material with no recognizable cell outlines or tissue architecture.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c021-->
- **Rim:** epithelioid macrophages, Langhans giant cells and lymphocytes, with fibrous tissue at the periphery.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c022-->
- **Langhans giant cell:** nuclei lined up in a horseshoe at the edge of the cell, unlike a foreign-body giant cell, whose nuclei are scattered.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c023-->
- **Lymphocyte collar:** CD4+ T cells encircle the inner zone of epithelioid cells, giant cells and necrosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c024-->
- **Immunocompromised host:** the granuloma may fail to organize, leaving loose collections of macrophages with no Langhans giant cells.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c025-->
- **Old lesions:** progressive fibrosis and calcification.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c026-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

- **Acid-fast stains** such as Ziehl–Neelsen show mycobacteria as slender, beaded rods.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c027-->
- **Silver impregnation:** in histoplasmosis it outlines the leftover, degenerating yeasts lying inside caseous foci.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c028-->
- **GMS (silver) and PAS** help to show fungi such as Coccidioides immitis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c029-->

## Clinical presentation

- **Primary infection:** most primary tuberculous infections (over 90% of normal adults) are contained and cause no symptoms.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c030-->
- **Active disease:** fever, weight loss, fatigue and night sweats; cough and hemoptysis appear once lung disease is established.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c031-->
- **Brain:** in countries with endemic tuberculosis, tuberculomas rank among the commonest intracranial masses.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c032-->
- **Spine (Pott disease):** caseating granulomas start in the vertebral marrow; weakened vertebral bodies collapse, causing kyphosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c033-->
- **Epididymis:** confluent caseating granulomas enlarge the epididymis and make the vas deferens beaded.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c034-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

- **Histology:** a granuloma with central caseation should prompt a search for tuberculosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c035-->
- **Organisms:** acid-fast stains on smears or tissue, and GMS (silver) and PAS stains when a fungus is suspected.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c036-->
- **Chest radiograph:** cavities in one or both lung apices point to secondary tuberculosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c037--> Healed, calcified primary lesions are visible on X-ray.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c038-->

## Complications and prognosis

- **Healing:** caseous foci in lungs and lymph nodes shrink, scar and undergo dystrophic calcification, often without functional consequence.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c039-->
- **Dormancy:** a few bacilli stay alive in healed foci for years and can reactivate if immunity declines.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c040-->
- **Cavitation and spread:** a caseous focus that erodes into a bronchus drains and leaves a cavity, and the discharged material spreads infection through the airways.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c041-->
- **Local complications:** rupture into the pleural space (empyema, pneumothorax), hemoptysis from eroded arteries, and Aspergillus colonizing an open cavity (aspergilloma).<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c042-->
- **Meningitis:** a brain tuberculoma that ruptures into the cerebrospinal fluid causes tuberculous meningitis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c043-->
- **Amyloidosis:** chronic cavitary tuberculosis was once a leading cause of secondary amyloidosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c044-->
- **Fungal nodules:** old histoplasmosis nodules calcify, whereas old coccidioidomycosis granulomas seldom do.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c045-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[coagulative-necrosis]] | Ghost outlines of dead cells persist, with deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm and nuclear loss, as in an infarct.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c046--> | Histology: preserved cell outlines |
| [[liquefactive-necrosis]] | Dead tissue is digested by neutrophil enzymes into fluid, forming an abscess cavity; brain infarcts also liquefy.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c047--> | Histology: cavity full of neutrophils |
| [[fat-necrosis]] | Chalky white foci in fat; basophilic calcium soaps at the edges of necrotic adipocytes.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c048--> | Histology of adipose tissue |
| [[fibrinoid-necrosis]] | Typically in injured vessel walls, which stain bright pink from leaked plasma proteins.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c049--> | Histology of the vessel wall |
| Nonnecrotizing granulomas (sarcoidosis, berylliosis) | Centers made of living epithelioid macrophages and giant cells; necrosis absent or only focal.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c050--> | Beryllium lymphocyte proliferation test separates berylliosis from sarcoidosis<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c051--> |
| Fungal granulomas (histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis) | Can caseate and closely resemble tuberculosis; yeasts or spherules in the necrosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c052--> | GMS (silver) and PAS stains |

## High-yield summary

- Caseous necrosis = **cheese-like** necrosis at the center of a **granuloma**.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c053-->
- Dead cells lose their outlines but are **not digested** by neutrophils: they persist as **amorphous granular eosinophilic** debris.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c054-->
- Classic cause: **tuberculosis**; also **histoplasmosis** and **coccidioidomycosis**.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c055-->
- Mechanism: **T-cell hypersensitivity** activates macrophages through **IFN-γ**, and released lytic enzymes kill tissue.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c056-->
- Rim: **epithelioid macrophages**, **Langhans giant cells** (horseshoe nuclei), lymphocytes, fibrosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c057-->
- Fate: **calcification** (healed Ghon complex) or erosion into a bronchus and **cavitation**.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c058-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

- **Caseation is not proof of tuberculosis.** Fungi such as Histoplasma and Coccidioides cause it too.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c059-->
- **Absence of caseation does not exclude tuberculosis.** In immunocompromised hosts granulomas may be poorly formed.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c060--> In tuberculous endometritis, the endometrium is shed each month before granulomas can mature and caseate, so the usual finding is poorly formed granulomas without necrosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c061-->
- **Caseous is not the same pattern as liquefactive necrosis**, which is digestion by neutrophils; still, caseum can soften and liquefy, and it leaves a cavity once it empties into a bronchus.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c062-->
- **Asteroid and Schaumann bodies** turn up in most granulomatous diseases, so neither one points specifically to sarcoidosis.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c063-->
- **Necrosis is not damage done directly by the bacillus alone**: much of it comes from the host immune response.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c064-->

## Mnemonics

**"Cheese stays on the plate"** (original): caseous debris loses the cell shapes like grated cheese, but it is not digested away by neutrophils as in liquefactive necrosis; it may soften, yet it stays on the plate until it calcifies or drains into a bronchus.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c065-->

**"One bacillus, two fungi"** (original): cheesy granulomas usually come from one bacillus, **M. tuberculosis**, and two fungi, **Histoplasma** and **Coccidioides**.<!--c:caseous-necrosis-c066-->
