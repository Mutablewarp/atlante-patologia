## Definition and epidemiology

**What is asthma?** Sudden bouts of cough, wheeze and breathlessness, caused by airflow obstruction that comes in episodes after many different stimuli.<!--c:asthma-c001--> Some patients are symptom-free between attacks; in others attacks arise on long-standing obstruction.<!--c:asthma-c002--> Feeling well does not mean healthy airways: lesions and some obstruction usually persist.<!--c:asthma-c003--> Status asthmaticus is a severe attack that treatment fails to control.<!--c:asthma-c004--> Up to 10% of US children and 5% of US adults have asthma.<!--c:asthma-c005-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What sets off an attack?** Each case is now described by its trigger and the shared effector pathway, not by the old allergic/intrinsic split.<!--c:asthma-c006--> Allergic asthma is the most frequent type and typically presents in childhood.<!--c:asthma-c007--> Often it is a respiratory virus, not an allergen, that starts a child's attack.<!--c:asthma-c008--> Exertion can provoke bronchospasm in many asthmatics.<!--c:asthma-c009--> Other NSAIDs are suspected too, but aspirin is the classic drug trigger.<!--c:asthma-c010--> As many as 10% of adults with asthma may react to aspirin.<!--c:asthma-c011--> Avoid β-blockers: they reliably narrow asthmatic airways.<!--c:asthma-c012-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does an allergen cause an attack (type 2 inflammation)?** Inflammation is the usual basis of airway hyperresponsiveness.<!--c:asthma-c013--> IgE-coated mast cells sit among the bronchial epithelial cells; in a sensitized person the inhaled allergen engages them and TH2 cells.<!--c:asthma-c014--> Both cell types secrete histamine and other type I mediators, plus IL-4 and IL-5.<!--c:asthma-c015--> Edema, excess mucus and smooth muscle spasm can each narrow the lumen reversibly.<!--c:asthma-c016--> Eosinophils discharge granule proteins into the lumen, damaging epithelium and slowing mucociliary clearance.<!--c:asthma-c017--> Autonomic output from mucosal nerves, likely triggered by epithelial injury, adds narrowing and mucus.<!--c:asthma-c018--> Leukotriene B4 and PAF keep the attack going and growing by bringing in more effector cells, eosinophils among them.<!--c:asthma-c019-->

## Gross morphology

**What do the lungs look like?** Autopsy lesions are the worst ones, since they come from deaths in status asthmaticus.<!--c:asthma-c020--> The lungs are markedly overinflated.<!--c:asthma-c021--> Thick mucus plugs fill the airways.<!--c:asthma-c022-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What does the bronchial wall show?** Many eosinophils and strips of epithelium are trapped in the plugs.<!--c:asthma-c023--> Charcot–Leyden crystals, which come from eosinophils, are also found.<!--c:asthma-c024--> Coughing can bring up Curschmann spirals, mucus molds of the airway.<!--c:asthma-c025--> Bronchial muscle overgrowth (hyperplasia) is typical of status asthmaticus.<!--c:asthma-c026--> Submucosal mucus glands are hyperplastic too.<!--c:asthma-c027--> Eosinophils in varying numbers sit among other inflammatory cells in the swollen submucosa.<!--c:asthma-c028--> Only basal cells may be left where the epithelium has been shed, and the normal pseudostratified pattern is gone.<!--c:asthma-c029--> Basal cells multiply, alongside goblet cell hyperplasia and squamous metaplasia.<!--c:asthma-c030--> Collagen laid down under the true basal lamina makes the basement membrane look thick.<!--c:asthma-c031-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does sputum show?** Late-attack sputum is thick and eosinophil-rich, and may show Curschmann spirals and Charcot–Leyden crystals.<!--c:asthma-c032-->

No special stain, IHC or molecular test is described.

## Clinical presentation

**What does a typical attack look like?** A dry cough and chest tightness mark the onset.<!--c:asthma-c033--> The patient becomes dyspneic and tachypneic; expiration is prolonged and wheezing is heard in both phases.<!--c:asthma-c034--> Thick mucus is coughed up in a violent fit, which often marks the end of the attack.<!--c:asthma-c035--> Status asthmaticus brings hypoxemia, frequently with hypercapnia.<!--c:asthma-c036--> It is serious and needs hospital admission.<!--c:asthma-c037-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**Which tests does the reference mention?** Skin-test reactivity correlates strongly with allergic asthma.<!--c:asthma-c038-->

## Complications and prognosis

**How does asthma evolve?** Many childhood cases relapse after age 30, although about half are much better or symptom-free by age 20.<!--c:asthma-c039--> Inhaled corticosteroids are a mainstay of treatment; inhaled bronchodilators often relieve symptoms dramatically.<!--c:asthma-c040-->

## High-yield summary

- **Episodic, reversible airflow obstruction** is the core of asthma, shown as cough, wheeze and dyspnea.<!--c:asthma-c044-->
- In allergic asthma, **IgE-bearing mast cells and TH2 cells** release **IL-4 and IL-5**, and **eosinophils** follow.<!--c:asthma-c045-->
- **Charcot–Leyden crystals** and **Curschmann spirals** accompany **mucus plugs** rich in eosinophils.<!--c:asthma-c046-->
- The wall shows **smooth muscle and gland hyperplasia** and a **thickened subepithelial basement membrane**.<!--c:asthma-c047-->
- **Status asthmaticus** is a severe attack that rescue drugs fail to break; admit the patient.<!--c:asthma-c048-->
- **β-Blockers** are contraindicated in asthma.<!--c:asthma-c049-->
- **Emphysema** declines progressively, whereas asthma is episodic.<!--c:asthma-c050-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is every case of asthma allergic?** No. Viral infection, exercise and drugs can also trigger it.<!--c:asthma-c051-->

**Is a patient with no symptoms free of disease?** Not necessarily: most keep partly obstructed, structurally altered airways.<!--c:asthma-c052-->
