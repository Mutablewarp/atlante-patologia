## Definition and epidemiology

**What does "precancerous" mean?** Chronic injury can leave areas of metaplasia, and cancer often arises in them.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c001--> Dysplasia is a microscopic pattern seen before cancer develops, so it counts as a precursor lesion.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c002--> Dysplastic cells are only partly autonomous, so intervention can still restore normal tissue.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c003--> In bladder, prostate, cervix and other organs, intraepithelial neoplasia passes through a dysplastic stage.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c004-->

## Etiology and risk factors

**What keeps injuring the epithelium?** Persistent injury can lead to metaplasia or to dysplasia.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c005--> Tobacco smoke turns the bronchial lining squamous, and dysplasia can later arise in that metaplastic lining.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c006-->
- Papillomavirus infection of the cervix, chronic colitis of the colon and sunlight on skin (actinic keratosis) can each be followed by dysplasia.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c007-->
- Barrett esophagus results from chronic gastroesophageal reflux.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c008-->
- The risk of Barrett esophagus is higher in white people, in obese people and in smokers.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c009-->
- A family history, obesity, smoking and a Western diet all raise the chance of colon adenomas.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c010-->
- Familial adenomatous polyposis is caused by a germline mutation that inactivates the APC gene.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c011-->

## Pathogenesis

**How does a normal epithelium become dysplastic?** Mutations can arise at any cell division.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c012--> A mutant clone expands when the mutation helps it outgrow or outlive neighboring cells.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c013--> Faster division gives the mutant lineage more chances to pick up further mutations.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c014--> The more mutations a lineage gathers, the less it obeys normal growth controls.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c015-->
- In adenomas, crypt cells that should mature and stop dividing keep proliferating and escape apoptosis.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c016-->
- Pancreatic ducts show dysplasia too, called pancreatic intraductal neoplasia (PanIN), and it comes in two grades.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c017-->
- Activation of the KRAS oncogene is an early event in low-grade PanIN.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c018-->
- Inactivation of tumor suppressor genes such as p53 comes later.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c019-->

## Gross morphology

**What can the naked eye see?**
- Barrett epithelium looks salmon-pink at endoscopy.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c020-->
- Colon adenomas can be stalked or sessile, and range in size from tiny nodules to large lesions.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c021-->
- The surface of a villous adenoma looks ragged, like a cauliflower, and the lesion is usually big and flat-based.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c022-->
- In familial adenomatous polyposis, the lining of colon and rectum is densely covered with adenomas.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c023-->

## Microscopic morphology

**What do dysplastic cells look like?** Maturation from base to surface, normally orderly, becomes chaotic.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c027--> Mitoses appear far from the basal layer.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c026--> Nuclei look dark and irregular, and they are enlarged.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c025--> From cell to cell, size and shape are uneven.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c024-->

**How is it graded?**
- Cigar-shaped dark nuclei mark low-grade dysplasia, the minimum an adenoma shows by definition.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c028-->
- High-grade dysplasia shows complex, sieve-like (cribriform) glands; its nuclei vary in shape and have large nucleoli.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c029-->
- In the cervix, telling advanced dysplasia from cancer in situ is hard, because the two look so alike.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c030-->
- An intact basement membrane confines carcinoma in situ, although its cells may reach every layer of the epithelium.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c031-->
- To call a polyp invasive adenocarcinoma, the pathologist must find tumor glands beneath the muscularis mucosae.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c032-->

## Special stains, IHC and molecular findings

**What does the pathologist look at, and with which test?**
- **How it is studied:** Barrett esophagus is diagnosed by endoscopy with biopsy.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c033-->
- On histology, judge cell and nuclear size, the order of maturation and the height of mitoses.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c034-->
- In Barrett esophagus, the squamous lining is lost to glandular epithelium that looks intestinal and contains goblet cells.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c035-->
- In a removed polyp, the pathologist checks whether the dysplasia stays within the mucosa, because then complete polypectomy cures it.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c036-->

This card's source gives no special stain, immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy or molecular test for grading these lesions.

## Clinical presentation

**How do these lesions present?** Reflux complaints usually lead to the diagnosis of Barrett esophagus, yet many patients have no such complaints.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c037--> Its prevalence rises with age.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c038--> Dysplasia develops in only a minority of Barrett patients.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c039--> In familial adenomatous polyposis, colorectal cancer is inevitable if the colon is left in place.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c040-->

## Diagnosis, key lab and imaging

**How are precancerous lesions followed (surveillance)?** Regular monitoring of Barrett patients is meant to catch dysplasia before adenocarcinoma can develop.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c041--> With no dysplasia, surveillance endoscopy is repeated every 3 to 5 years.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c042--> A longer Barrett segment or a higher grade of dysplasia means a higher cancer risk.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c043--> Each year, adenocarcinoma is estimated to develop in 0.1% to 0.3% of patients with Barrett esophagus, a low risk.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c044--> Visible nodular areas in high-grade dysplasia should be resected endoscopically and the Barrett segment ablated, according to the 2016 guidelines.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c045--> Relatives of a patient with serrated polyposis should start screening colonoscopy earlier, since their colorectal cancer risk is raised.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c046-->

## Complications and prognosis

**What happens if the lesion is left alone, or removed?** Quitting smoking is an example: once the stressor is gone, dysplasia usually regresses.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c047--> Severe dysplasia calls for intervention before cancer appears: remove the abnormal tissue or deal with its cause.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c048--> An adenoma with high-grade dysplasia can progress to invasive adenocarcinoma.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c049--> Bigger adenomas, and those that are villous or highly dysplastic, more often hold invasive carcinoma.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c050--> Complete polypectomy cures a lesion whose dysplasia has not gone beyond the mucosa.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c051--> Invasion has not happened in carcinoma in situ, so cure is invariably possible.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c052--> A patient with familial adenomatous polyposis is cured by total colectomy if it is done before cancer begins.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c053-->

## Differential diagnosis

| Entity | Discriminating features | Key test |
|---|---|---|
| [[metaplasia]] | One mature cell type is replaced by another, usually as an adaptation.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c054--> Removal of the stimulus usually allows complete reversal.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c055--> | Histology: orderly, mature cells of the new type |
| [[dysplasia]] | Disorderly maturation, atypical nuclei and mitoses above the basal layer.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c056--> | Histology: grade the atypia |
| [[carcinoma-in-situ]] | Basement membrane intact; malignant cells reach all epithelial levels.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c057--> | Histology: is the basement membrane crossed? |
| [[hyperplasia]] | The number of cells is increased.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c058--> | Histology: cell count and cell shape |

## High-yield summary

- **Metaplasia** can precede cancer: lung and bladder cancers, for example, often develop in metaplastic areas.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c059-->
- **Dysplasia** is the precursor lesion and usually goes away when its trigger is removed.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c060-->
- **Intraepithelial neoplasia** is graded: adenomas carry at least **low-grade** dysplasia, and **high-grade** dysplasia comes closer to cancer.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c061-->
- **Carcinoma in situ** has not crossed the **basement membrane**, which is why it is invariably curable.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c062-->
- **Barrett esophagus** is followed by **surveillance endoscopy** with biopsy to detect dysplasia.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c063-->
- **Familial adenomatous polyposis** (germline **APC** mutation) always leads to colorectal cancer without colectomy.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c064-->

## Common misconceptions and exam traps

**Is metaplasia the same as dysplasia?** No. Metaplasia replaces one mature cell type with another and usually reverses, whereas dysplasia is disordered growth and maturation.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c065-->

**Is dysplasia already cancer?** No. Dysplastic cells are only partly autonomous, so with treatment the tissue can go back to normal.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c066-->

**Does every precancerous lesion turn into cancer?** No. Dysplasia usually goes away once its cause is removed.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c067-->

**Is Barrett esophagus a cancer?** No. It is metaplasia, and only a minority of patients develop dysplasia.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c068-->

## Mnemonics

**M-D-I-C: "My Doctor Inspects Carefully".** Metaplasia, Dysplasia, In situ carcinoma, Invasive Carcinoma: dysplasia can arise in metaplastic areas, and severe dysplasia looks very much like in situ cancer.<!--c:precancerous-lesions-c069-->
