## Overview

Necrosis is cell death imposed on a tissue by an injury its cells cannot adapt to; it usually involves a contiguous, geographic area rather than isolated cells, and the dead tissue usually provokes acute inflammation.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c001--> Coagulative, liquefactive, caseous, fat and fibrinoid necrosis are descriptive labels for distinct appearances under the microscope, and each one points towards a cause and a likely organ.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c002--> Gangrene is different in kind: it names death of a large mass of tissue, typically a foot or leg, and dry gangrene of a limb is in substance a large pale infarct, that is, coagulative necrosis.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c003--> The matrix compares the six patterns side by side; each row links to its full card.

## What decides the pattern

The pattern depends on what happens to the dead cells next: whether their proteins persist, whether enzymes digest them, and what material the tissue contains.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c004-->

- **Structure persists (coagulative):** after ischemic death the outline of each cell and the plan of the tissue stay visible for a while, and the breakdown of cytoplasmic RNA removes the blue tint, so the cytoplasm looks more eosinophilic.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c005-->
- **Digestion wins (liquefactive):** when dead cells are dissolved much faster than repair can proceed, the tissue turns liquid; the hydrolases usually come from neutrophils gathered around bacteria.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c006--> In the brain, an infarct liquefies without any need for acute inflammation, perhaps because of the abundant lysosomal enzymes of nervous tissue.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c007-->
- **Lipid substrate (fat necrosis):** lipases released from an injured pancreas, or from damaged fat cells after trauma, split triglycerides, and the free fatty acids bind calcium and precipitate as soaps (saponification).<!--c:p01-necrosis-c008-->
- **Mycobacterial wall (caseous):** the waxy lipids of the mycobacterial cell wall are thought to explain a pattern halfway between coagulative and liquefactive necrosis: in the centre of tuberculous granulomas no cell outlines survive, yet the remains are not dissolved and they stay.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c009-->
- **Leaky vessel wall (fibrinoid):** plasma proteins leak into an injured vessel wall and fibrin is laid down in it, so the wall looks bright pink on H&E.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c010-->

## Type by type: organ examples

### Coagulative necrosis

The model lesion is the infarct: occlusion of an end artery causes coagulative necrosis of the tissue downstream.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c011--> Pale infarcts with uniform coagulative necrosis are typical of the heart, kidney and spleen; in the spleen they form wedges under the capsule, and in the kidney the pale zone reaches the capsular surface.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c012--> In heart muscle the nuclei lag behind the cytoplasm: about 24 hours after the infarct the dead fibers already look intensely pink, but only after several days have all their nuclei gone.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c013--> The kidney adds two more examples: diffuse cortical necrosis in severe shock, classically after premature separation of the placenta, and papillary necrosis in analgesic nephropathy.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c014--> It takes a radiation dose much higher than 10 Gy to kill cells outright within a short time, and the microscope then gives no clue to the cause: the picture is ordinary coagulative necrosis.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c015-->

### Liquefactive necrosis

The usual example is the abscess, a cavity hollowed out of a solid organ by a localized bacterial infection and the enzymes of the neutrophils it attracts.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c016--> The ring of contrast enhancement around a brain abscess is its granulation-tissue lining, which has no blood-brain barrier.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c017--> The abscess starts as cerebritis that liquefies within days and is then enclosed, from inside out, by granulation tissue, a collagen capsule and a zone of reactive astrocytes.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c017--> An ischemic infarct of the brain also liquefies and can leave a fluid-filled cyst that persists for life.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c018--> Severe carbon monoxide poisoning destroys both globus pallidus nuclei by liquefactive necrosis, a nearly specific finding.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c019--> An infarct elsewhere turns liquefactive when it becomes infected: a septic infarct may evolve into an abscess.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c020-->

### Caseous necrosis

Tuberculosis is the classic cause of caseous necrosis, though not the only one.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c021--> Seen from the outside in, a tuberculous granuloma has a cuff of lymphocytes and fibrous tissue, then a layer of epithelioid macrophages and Langhans giant cells, and at its centre a soft zone of caseous necrosis.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c022--> Typical sites are the subpleural Ghon focus with its hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes in primary infection, apical cavities filled with caseous material in secondary tuberculosis, and tuberculomas in the brain.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c023--> Tuberculomas rank among the commonest intracranial masses in regions where tuberculosis is endemic.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c024-->

### Fat necrosis

Fat necrosis is confined to adipose tissue and is caused mainly by pancreatitis or by trauma.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c025--> In acute pancreatitis, chalky yellow-white foci appear in the fat around the pancreas and in the mesentery, and enzymes escaping into the peritoneum cause peritonitis with fat necrosis.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c026--> In the breast, fat necrosis after trauma forms a hard lump, sometimes tethered to the skin, that can be taken for carcinoma, particularly once it has calcified.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c027--> Fat necrosis is also part of the tissue reaction around a ruptured silicone breast implant.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c028-->

### Fibrinoid necrosis

Fibrinoid necrosis is a lesion of blood-vessel walls.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c029--> It marks immune-complex vasculitis (the Arthus reaction is its experimental model), polyarteritis nodosa, cutaneous small-vessel (hypersensitivity) vasculitis of venules and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg–Strauss syndrome).<!--c:p01-necrosis-c030--> In malignant hypertension, plasma forced into the walls of injured renal arterioles produces arteriolar fibrinoid necrosis.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c031--> Antibody-mediated rejection of a kidney graft, hyperacute or acute, can show arteritis with fibrinoid necrosis of the vessel wall.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c032-->

### Gangrene

Dry gangrene of a foot or leg follows arterial occlusion, typically thrombosis on atherosclerotic leg arteries or diabetic vascular disease.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c033--> To the eye the dead part is dry, shrunken and dark to black (mummified), with a sharp line where it meets living tissue.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c056--> Diabetes accounts for most limb amputations not caused by trauma in the United States, and ischemic gangrene is the main reason.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c034--> When dead tissue becomes infected, neutrophil enzymes liquefy it: this is the picture usually taught as wet gangrene.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c035--> Gas gangrene (clostridial myonecrosis) is a separate process: *Clostridium perfringens* growing in devitalized wound tissue releases a phospholipase toxin that kills healthy muscle, with gas in the tissues, few neutrophils and a course that can be fatal within hours.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c036--> Meningococcal sepsis can also end in gangrene of the fingers, toes or limbs, through clotting in the small vessels of the skin during the sepsis (purpura fulminans) or, in some survivors, through severe vasculitis; infarction of the bowel is another cause of gangrene.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c037-->

## How to recognize it (low to high power)

- **Is the architecture still there?** Ghost outlines of cells and tissue mean coagulative necrosis; a cavity full of neutrophils and debris means liquefactive necrosis; structureless granular pink debris inside a granuloma means caseous necrosis.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c038-->
- **Where is it?** If the change is confined to a vessel wall, where part of the wall is soaked with uniform, intensely pink material, it is fibrinoid necrosis; if it sits in fat, where the edges of the necrotic fat carry shapeless calcium soaps that stain blue, it is fat necrosis.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c039-->
- **Look at the nuclei.** In coagulative necrosis the nucleus shrinks and darkens (pyknosis), breaks into fragments (karyorrhexis) or fades away (karyolysis).<!--c:p01-necrosis-c040-->
- **Look at the rim.** A granuloma with epithelioid macrophages and Langhans giant cells around the necrosis points to caseation; foamy macrophages and giant cells engulfing lipid point to fat necrosis.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c041-->

## Typical outcome

- **Coagulative:** neutrophils enter from the living border within days, macrophages then clear the debris, granulation tissue forms and a scar results; the centre of a large infarct can stay unresorbed for months.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c042-->
- **Liquefactive:** a fibrous capsule eventually forms around an abscess, and in the brain or spinal cord a permanent cyst can mark the place where a large area has liquefied.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c043--> An untreated brain abscess can kill by herniation or by rupture into a ventricle.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c044-->
- **Caseous:** contained lesions shrink, scar and calcify, as in a healed Ghon complex; uncontrolled ones cavitate and spread, for example by eroding into a bronchus or rupturing into the pleural cavity.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c045-->
- **Fat:** in the breast, lipid-laden (foamy) macrophages and giant cells clear the fat, after which the area scars and may calcify (dystrophic calcification); in acute pancreatitis the soaps trap calcium and blood calcium can fall.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c046-->
- **Fibrinoid:** the damaged wall can thrombose, rupture and bleed, or form aneurysms, and arteriolar thrombosis causes small infarcts downstream.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c047-->
- **Gangrene:** dry gangrene often ends in debridement or amputation; gas gangrene can bring hemolytic anemia, shock and renal failure.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c048-->
- **Any type:** necrotic tissue can calcify (dystrophic calcification), as in tuberculous lymph nodes or in the kidneys of patients who survived cortical necrosis.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c049-->

## Pitfalls

- **"Fibrinoid necrosis" is a misnomer:** the eosinophilic plasma proteins mask the wall, so it is often impossible to say whether its cells are actually dead.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c050-->
- **Not every granuloma caseates:** sarcoidosis, berylliosis and hypersensitivity pneumonitis form granulomas without caseation; caseation strongly suggests tuberculosis but does not prove it.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c051-->
- **A brain infarct changes pattern:** it begins as coagulative necrosis and then liquefies, and acute inflammation is not what dissolves it.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c052-->
- **Red infarcts are coagulative too:** the red colour comes from blood entering the dead zone, typical of organs with a double or collateral supply such as the lung and small bowel.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c053-->
- **Morphology lags behind the event:** an acute myocardial infarct cannot be seen with the naked eye in the first 12 hours and shows as pallor by about 24 hours.<!--c:p01-necrosis-c054-->
- **Apoptosis is not a type of necrosis:** it strikes single cells scattered among living ones and does not provoke inflammation (see [[apoptosis]]).<!--c:p01-necrosis-c055-->
